# תולדות חב"ד בפולין, ליטא ולטביא

## Chabad History in Poland, Lithuania, Latvia

**Series:** מסביב לחסידות (Mesibbv L'Chassidus), Vol. 5  
**Publisher:** Otzar HaChassidim, 770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn  
**Editor:** R' Shalom DovBer Levin  
**Published:** 2011  
**Length:** 15,586 lines — the largest volume in the series

---

## Overview

This massive volume — the largest in the series — covers Chabad's presence across Poland, Lithuania, and Latvia primarily during the interwar period (1920–1945), with substantial sections on earlier history including the Vilna Gaon's anti-Chassidic campaign. Its principal focus is the Rayatz's leadership from exile: the years when Chabad's headquarters moved westward as Soviet persecution made Russia uninhabitable.

The book serves as the primary record of Chabad's institutional life during the interwar years and the devastation of the Holocaust.

---

## Early History: The Vilna Gaon's Ban

The book opens with significant historical context: the documented opposition of the Vilna Gaon (R' Elijah of Vilna, 1720–1797) and his followers to the Chassidic movement. The **Hoshana Raba 5559 (1798)** ban against Chassidim is documented, including the arrests of R' Meir Refael and other Chabad chassidim in Vilna — an early instance of internal Jewish conflict spilling over into legal/governmental channels.

Chabad's presence in Lithuania began within a context of serious religious opposition from the Mitnagdim (opponents of Chassidism). The book traces how Chabad slowly established itself despite this resistance, building communities from Vilna through the Lithuanian countryside.

---

## The Rayatz's Westward Journey

The book's central narrative is the Rayatz's westward movement as Soviet conditions deteriorated:

### Riga (1928–1933)

After the Rayatz escaped Soviet Russia in 1927, he established himself in **Riga, Latvia** — then an independent republic with a significant Jewish population. The Riga years were a period of reconstruction: reestablishing Chabad's central institutions, maintaining contact with remaining communities inside the USSR, and beginning the Tomchei Tmimim yeshiva network anew outside Russia.

### Warsaw (1934–1935)

The headquarters moved to **Warsaw**, the largest Jewish city in Europe, with a Jewish population of approximately 350,000. Chabad established significant institutional infrastructure here.

### Otwock (1936–1939)

A Warsaw suburb, Otwock became the Rayatz's residence and the practical center of Chabad operations in the late 1930s. The nearby forests gave it a sanatorium character; the Rayatz's health was a concern during these years.

### Warsaw/Riga (1940) and Escape

When Germany invaded Poland in September 1939, the Rayatz was in Warsaw. Through extraordinary diplomatic intervention (including efforts by American government officials and Chabad supporters), he secured escape permits. The escape route went through **Kobe, Japan → Shanghai → Canada/USA**, demonstrating the global scope of rescue efforts.

---

## Tomchei Tmimim Yeshiva Network

The yeshiva system founded by the Rashab in Lubavitch in 1897 was reconstituted outside Russia. The Warsaw-Otwock center of Tomchei Tmimim became the flagship, with branches throughout Poland, Lithuania, and Latvia. The book documents individual yeshiva branches, their faculty (mashpiim/spiritual guides, menahel/directors), student bodies, and the curricula combining Talmudic study with Chassidic philosophy.

---

## The Holocaust

The book's most devastating chapter documents the destruction of these communities. Most yeshiva students and faculty who did not escape were murdered in the Holocaust. The Warsaw ghetto, the mass shootings of Lithuanian Jewry (particularly at Ponary/Paneriai), and the destruction of Latvian Jewish communities all took their toll on Chabad communities that had only recently been rebuilt from Soviet persecution.

---

## Historical Significance

This book is both a record of institutional triumph — the remarkable rebuilding of Chabad in the interwar period — and of catastrophic loss. The 15,586 lines represent the most detailed record of Chabad's European infrastructure before its annihilation. It preserves names, institutions, and stories that would otherwise be lost.
