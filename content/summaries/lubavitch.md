# ליובאוויטש

## Lubavitch

**Series:** מסביב לחסידות (Mesibbv L'Chassidus), Vol. 9  
**Publisher:** Otzar HaChassidim, 770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn  
**Editor:** R' Shalom DovBer Levin  
**Published:** 2001 (for opening of Lubavitch Museum)  
**Length:** 270 lines (short; a focused town history)

---

## Overview

This slim volume serves as a comprehensive town history of Lubavitch — the small town in the Smolensk region of Russia that served as the world center of Chabad Hasidism for 102 years (1813–1915). Published in 2001 in connection with the opening of the Lubavitch Museum, it traces the town's history from its founding through the Holocaust and into the contemporary reconstruction of memorial sites.

---

## The Town's Origins

Lubavitch was founded approximately 500 years ago and first appears in historical documentation in **1521**. The town's name derives from its founder, referred to as "Lubiyetzky" — a Slavic name meaning "lover" or related to love (Russian: любовь / lyubov). For five centuries before Chabad arrived, Lubavitch was an ordinary provincial Russian town.

---

## Chabad's Arrival: 1813

Chabad came to Lubavitch in **1813**, when the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]] (R' DovBer Schneersohn) settled there after the upheavals of the Napoleonic Wars. The Alter Rebbe had died in 1812 while fleeing Napoleon's advance; his successor chose Lubavitch as the new center.

From 1813 forward, Lubavitch became the spiritual capital of an enormous religious movement — its population of perhaps a few thousand housing the Rebbe's court, the yeshiva, administrative offices, a printing house, and the constant flow of pilgrims and supplicants from across the Russian Empire and beyond.

---

## The Rebbe Dynasty in Lubavitch

The book documents each Rebbe's presence in and impact on the town:

**Mitteler Rebbe (in Lubavitch 1813–1827):** Established the town as Chabad's center; built the Rebbe's court infrastructure; died 9 Kislev 5588 (1827). His ohel (memorial tomb) became a pilgrimage site.

**Tzemach Tzedek (1827–1866):** The longest-serving Rebbe presided over Lubavitch for nearly 40 years. He expanded the court's influence and navigated complex politics with the Czarist government, including the debates over Cantonism (forced conscription of Jewish children).

**Rebbe Maharash (1834–1882):** Born in Lubavitch; led the movement for 13 years. His personal residence and the character of the court during the era of increasing Russian antisemitism.

**Rebbe Rashab (1860–1915):** The final Rebbe to be based in Lubavitch. In **1897** he founded **Yeshiva Tomchei Tmimim** in Lubavitch — the flagship institution that trained generations of Chabad emissaries. He led the community through WWI until the German advance made Lubavitch untenable.

---

## The End of Lubavitch as Chabad Center

**1915:** The Rashab relocated from Lubavitch as WWI brought the front dangerously close. Lubavitch's era as Chabad's world center ended after 102 years.

**1918:** Following the Russian Revolution, Yeshiva Tomchei Tmimim went underground.

**1921:** The entire Rebbe's court (the physical building complex) was burned — likely by Bolshevik or revolutionary forces, ending the visible institutional presence.

---

## The Holocaust

**November 1941:** The Nazis murdered all **483 remaining Jews** of Lubavitch. The town that had been Chabad's world center became one of the countless Soviet Jewish communities annihilated in the Holocaust. The entire Jewish population was killed, with virtually no survivors.

---

## Contemporary Memorial

**1989:** The ohel (grave marker) of the Tzemach Tzedek and other Rebbes was rebuilt after decades of Soviet-era destruction or neglect, enabling renewed pilgrimage.

**2001:** The Lubavitch Museum opened, commemorating the town's history and the destroyed community — the occasion for this book's publication.

---

## Historical Significance

The 270-line volume concentrates a century of Chabad history into concise, factual form. Lubavitch's story — from obscure Russian town to world religious center to Holocaust victim to memorial site — encapsulates the entire twentieth-century Jewish experience: the flourishing of religious civilization, its destruction, and the attempt at memory and renewal.
