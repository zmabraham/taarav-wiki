# אדמו"רי חב"ד ויהדות בוכרה

## Chabad Rebbes and Bukharan Jewry

**Series:** מסביב לחסידות (Mesibbv L'Chassidus), Vol. 11 (Vol. 15 in the extended series)  
**Publisher:** Otzar HaChassidim, 770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn  
**Editor:** R' Shalom DovBer Levin  
**Source type:** Regional Jewish history and Chabad-community relations

---

## Overview

This volume documents the relationship between the Chabad Rebbes and the ancient Bukharan Jewish community — one of the most isolated and historically distinct Jewish communities in the world. Bukharan Jews trace their roots to the Jewish communities of Central Asia (present-day Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and surrounding regions) that maintained Jewish life and practice largely separated from mainstream Ashkenazic and Sephardic developments for over a millennium.

The book covers how Chabad reached this remote community, what relationship developed between the Bukharan Jews and successive Chabad Rebbes, and how WWII transformed Bukharan Jewish life by bringing thousands of refugees from occupied Soviet territories into direct contact with the already-established Bukharan Jewish world.

---

## Structure of the Volume

The book is organized into distinct periods:

**Pre-history: Toldot Yehudut Bukhara (History of Bukharan Jewry)**  
Background on the Bukharan Jewish community before significant Chabad contact — their customs, language (Bukhori/Judeo-Tajik), communal organization, and religious practice.

**The Rashab Period (Rebbe Rashab, 1882–1920)**  
The era of initial significant contact between Chabad and Bukharan Jews. The Rashab's leadership of Chabad coincided with the Russian Empire's consolidation of control over Central Asia, which brought Russian Jews and Bukharan Jews into closer contact.

**The Rayatz Period (1920–1950)**  
The Rayatz continued and deepened Chabad's engagement with Bukharan Jewry. During WWII, Bukharan cities — particularly **Samarkand** — became crucial refuge points for Chabad chassidim fleeing the Nazi advance. This encounter between refugees and local Bukharan Jews created lasting bonds.

**Erei Miklat beTekufat haMilchama (Cities of Refuge During the War)**  
The WWII period when Samarkand and other Central Asian cities served as sanctuaries. Chabad chassidim who survived this period (including the memoirists of Books 7 and 8) spent years in these cities.

**After the War**  
Post-war Chabad-Bukharan relations, including the emigration of Bukharan Jews to Israel and New York where they encountered Chabad institutions.

**The Rebbe and Bukharan Jewry**  
The contemporary Rebbe (R' Menachem Mendel Schneerson, 1902–1994) and his particular attention to the Bukharan community.

---

## Historical Significance

The Chabad-Bukharan connection represents an unusual dimension of Chabad history — not the movement's home territory in Russia/Eastern Europe but its reach into a genuinely different Jewish civilization. Bukharan Jews brought distinctive customs, melodies, and practices that enriched both communities when contact deepened. The WWII refugee period, when Ashkenazic Chabad chassidim lived in Bukharan Jewish communities for years, created one of history's more unusual Jewish cultural encounters.

## The Rashab Period in Detail: Rabbi Shlomo Yehuda Eliezrov

The central figure of the Rashab-era section is **Rabbi Shlomo Yehuda Leib Eliezrov** (born 5623/1863, Jekabpils, Latvia) — a Chabad emissary whose biography bridged Eastern Europe, Ottoman Palestine, and Central Asia.

### Family and Background

Eliezrov's great-grandmother was Rebbetzin Menucah Rachel Slonim, a daughter of the Mitteler Rebbe — placing him within the direct Chabad lineage. His father was Rabbi Eliezer Shimon Kazarnovsky of Jekabpils. The family moved to Hebron when Eliezrov was approximately ten years old. He married Chana Bracha Ashkenazi (11 Sivan 5640/1880), whose father Rabbi Dovber Ashkenazi had learned with both the Tzemach Tzedek and the Maharash.

Before his Central Asian mission, Eliezrov co-founded the **Magen Avot yeshiva** in Hebron with his brother-in-law Rabbi Menachem Mendel Na'ah and his cousin Rabbi Shneur Zalman Slonim (who later served 37 years as rabbi of the Tel Aviv-Jaffa Chabad community).

### Shadar Mission Beginnings

Eliezrov began periodic shadar (fundraising emissary) trips to Bukhara and Georgia on behalf of the Hebron Chabad community from approximately 5647 (1887). During one journey (Chanukah 5653/1892) he stopped at Lubavitch and stayed as a guest in the Rashab's courtyard. The Rashab's first letter to him regarding the Bukharan mission is dated 24 Menachem Av 5653 (1893).

### Appointment as Rav of Samarkand (5658/1898)

On **20 Iyar 5658 (May 1898)**, Eliezrov was formally appointed as rabbi of Samarkand — arranged through the Rishon L'Tzion (Sephardic Chief Rabbi of Jerusalem), Rabbi Yaakov Shaul Elyashar. The three-year contract specified a salary of 2,400 rubles annually and required him to rule by Sephardic custom (Shulchan Aruch / Maran), with permission for Ashkenazic stringency where SA permits.

The appointment was endorsed by the Shadei Chamad and by the **Aruch HaShulchan**, who called Eliezrov "a great and distinguished rabbi, sharp and expert, a pious and holy man, a descendant of the Baal HaTanya."

### Transforming Samarkand

Eliezrov's most dramatic intervention was declaring all existing meat in Samarkand **treif** — the shechita practices were deficient. He trained new shochetim, built a new slaughterhouse with gender separation, and established a reliable kosher food supply. The displaced shochetim fought back: they tried to have him arrested by the Amir (who embraced him instead), and attempted poisoning — a dog ate the food and died, saving his life. These episodes earned him the epithet "**Baal Shem Tov of Bukhara**."

He also composed a halakhic responsum (5660) on the Zerafshan River and its tributaries for purposes of gittin (divorce documents) — demonstrating serious scholarship alongside practical leadership.

He founded yeshivot, talmudei Torah, and mikvaot in Samarkand — the institutional infrastructure that later supported thousands of Chabad refugees during World War II.

### Return to Hebron and Later Appointment

After his three-year contract ended (~5661-5662), Eliezrov returned to Hebron. The Shadei Chamad facilitated his appointment as **Rav of Hebron** (~5663/1903). The Rashab gave his enthusiastic blessing.
