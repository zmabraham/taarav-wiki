# תולדות חב"ד בארץ הקודש

## Chabad History in the Holy Land

**Series:** מסביב לחסידות (Mesibbv L'Chassidus), Vol. 3  
**Publisher:** Otzar HaChassidim, 770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn  
**Editor:** R' Shalom DovBer Levin  
**Source type:** Institutional and community history  
**Length:** 2,540 lines; 141 pages, 43 chapters

---

## Overview

This comprehensive volume chronicles Chabad's presence in the Land of Israel from its earliest organized settlement in 1777 through approximately 1950. It covers all four major centers of Jewish life in Eretz Yisrael — Hebron, Jerusalem, Tzfat (Safed), and Tiberias — as well as the later emergence of Tel Aviv as a Chabad center. The book documents not just the history of individual communities but the organizational structures, fundraising networks, and relationships between the Eretz Yisrael Chabad community and the Rebbes in Russia.

---

## The First Aliyah: 1777

The story of Chabad in Eretz Yisrael begins with the **1777 aliyah** of a group of Chassidim who came to the Land of Israel while the Alter Rebbe (the movement's founder) was still establishing Chabad. This early presence predates Chabad's formal crystallization as a distinct movement and reflects the broader Chassidic enthusiasm for settlement in the Holy Land.

---

## Hebron Community (from 1823)

The Hebron community emerged as one of the most significant Chabad centers in Eretz Yisrael. The book documents:

**Institutional infrastructure:**

- Rabbis serving the community across decades
- Synagogues, including the distinctive character of Chabad prayer spaces
- Yeshiva **Magen Avos** (later called **Toras Emes**) — the central Chabad learning institution in Hebron

**The Sephardic-Chabad Agreement:** A formal arrangement was established between the Chabad community and the Sephardic Jewish community already resident in Hebron. This reflects how Chabad newcomers had to negotiate their place within existing communal structures, a theme throughout Chabad's history in Eretz Yisrael.

**The 1834 Hebron Riots:** The book documents the violent upheaval of 1834, when local Arab unrest and regional political turmoil (connected to the Egyptian occupation under Ibrahim Pasha) resulted in devastating attacks on the Jewish community. The Hebron Jewish community suffered grievously. Sir Moses Montefiore's subsequent philanthropic support helped the community rebuild.

---

## Kol Chabad — The Fundraising Network

A distinctive feature of the Eretz Yisrael Chabad community was its organized fundraising structure: **shdarus** (emissaries/messengers) sent to Russia and Europe to collect funds for the community. This network, sometimes called "Kol Chabad," created a financial lifeline connecting the impoverished Holy Land settlers with wealthier diaspora communities.

The dependence on diaspora fundraising also meant that the Chabad community in Eretz Yisrael was deeply integrated into the broader Chabad world. The Rebbes in Lubavitch took active interest in the welfare of the Eretz Yisrael communities, and the shdarus would often seek direct guidance and blessing from the Rebbe during their fundraising journeys.

---

## Jerusalem, Tzfat, Tiberias, Tel Aviv

Beyond Hebron, the book traces Chabad communities in:

- **Jerusalem:** Development of Chabad presence in the capital alongside other Chassidic and Lithuanian communities
- **Tzfat (Safed):** One of the four holy cities with historic Jewish presence; Chabad's role in the mystically-oriented community there
- **Tiberias:** Another of the four holy cities, on the Sea of Galilee
- **Tel Aviv:** The modern city that emerged in the twentieth century, and Chabad's adaptation to urban Zionist Palestine

---

## Historical Significance

This volume documents one of the least-known dimensions of Chabad history: the Chabad community that existed in Eretz Yisrael throughout the nineteenth century, long before the movement became associated with large-scale American and Israeli presence. These early settlers practiced [[concepts/mesirus-nefesh|mesirus nefesh]] (self-sacrifice) in a different mode — not resisting Soviet persecution but simply surviving in poverty, disease, and periodic violence while maintaining Chabad institutions.

The book also illuminates the complex relationship between Chabad and other Jewish communities in Eretz Yisrael: Sephardic communities, other Chassidic groups, the Old Yishuv (pre-Zionist settlement), and eventually the New Yishuv (Zionist pioneers).
