# Undaunted — Biography of the Rayatz

**Source**: English-language biography of Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (6th Lubavitcher Rebbe)  
**Author**: David Eliezrie  
**Coverage**: ~1880–1951, from childhood in Lubavitch through his death in Brooklyn  
**Chapters**: 11 chapters + 2 appendices

---

## Overview

This English-language biography traces the life of [[entities/people/rayatz|Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (the Rayatz)]] — sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe — across five countries and four decades. Written with access to primary Chabad archives, it weaves together personal narrative, institutional history, and geopolitical context. The book is an invaluable English-language companion to the Hebrew histories in this corpus, providing many details not found elsewhere.

The Rayatz is described as having "started from scratch three times" (chasidic historian Menachem Zigelbaum): after his father's death in 1920, after arriving in Riga in 1927, and after reaching New York in 1940.

---

## Chapter 1 — Lubavitch: Childhood and Formation

The Rayatz was born on 12 Tammuz 5640 (1880) in [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]], the eldest son of the Rebbe Rashab and Rebbetzin Shterna Sara. His childhood in Lubavitch was marked by an unusually close relationship with his father.

**The Jail Incident (age 11):** As a boy, he demanded that a Jewish blacksmith imprisoned locally be released before Shabbat. When told this was impossible, he organized a group of boys and they broke the Jew out of jail. The Rashab disciplined him but later privately acknowledged this demonstrated exceptional character.

**The Akedah Ceremony (age 15, 1895):** In the most formative event of his early life, the Rashab called his son into his private chambers and conducted what the Rayatz called "the akedah" (binding of Isaac). The Rashab told him he would be the next Rebbe, explaining that G-d had demanded the "binding" of the son for the sake of the generation. The Rashab wept, the son wept, and the Rayatz later described this as the moment he understood his life's mission.

**Private Secretary**: From age 15, the Rayatz served as his father's private secretary — traveling to conferences in Kovno (1895) and Vilna (1896) to represent the Rashab, managing correspondence with Jewish leaders, and accompanying him on missions to St. Petersburg.

## Chapter 2 — Tomchei Temimim: The Great Yeshiva

The founding of [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|the Rashab's]] flagship institution is described in extraordinary detail.

**The Wedding Gift (1897):** At age 16 (1896), the Rayatz became engaged to Nechama Dina Schneersohn, a second cousin. The Rashab bore all wedding expenses. At the wedding itself (September 10, 1897 / 13 Elul 5657), the Rayatz made a request of his father: "Open a yeshivah that integrates Talmud and Chasidic philosophy." This became his greatest wedding gift.

**The Founding Meeting**: The day of the wedding reception (September 12, 1897 / 15 Elul 5657), the Rashab convened fifty distinguished guests and announced the yeshiva. Skepticism was universal — the guests doubted that young men could be attracted to serious Torah study in an era of Enlightenment and Zionism. The Rashab rejected the skepticism, seeing the youth's receptivity as the key to Jewish revival.

**The Name**: On Simchat Torah 1898, during the seventh hakafah, the Rashab named the yeshiva _Tomchei Temimim_ ("Supporters of the Sincere"), from the verse recited at that point. Students would be called _temimim_ (the sincere ones).

**Rayatz as Director (1898)**: After one year, at age 18, a group of Chasidim asked the Rayatz to become director. He demurred; they appealed to the Rashab, who endorsed the idea. From 24 Elul 5659 (September 11, 1898), the Rayatz served as director — responsible for fundraising, student selection, faculty hiring, and expansion.

**Curriculum Innovation**: Unlike traditional Lithuanian yeshivas (focused exclusively on Talmud) or Musar institutions, Tomchei Temimim:

- Combined 8 hours daily of Talmud/halakha with 4 hours of Chassidic philosophy
- Had a _mashpia_ (spiritual mentor) as its lead educator, not merely a _rosh yeshiva_
- Required student to become a _penimi_ (internalized values), not merely a _gadol_ (great scholar)
- Provided in-house meals (revolutionary vs. the essen teg system)
- Operated branches in Zembin, Doksycy, Koblitz, Vitebsk

**Growth**: From 18 students in Year 1 to 200 by Year 5. Budget grew from 3,120 rubles (1899) to 20,000+ rubles (1903).

**The Poalei Tzion Confrontation (1906)**: When a yeshiva student defected to the Marxist-Zionist Poalei Tzion group, armed confrontation followed. The Rayatz refused to submit to armed demands, declaring "Get out of here! I'm not afraid of your guns." A subsequent mob of 200 shot at the yeshiva; yeshiva students drove them off. The Rayatz carried a gun during this period and narrowly escaped an ambush at a train station.

**Toras Emes in Hebron (1911)**: The Rashab purchased Beit Romano in Hebron (a large complex) and sent Rabbi Zalman Havlin to establish a branch in Eretz Yisrael. The new yeshiva, named Toras Emes, became the first Chasidic yeshiva in the Jewish homeland. WWI forced the Russian students to return; after the Rashab's death, Rabbi Havlin reconstituted it in Jerusalem in 1922. See [[entities/places/eretz-yisrael|Eretz Yisrael]].

## Chapter 3 — The Secret Covenant: Soviet Underground

After the Rashab's death in Rostov (1920), the Rayatz assumed leadership in the most hostile environment any Chabad Rebbe had faced. The Soviet state, through the _Yevsektsiya_ (Jewish Section of the Communist Party), was systematically dismantling Jewish religious life.

The Rayatz organized a clandestine educational network:

- Underground yeshivos in multiple cities
- Secret _maamad_ funds to support activities
- Coded correspondence with underground activists
- Personal visits and public appearances under surveillance

Three weeks before his death, the Rashab told the Rayatz: "There will be difficult times; you will need to have _mesirat nefesh bepoel_ (actual self-sacrifice). But eventually, the Communist government will disintegrate."

## Chapter 4 — Armed Men at Midnight: The 1927 Arrest

See [[events/soviet-persecution|Soviet Persecution of Chabad]] for full detail.

Key points from this source: The Rayatz was arrested by the GPU in Leningrad. He told his interrogators that he recognized only one authority — the Almighty. He was sentenced to death, then commuted to Solovki labor camp, then commuted to three years exile in Kostroma. International pressure secured his release. The liberation date, 12-13 Tammuz 5687 (1927), became the Chabad holiday of _Yud-Beis Tammuz_.

---

## Chapter 5 — Exile to Riga (1927–1933)

The Rayatz arrived in Riga on 25 Tishrei 5688 (October 21, 1927), thousands having seen him off from Leningrad station. He described the overnight train ride as "eighteen hours with anguish in the heart, pain in the soul."

**Riga as Waystation**: The word "temporary" appeared on his Riga stationery. "I fled the bad, but I have not yet found the good." He described the six-year Riga period as one of perpetual searching for a permanent base. Key travels: Berlin, Vienna, Paris, Marienbad (health spas), and extended trips to Eretz Yisrael and America (1929–30).

**Continuing Russia Work**: From Riga, the Rayatz launched 70 letters to Russian Chasidim within his first month. He organized Vaad Magen UMekhaseh (Committee for Protection of Russian Jewry). He coordinated the 1929 Passover Matzah Campaign — an international diplomatic and fundraising effort to ship matzah into the Soviet Union despite the famine, involving negotiations with the Russian ambassador via Latvian MP Rabbi Mordechai Dubin.

**Rabbi Mordechai Dubin**: The most important Jewish figure in Riga — member of Latvian parliament, chairman of the Jewish community, swing-vote holder. A devoted Chasid of the Rayatz. He played a central diplomatic role in negotiations with Soviet and Latvian authorities on the Rayatz's behalf.

**Financial Crisis**: The Rayatz relied on thin donations from small European Chabad communities and American synagogues (most immigrants with weak Chasidic ties). He took loans to keep the Russian underground funded, sometimes taking one loan to repay another.

**Philosophy of "Principled Engagement"**: In Riga, the Rayatz developed his signature approach: neither insular defensiveness nor assimilation, but "principled engagement" — living in and engaging Western society while firmly maintaining Jewish values as the cornerstone.

## Chapter 6 — The Royal Wedding: Chaya Mushka's Marriage

The wedding of the Rayatz's daughter Chaya Mushka to Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson (the Ramash, later the seventh Rebbe) was held in Warsaw in 1928. This cemented the future succession.

---

## Chapter 7 — The Voyage: America (1929–30 Visit)

The Rayatz made an extended trip to Eretz Yisrael and the United States in 1929–1930, his first encounter with American Jewry. He observed the small, weak Chabad community in the US and assessed its potential. He kept detailed notes; this trip prepared him for his eventual permanent immigration a decade later.

---

## Chapter 8 — Poland: Starting Anew (1933–1939)

After six years in Riga, the Rayatz relocated to Poland in 1933 to be closer to the Tomchei Temimim branches in Warsaw. He set up a network of yeshivos across Poland and Lithuania. The wartime Polish period ended on September 1, 1939, when Nazi Germany invaded. The Rayatz narrowly escaped Warsaw; with American diplomatic intervention (particularly Senator James Byrnes) he was evacuated to Riga and then onward to the US.

---

## Chapter 9 — The World Is Shattered: Holocaust Years

During the first years in America (1940 onward), the Rayatz was consumed by two simultaneous missions:

1. **Save the yeshiva students** stranded in Poland, Lithuania, and eventually Japan/Shanghai
2. **Transform American Jewry** from a declining immigrant community into a new center of Torah

**The Shanghai Rescue**: When students in Lithuania realized there was a visa-free route to Curacao (Dutch consular stamps by Jan Zwartendyk), and Japanese consul Chiune Sugihara began issuing transit visas, the path opened: Vilna → Moscow → trans-Siberian railway → Vladivostok → Kobe, Japan → Shanghai. The Rayatz coordinated funding, US Embassy negotiations, and logistics. The Chabad students "broke the ice" — they were among the first groups to use this route. Eventually ~40 Chabad students and ~200 Mir Yeshiva students spent the war in Shanghai. Rabbi Meir Ashkenazi (a Chabad Chasid) was the Ashkenazi chief rabbi there and facilitated the students' stay.

## Chapter 10 — America Iz Nisht Anderesh: Arrival in America (1940)

The Rayatz arrived by ship (SS Drottningholm) on March 19, 1940 (9 Adar II), docking at Pier 97 in Manhattan. He came in a wheelchair but rose to speak to the crowd.

**Famous Declaration**: When a reporter skeptically asked about his plans, the Rayatz declared: _"America iz nisht anderesh — America is no different. I came to make America a center of Torah."_

**770 Eastern Parkway**: The Chasidim initially searched for a suburban home; the Rayatz insisted on the visible and prestigious Crown Heights neighborhood. A three-story brownstone at 770 Eastern Parkway was purchased on 12 Av 5700 (August 16, 1940). It became the iconic Chabad world headquarters.

**American Religious Landscape**: The biography documents the weak state of American Orthodoxy at the time — 75% of NY Jews hadn't attended synagogue in the previous year. Reform and Conservative movements were dominant. Torah Vodaath (Rabbi Mendlowitz) and Yeshiva University (Rabbi Revel) represented different Orthodox approaches. The Rayatz's strategy was neither defensive preservation nor accommodation — it was offensive transformation.

**Tomchei Temimim Opens**: On his first full day in America, the Rayatz announced the opening of a US Tomchei Temimim yeshiva. "If you don't promise me that you will open the yeshivah, I will not go to bed to sleep tonight." Studies began a week later in Congregation Oneg Shabbat in East Flatbush.

## Chapter 11 — A Global Vision: Postwar Reconstruction (1945–1950)

**Weeping for the Fallen**: After the war, the Rayatz sat with pictures of his fallen Chasidim — Rabbis Feigin, Horowitz, and others killed by the Nazis. Uncontrollable tears, only lifted by visits from relatives.

**The Great Escape (1946–47)**: After the war, approximately 1,000 Chabad Chasidim escaped the Soviet Union using forged Polish passports. The operation:

- Chasidim in Uzbekistan (where they had fled Nazi invasion) acquired Polish papers on the black market
- Skilled counterfeiters modified names; women (less conspicuous) served as messengers
- Cattle cars transported groups from Lvov across the Polish border
- A secret rabbinical court of 23 judges in Lvov set guidelines and pooled resources
- The Rayatz coordinated from Brooklyn, communicating in code through intermediaries
- During one border crossing, the Rayatz was observed saying _Az Yashir_ (the Song of the Sea) and swaying as if walking — when he said "they got through," a telegram arrived confirming 232 persons had crossed safely

**Dispersion**: The Rayatz choreographed the resettlement of Chasidim across the globe:

- France (became the nucleus of the largest Jewish community in Europe)
- Australia (6 Chabad families sent to Melbourne and surroundings)
- Canada, US, UK, South Africa, Cuba, Sweden, South America
- Israel (culminating in Kfar Chabad, 1949)

**Global Vision Philosophy**: The Rayatz saw every refugee not as a person seeking haven but as an emissary. "The reason that divine providence has brought you here is to warm up the tepid America with reverence of Heaven and chasidic passion." He insisted Chasidim become self-sufficient and reject permanent dependency on JDC charity.

**The Campuses**: In 1949, the Rayatz sent two yeshiva students to Brandeis University — the first formal Chabad campus outreach. (The seventh Rebbe later expanded this globally, beginning with UCLA in 1969.)

**Kfar Chabad (1949)**: Established for Soviet-era survivors settling in Israel — see [[entities/places/eretz-yisrael|Eretz Yisrael]].

**Passing**: The Rayatz died on 10 Shevat 5710 (January 28, 1950) in Brooklyn.

---

## Afterword: The Succession

The book ends with the succession of the seventh Rebbe. One year after the Rayatz's passing, on 10 Shevat 5711 (1951), a farbrengen was held at 770. The Ramash (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson) had repeatedly refused calls to become Rebbe. When elder Chasid Rabbi Sender Nemtzov rose and called out "We want the Rebbe to say Chasidut!", the Ramash — after a moment of silence — began his first maamar, building on the last maamar the Rayatz had published before his passing. Nemtzov leaped onto a table: "Chasidim, we have a Rebbe!"
