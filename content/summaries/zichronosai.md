# זכרונותי

## Zichronosai (My Memoirs)

**Series:** מסביב לחסידות (Mesibbv L'Chassidus), Vol. 8  
**Publisher:** Otzar HaChassidim, 770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn  
**Editor:** R' Shalom DovBer Levin (son of the author)  
**Author:** R' Yisrael Yehuda Levi  
**Written:** Over 35 years beginning approximately 1960  
**Length:** 1,447 lines

---

## Overview

The memoirs of R' Yisrael Yehuda Levi, born **7 Tammuz 5672 (1912)** in **Zviitov, Ukraine**, constitute one of the most detailed first-person accounts of underground Chabad yeshiva life in the Soviet period. The memoir's composition over 35 years reflects both the complexity of the material and the author's determination to record everything accurately. His son, R' Shalom DovBer Levin — who went on to become the editor of the entire Mesibbv L'Chassidus series — edited the work for publication.

---

## Underground Yeshiva: A Decade of Movement (1927–1938)

R' Yisrael Yehuda spent over a decade moving through Soviet cities as part of the clandestine Tomchei Tmimim network. The itinerary reads like a map of Soviet Jewish geography:

**Vitebsk → Kiev → Baku → Kutaisi → Kolash → Moscow → Chernigov → Malakhovka → Leningrad → Voronezh → Krivoy Rog → Kharkov**

Each city represented a period of study — sometimes months, sometimes a year or more — before the group had to relocate to evade GPU (secret police) surveillance. The Caucasian cities (Baku, Kutaisi) represent the unusual westward extension of the network into Soviet Azerbaijan and Georgia, where less-penetrated communities offered temporary refuge.

---

## Arrests and Survival

R' Yisrael Yehuda was arrested multiple times during this period — occupational hazard of underground religious activity in the Soviet Union. He survived through a combination of courage, wit, and what he understood as miraculous divine assistance.

**The Eyeglasses Miracle:** One of the memoir's most remarkable episodes involves military conscription. Facing induction into the Red Army — which would have meant the end of his religious life and potentially his physical survival given the treatment of observant Jews in the military — he sought guidance from the Rebbe (the Rayatz). The Rebbe's advice was specific: **wear glasses**. R' Yisrael Yehuda obtained and wore glasses, and was exempted from military service on vision grounds. He experienced this as a direct miracle facilitated by the Rebbe's spiritual insight.

He also survived by maintaining **forged papers** with false identities, a necessity for underground religious activists in Stalin's Soviet Union.

---

## World War II: Samarkand

When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, R' Yisrael Yehuda joined the mass flight eastward. He reached **Samarkand** in Soviet Uzbekistan, the Central Asian city that became home to a significant concentration of Chabad chassidim during the war. There, tragedy struck: his **young son died** during the journey or early in Samarkand — a devastating personal loss amid communal crisis.

---

## Escape and Aliyah

In 1946, R' Yisrael Yehuda escaped the Soviet Union using **forged Polish papers** in the name "**Naftali Bat**" — taking on a Polish Jewish identity to join the repatriation movement of Polish citizens. This was the same strategy used by many Chabad chassidim (see also [[summaries/lemaan-yedau|Lemaan Yede'u Banim Yivaledu]]).

Rather than settling in Western Europe or America, R' Yisrael Yehuda chose **Eretz Yisrael**:

- **1949:** Arrived and settled in **Kfar Chabad** — the Chabad village established near Tel Aviv

**First Visit to the Rebbe (Tishrei 5723/1962):** After more than a decade in Kfar Chabad, R' Yisrael Yehuda made his first trip to Brooklyn for the Tishrei holidays — the annual pilgrimage that tens of thousands of Chabad chassidim make to be near the Rebbe. His account of this visit conveys the emotional weight of a chassid finally reaching his Rebbe after decades of separation and persecution.

---

## Historical Significance

Zichronosai is remarkable for its geographic breadth (twelve cities in a decade), its insider account of underground yeshiva organization, and its personal honesty about fear, loss, and survival. R' Yisrael Yehuda's trajectory — from Soviet underground to Israeli Chabad village — represents one major route by which Soviet-era Chabad chassidim rebuilt their lives. That his son became the editor of this entire historical series gives the work an additional dimension of family and institutional legacy.
