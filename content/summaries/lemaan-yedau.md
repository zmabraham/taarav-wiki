# למען ידעו בנים יוולדו

## Lemaan Yede'u Banim Yivaledu (So That Children Yet to Be Born Will Know)

**Series:** מסביב לחסידות (Mesibbv L'Chassidus), Vol. 7  
**Publisher:** Otzar HaChassidim, 770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn  
**Editor:** R' Shalom DovBer Levin  
**Author:** M. Shusterman  
**Length:** 1,625 lines

---

## Overview

The memoirs of M. Shusterman, born **1 Elul 5674 (1914)** in **Zlobin** (a town between Gomel and Bobruisk in Belarus), cover the full arc of a Chabad chassid's life from Soviet-era yeshiva study through WWII survival, escape from the USSR, Paris exile, and eventual settlement in America — where he found his calling as a printer of Chabad publications.

The title, drawn from Psalms (78:6), frames the memoirs as legacy-transmission: recording memories so that future generations will know what their ancestors experienced.

---

## Zlobin and Its Historical Connection

Shusterman begins with his hometown of Zlobin, which carried significant Chabad historical resonance: a famous wedding of the **Mitteler Rebbe's daughter to a grandson of R' Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev** took place there. This connection anchors the memoir in Chabad dynastic history from its opening.

---

## Underground Yeshiva Years

Shusterman studied in the clandestine Tomchei Tmimim network during the Soviet era, moving through multiple cities as the underground yeshiva relocated to evade authorities:

- **Vitebsk** — Chabad's historical heartland
- **Mogilev** — significant Jewish city
- **Kherson** — the southern yeshiva center

The underground yeshiva experience, shared with the memoirist in Book 8 (Zichronosai), paints a consistent picture: small groups of students studying in back rooms, constant movement, forged documents, and the ever-present threat of arrest.

---

## Marriage and WWII

Shusterman married during the Soviet era — an act of continuity amid crisis. When WWII reached the Soviet Union after Germany's invasion in 1941, he found himself in **Moscow** and later in **Samarkand** (Uzbekistan), one of the Central Asian refuge cities where thousands of Jews from occupied Soviet territory fled.

---

## Escape from the USSR

After the war, Shusterman escaped the Soviet Union using the strategy employed by many Chabad chassidim: claiming Polish citizenship and joining the repatriation of Polish refugees to Poland. From Poland the path westward continued:

- **Paris (1947):** A significant number of Chabad refugees gathered in post-war Paris before further emigration decisions
- **America:** Final destination

---

## Work in America: Printing for Chabad

In America, Shusterman found his most significant contribution: he worked as a **Linotype typesetter** for Chabad publications at **770 Eastern Parkway** (Chabad's world headquarters in Brooklyn). His technical skills contributed directly to the physical production of Chabad literature.

His most notable achievement was participating in the printing of **the Tanya** and other major Chabad works. Given the Tanya's status as Chabad's foundational text, composed by the Alter Rebbe and central to all Chabad study and life, Shusterman's role in physically producing it represents a form of sacred work — the continuation through print of the oral/manuscript tradition he had studied underground.

---

## Historical Significance

Shusterman's memoirs are representative of an entire cohort: the generation born in Russia shortly before the Revolution, who spent their formative years in underground yeshivos, survived WWII in Soviet Central Asia, and then successfully escaped to rebuild their lives in the West. His specific contribution — the work of printing — highlights how Chabad's post-war revival depended on both spiritual leadership and technical infrastructure.
