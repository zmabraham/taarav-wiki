# Tzimtzum in the Hemshech vs. the Tanya: A Comparative Analysis

> How the Rashab's treatment of Tzimtzum in the Hemshech Taarav develops and diverges from the Alter Rebbe's foundational account in the Tanya

---

## Introduction

The Tzimtzum — the "contraction" or "withdrawal" of the infinite Or Ein Sof to make room for created worlds — is the foundational event of Lurianic Kabbala. Every Chabad work deals with it, but not identically. The Tanya (Shneur Zalman of Liadi, the Alter Rebbe, 1796) and the Hemshech Taarav (Shalom DovBer Schneersohn, the Rashab, 1901–1920) represent two distinct intellectual moments in Chabad thought, separated by a century and five generations. Their treatments of Tzimtzum agree on basics but diverge in emphasis, application, and depth in ways that illuminate the Rashab's specific contribution.

---

## Common Ground: Tzimtzum as Not-Literal

Both the Tanya and the Hemshech Taarav maintain the fundamental Chabad principle: *Tzimtzum kiPshuto eino* — the Tzimtzum is not to be understood literally. God did not vacate a region of space; the Ein Sof did not "withdraw" in any spatial sense. Rather, the Tzimtzum describes a *relational* change in how the infinite light relates to the possibility of finite existence.

Both works ground this in the Reshimu: the "impression" or "residue" of Or Ein Sof that remains after the Tzimtzum and serves as the root of all created worlds. If the Tzimtzum were absolute, there would be nothing; the Reshimu is why "something" can exist at all.

Both also affirm that from the divine perspective, *lo nafka minei kelal* — "nothing changed at all." The Or Ein Sof is not diminished by the Tzimtzum; the infinite remains infinite. The change is in the *appearance* of creation's independence, not in God's actual reality.

---

## The Tanya's Frame: Tzimtzum and the Two-Level Universe

The Tanya's primary concern with Tzimtzum is soteriological and ethical: it explains *why there can be evil*. The fundamental structure is:

1. God is entirely good (*tov l'chol*)
2. Yet created beings include evil, ego, and the *klipot*
3. If God is infinite and everywhere, how can there be a "place" for evil?

The Tzimtzum's function in the Tanya is to create the *possibility of concealment* — a world where the divine light is not manifest, where creatures can perceive themselves as independent, where choice (including the choice of evil) becomes possible. The *Sitra Achra* (the Other Side) exists in the "space" created by the Tzimtzum's concealment.

The practical application in the Tanya is the *beinoni* (intermediate person) framework: because the divine light is present but concealed in this world (due to the Tzimtzum), the beinoni can maintain Torah-conduct even while the animal soul (*nefesh habehemit*) continues to feel its contrary drives. The work of divine service is to *reveal* the Or Pnimi that the Tzimtzum concealed — to make God's presence felt from within the world's apparent opacity.

---

## The Hemshech's Frame: Tzimtzum as Structure of Revelation

The Rashab's treatment in the Hemshech Taarav shifts emphasis dramatically. The Tzimtzum is not primarily about explaining evil or enabling free choice — it is about explaining **how revelation is possible at all**.

The hemshech's working paradox is: if Or Ein Sof is truly infinite, any finite contact with it would consume the finite entirely. A person before infinite light would not be illuminated — they would be annihilated. The Tzimtzum's function is therefore not to enable *concealment* but to enable **genuine disclosure at an appropriate scale**.

This appears strikingly in the year תרד"ע (1903-04) treatment of *Elokim* at Sinai:

> "Why does Scripture use Shem Elokim for the giving of Torah, when Matan Torah was the greatest divine revelation in history?"

The Tanya's answer would emphasize: Elokim is the divine name associated with the *tzimtzum*-concealment that allows the world to exist — so Torah is given through the concealment that makes finite reception possible.

The Rashab's answer goes further: *Elokim* at Sinai represents **rommemut atzmi** — essential transcendence that is *not* self-perceived. The Tzimtzum is not a diminishment but the specific divine mode (self-transcendence) that allows infinite wisdom to be shared with finite minds *without consuming them*. Torah was given through the Elokim-mode because only through essential self-transcendence can the infinite share itself generatively.

---

## Key Divergence 1: Or Mekif and the Tzimtzum's "Purpose"

In the Tanya, the primary lights discussed are **Or Pnimi** (the inner light that invests in vessels) and an *implicit* Tzimtzum-concealment that enables this investment. Or Mekif appears but is not the central category.

In the Hemshech Taarav, **Or Mekif** (encompassing light) is elevated to equal status with Or Pnimi and becomes the lens through which the Tzimtzum is re-examined:

- The Tzimtzum creates the possibility of Or Pnimi (inner light within vessels)
- But it simultaneously *enables* Or Mekif to maintain its character as "encompassing" rather than consuming
- Pre-Tzimtzum, the Or Ein Sof was so pervasive that there was no "inside" or "outside" — no difference between Or Pnimi and Or Mekif modes
- Post-Tzimtzum, these two modes are differentiated: Or Mekif encompasses without penetrating; Or Pnimi penetrates without overwhelming

The Tzimtzum in the hemshech is thus the event that **creates the Or Mekif / Or Pnimi distinction itself**. This is a more structural reading than the Tanya's: not "the Tzimtzum hides the light so we can survive" but "the Tzimtzum differentiates light into modes, enabling a richer divine-created relationship."

---

## Key Divergence 2: The Reshimu and the Kav

Both texts discuss the Reshimu (impression remaining after Tzimtzum) and the Kav (the thin "line" or "beam" of Or Ein Sof that enters the post-Tzimtzum space). But their emphases differ:

**In the Tanya**, the Reshimu and Kav explain metaphysical structure but are not the focus of extended analysis. The Tanya's reader is directed toward the practical: the divine light is present (via Reshimu/Kav), I can access it through Torah and mitzvot, and that is what matters.

**In the Hemshech**, the Kav becomes a site of extended analysis in תרע"ג (1902-03):
- The Kav contains the *ע"ס הגנוזות* (ten hidden Sefirot) in a pre-individuated state
- These hidden Sefirot differ qualitatively from the revealed Sefirot of Atzilut
- The distinction between Sefirot-in-Kav and Sefirot-in-Atzilut maps onto the hidden/revealed distinction that runs throughout the hemshech
- The Kav's Or Chozer (the capacity of the Kav's light to reflect back toward Ein Sof) is the metaphysical ground of prayer's efficacy

The Rashab thus treats the Reshimu and Kav not as transitional concepts on the way to more practical analysis, but as the *structural site* where Or Yashar and Or Chozer first meet — the original locus of the divine-created encounter.

---

## Key Divergence 3: Tzimtzum and Ta'anug

The Tanya does not develop an analysis of **Ta'anug haAtzmi** (essential divine delight) as a pre-Tzimtzum reality. The Tanya's primary Keter analysis focuses on Ratzon (will) and the structure of the Sefirot from Chochma downward.

The Hemshech's major advance in תרד"ע (1903-04) is precisely this: Ta'anug haAtzmi is identified as a dimension of Keter that is:
1. **Deeper than Ratzon** — it is not the divine will but the divine delight that precedes and grounds all will
2. **Pre-Tzimtzum** — the Tzimtzum occurred within the Ratzon-dimension; Ta'anug haAtzmi is by its nature not subject to Tzimtzum because it does not "face" the world at all
3. **The level Teshuva reaches** — Teshuva uniquely bypasses the post-Tzimtzum Ratzon structure to touch the pre-Tzimtzum Ta'anug

This creates a profound difference in what "reaching God" can mean:
- In the Tanya's framework, the highest a person can reach is the Or Mekif level of the divine light as structured by the Kav
- In the Hemshech's framework, Teshuva (and by extension, Yechida-level Or Chozer) reaches *beyond* the Tzimtzum structure itself — to the Ta'anug that precedes all structure

---

## Key Divergence 4: The Hemshech's "Higher" Tzimtzum

The most radical development in the Hemshech Taarav is the identification of what might be called a "higher Tzimtzum" — the *tzimtzum of Ta'anug into Ratzon*.

Before the cosmological Tzimtzum (the contraction of Or Ein Sof to make space for worlds), the Rashab identifies a prior intra-divine dynamic: Ta'anug is so inward and self-sufficient that it requires a kind of self-contraction to generate Ratzon (will toward an other). This "contraction of delight into will" is the pre-cosmic Tzimtzum that makes the cosmological Tzimtzum possible.

The Tanya does not develop this analysis. Its Tzimtzum is the event that creates the space for worlds — it is not preceded by an internal divine dynamic of the same type.

For the Rashab, this means:
- The cosmological Tzimtzum is a **second-order event**, mirroring a first-order dynamic within the divine Keter itself
- When we understand what the Tzimtzum is "doing," we are not just explaining world-creation but understanding something about the inner movement of the divine life
- The divine is not a static infinite that contracts for our sake; the contraction is *structurally native to how infinite delight generates will*

---

## The Elokim Name: Convergence and Divergence

Both the Tanya and the Hemshech treat *Shem Elokim* (the divine name associated with Tzimtzum) as distinct from the *Shem Havaya* (the name associated with the infinite beyond Tzimtzum). Both affirm that in reality (*ba'emet*) there is no difference — "You are Elokim and You are Havaya" (*Atah hu Elokim v'Atah hu Hashem*).

But the practical valence differs:
- **In the Tanya**, Elokim is the name of divine concealment within nature (*ha-teva*, which has the same numerical value as *Elokim*). The work is to pierce this concealment and restore the sense of divine presence.
- **In the Hemshech**, Elokim at Sinai is not concealment but *rommemut atzmi* — divine self-transcendence. Elokim is not the name of God hiding behind nature but of God relating to creation in the mode of *not-asserting-its-own-existence*, which is precisely what enables finite beings to genuinely receive.

This is a subtle but important shift: the Tanya orients us toward piercing Elokim-concealment; the Hemshech orients us toward understanding Elokim-structure as itself an expression of the divine's highest mode of relating.

## Summary Table

| Dimension | Tanya | Hemshech Taarav |
|-----------|-------|-----------------|
| Primary function of Tzimtzum | Enables evil / free choice by concealment | Enables genuine finite reception of infinite by creating Or Pnimi / Or Mekif distinction |
| Elokim name | Concealment within nature (*teva*) | *Rommemut atzmi* — divine self-transcendence enabling revelation |
| Or Mekif | Present but secondary | Central structural category alongside Or Pnimi |
| Kav and hidden Sefirot | Transitional concept | Extended analysis; site of Or Yashar / Or Chozer meeting |
| Ta'anug haAtzmi | Not developed | Major advance: pre-Tzimtzum, reached by Teshuva |
| Ceiling of human ascent | Or Mekif via Or Pnimi | Ta'anug (via Teshuva / Yechida Or Chozer) and ultimately Atzmus (via collective Nitzavim Or Chozer) |
| "Higher Tzimtzum" | Not discussed | Ta'anug contracting into Ratzon as pre-cosmological dynamic |

---

## Significance

The Hemshech Taarav does not contradict the Tanya; it builds on it at a different register. Where the Tanya is a practical manual for the spiritual life, the Hemshech is a sustained metaphysical inquiry into the structure of divine reality.

The Tzimtzum in the Tanya is the condition of our situation: we live in a world where the divine is concealed, and our work is to reveal it. The Tzimtzum in the Hemshech is a structural feature of how infinite and finite can genuinely meet — not a problem to be solved but a principle to be understood. This shift from "overcome the concealment" to "understand the structure of revelation" marks the Hemshech's distinctive contribution to Chabad metaphysics.
