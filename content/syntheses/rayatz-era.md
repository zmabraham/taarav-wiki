# The Rayatz Era (1880–1950)

**Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn — Leading Through Soviet Terror, War, and Exile**

---

## Overview

The Rayatz era is the most dramatic in Chabad history: thirty years of leadership (1920–1950) through Soviet persecution, a death sentence, international rescue, exile, the Holocaust, a miraculous escape from Nazi-occupied Warsaw, and the rebuilding of Chabad on American soil. Chassidic historian Menachem Zigelbaum said the Rayatz "started from scratch three times" — after his father's death in 1920, after arriving in Riga in 1927, and after reaching New York in 1940.

The Rayatz was born on 12 Tammuz 5640 (1880) in Lubavitch to the Rashab and Rebbetzin Shterna Sara — born into the very center of the Chabad world. From age 15, when his father conducted the "Akedah" ceremony designating him as future Rebbe, his entire life was oriented toward leadership. He served as director of Tomchei Temimim from age 18, representing his father at conferences, lobbying Russian officials in St. Petersburg, and managing the entire apparatus of the movement.

When the Rashab died in 1920, the Rayatz inherited leadership amid catastrophe. The Bolsheviks had seized power, the Yevsektsiya (Jewish Section of the Communist Party) was actively dismantling Jewish religious infrastructure, and Chabad chassidim were being arrested, exiled, and killed. The Rayatz's response was extraordinary: he organized an underground network of yeshivos, religious schools, matzah bakeries, and ritual life that operated across the Soviet Union for years, sustained by coded correspondence and networks of trusted messengers.

His 1927 arrest — at midnight on 15 Sivan — and the subsequent death sentence shocked the world. An international rescue campaign mobilized American Jewish organizations, US State Department officials, German parliamentarians (including Dr. Oskar Cohn), and even the German Foreign Ministry. The death sentence was commuted to three years' exile in Kostroma; the exile was then converted to expulsion from the USSR. On 12-13 Tammuz 5687 (1927), now celebrated annually as *Chag HaGeulah* (the Festival of Liberation), the Rayatz left Soviet soil and arrived in Riga, Latvia.

From Riga (1927–1933) and then Warsaw/Otwock (1933–1940), the Rayatz rebuilt Chabad's institutional life — reestablishing Tomchei Temimim, maintaining contact with remaining communities inside the USSR, publishing Chassidic literature, and sending emissaries across Europe and to the United States and Eretz Yisrael. His daughter Chaya Mushka's wedding to Menachem Mendel Schneerson in Warsaw in 1928 — attended by thousands — was the defining dynastic event of this period.

When Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, the Rayatz was in Otwock. Another rescue mission — this time involving American diplomatic pressure on Nazi Germany at the highest levels — succeeded in extracting him from Warsaw through a harrowing escape that passed through Germany, Sweden, and Latvia before arriving in New York on March 9, 1940.

In America, the Rayatz famously declared "America is not different" (*Amerika iz nisht andersh*) — the assimilationist argument that America's culture made traditional Jewish practice impossible would not be accepted. He established Tomchei Temimim in America, founded Kehot Publication Society, Merkos L'Inyonei Chinuch, and Machne Israel, acquired 770 Eastern Parkway as Chabad's world headquarters, and began the global outreach that his successor the 7th Rebbe would dramatically expand.

The Rayatz died on 10 Shevat 5710 (January 28, 1950), in Brooklyn. His son-in-law Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson accepted the Rebbiship exactly one year later, on 10 Shevat 5711.

## Theological Legacy

### Absolute Defiance of Soviet Power
The Rayatz's theological contribution was not primarily textual but existential. His public refusal to compromise with Soviet demands — captured in his response to GPU interrogators that he was unafraid of their pistols, for "you can only threaten one who has one world" (physical existence); "I have two worlds" — became a defining Chabad statement of spiritual courage.

The Rashab had prophesied that the Rayatz would need *mesirat nefesh b'poel* (actual self-sacrifice, not merely willingness). The Rayatz embodied this: he genuinely risked death, accepted a death sentence, and chose defiance over compromise.

### "America Is Not Different"
The Rayatz's declaration upon arriving in America — that American culture does not make traditional Jewish practice impossible — was more than a slogan. It represented a theological argument: the Torah is not a product of Eastern European circumstances but a divine eternal truth that can and must be lived in every environment. This argument, which seemed quixotic in 1940, was vindicated by the extraordinary success of Chabad's American outreach.

### Building Institutions in Exile
The Rayatz's most practical contribution was institutional: creating the network of organizations (Kehot, Merkos, Machne Israel, 770) that would serve as the infrastructure for the 7th Rebbe's global expansion. He understood that Chabad's survival required not just passionate individuals but durable institutions.

### Memoirs and Documentation
The Rayatz was an active writer of memoirs, letters, and diaries that provide invaluable historical documentation of Chabad life in the Soviet era and Polish interwar period. His *Zichronot* (memoirs) and extensive correspondence are primary historical sources.
