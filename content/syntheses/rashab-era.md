# The Rashab Era (1860–1920)

**Rabbi Shalom DovBer Schneersohn — Founding Tomchei Temimim; Confronting Modernity**

---

## Overview

The Rashab era stands as one of the most intellectually rich and institutionally decisive in Chabad history. Rabbi Shalom DovBer Schneersohn, the fifth Rebbe, presided over 38 years of leadership (1882–1920) against the backdrop of the late Czarist empire's disintegration: pogroms, World War I, the Russian Revolution, and the Bolshevik takeover that would threaten the entire fabric of Jewish religious life.

The Rashab's central achievement was the founding of **Tomchei Temimim** yeshiva in 1897 — a revolutionary institution that combined rigorous Talmud study with Chassidic philosophy, producing the cadre of dedicated scholars who would become the backbone of Chabad's survival under Soviet persecution and its global expansion under the 7th Rebbe. The Rayatz requested the yeshiva as a "wedding gift," and the Rashab made it his life's project: building it from 18 students to hundreds, establishing branches across Russia, and eventually extending it to Eretz Yisrael (as *Toras Emes* in Hebron).

The Rashab was also the most prolific Chabad author since the Alter Rebbe. His masterwork, the *Hemshech Samech-Vav* (the connected discourses of the years 5666/1906), is considered one of the deepest works of Kabbalistic and Chassidic thought ever written. He produced an enormous body of discourses, letters, and teachings covering virtually every dimension of Jewish life and thought.

Externally, the Rashab faced unprecedented challenges:
- The rise of Zionism, which he opposed on theological grounds (seeing it as a secular usurpation of the Jewish redemptive process)
- The Haskalah's continued assault on traditional Jewish life
- World War I, which displaced Chabad from Lubavitch in 1915 — ending the 102-year Lubavitch era
- The Russian Revolution (1917) and the Bolshevik seizure of power

The Rashab died in Rostov-on-Don on 2 Nissan 5680 (1920), having lived long enough to see the Bolsheviks triumph but not long enough to see the full horror of what Soviet rule would mean. His son, the Rayatz, inherited leadership in the worst possible circumstances and immediately set about building Chabad's underground resistance.

## The Rebbe

**Full name:** Rabbi Shalom DovBer Schneersohn  
**Born:** 20 Cheshvan 5621 (1860), Lubavitch  
**Died:** 2 Nissan 5680 (1920), Rostov-on-Don  
**Buried:** Rostov-on-Don (original grave); later reinterred  
**Rebbe years:** 1882–1920 (38 years)  
**Acronym:** Rashab (Moreinu HaRav Shalom Ber)  
**Major work:** Hemshech Samech-Vav (5666/1905–1906) — deepest work of Chabad Kabbalah  
**Chabad-wiki:** [[entities/rebbe-rashab|rebbe-rashab.md]], [CW] E002-rashab, P0129-the-rashab

The Rashab was the only son of the Maharash to receive the main Lubavitch succession. He suffered from respiratory problems throughout his life, making extended stays in Central European health resorts (Marienbad, Vienna, Würzburg, Berlin) a necessity — and these visits became opportunities for contact with Western Orthodoxy. He had an exceptionally close relationship with his son the Rayatz, whom he designated as successor through an extraordinary private ceremony ("the Akedah") when the boy was 15.

## Theological Legacy

### Hemshech Samech-Vav (1905–1906)
The Rashab's greatest work is the *Hemshech Samech-Vav* — a series of connected Chassidic discourses delivered over several years, beginning on Shavuot 5666 (1906). These discourses represent perhaps the deepest sustained treatment of Kabbalistic themes in Chabad literature: the nature of divine light, the relationship between the infinite (Ein Sof) and finite creation, the structure of the Sefirot, and the spiritual mechanics of redemption.

The work is "hemshech" (connected) because the discourses form a single extended argument that cannot be understood in fragments — each section builds on the last. It remains required study for serious students of Chabad Chassidus.

### Tomchei Temimim — A Revolution in Jewish Education
The Rashab's pedagogical innovation was to create a yeshiva that combined equal emphasis on halachic scholarship and Chassidic philosophy. Traditional Lithuanian yeshivas focused exclusively on Talmud; Musar yeshivas added ethical training. Tomchei Temimim added *Chassidus* — the inner dimension of Torah — as a daily discipline equal in weight to Talmud.

The results were transformative. Students emerged as *temimim* — "whole ones" — whose intellectual training, emotional refinement, and commitment to Jewish practice were integrated. These students would form the core of Chabad's underground resistance in Soviet Russia and the generation of emissaries sent worldwide by the 7th Rebbe.

### Opposition to Zionism
The Rashab was the most vocal Chabad opponent of political Zionism, writing against it in strong terms. He argued that Zionism substituted national political identity for the deeper spiritual essence of Judaism and would corrupt Jewish youth by replacing Torah with nationalism. His opposition was theological, not anti-Zionist in the sense of opposing residence in Eretz Yisrael — he himself founded a yeshiva there. He objected specifically to the secular nationalist movement.

### The German Orthodox Connection
The Rashab's repeated visits to Germany created a unique cross-cultural relationship between Eastern European Chassidism and Western German Orthodoxy. He met with Azriel Hildesheimer, Meir Hildesheimer, Shlomo Zalman Breuer, Yitzchak Eisik HaLevi, and others — representing very different strands of Orthodox thought. These relationships would become important when Germany became a lifeline for the Rayatz's liberation in 1927.
