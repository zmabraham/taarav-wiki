# The Maharash Era (1834–1882)

**Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn — "L'Chatchila Ariber"**

---

## Overview

The Maharash era, though relatively brief (16 years of active leadership, 1866–1882), represents a period of consolidation and diplomatic advocacy in Chabad history. Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn led Chabad during the comparatively liberal reign of Czar Alexander II — a period of tentative Jewish emancipation that made European travel and lobbying more feasible — and immediately before the catastrophic pogroms of 1881–1882 that would shatter the relative optimism of the reform era.

The Maharash was born in Lubavitch on 2 Iyar 5594 (1834) as the youngest son of the Tzemach Tzedek, and it was he — not his older brothers, who established their own courts — who inherited the main Lubavitch line. His mother, Rebbetzin Rivkah, who survived her husband by many decades, was a formidable personality in her own right.

The Maharash's most lasting contribution to Chabad culture was not a theological innovation but a behavioral maxim: **"L'Chatchila Ariber"** — "from the outset, go over." Where others say "if you cannot go under [an obstacle], go around it," the Maharash taught that one should attack obstacles directly, not seek workarounds. This aphorism captured his personality — energetic, direct, uncompromising — and became embedded in Chabad's culture of facing challenges head-on.

Diplomatically, the Maharash continued the Tzemach Tzedek's tradition of Rebbe-as-advocate. He traveled repeatedly to Western European capitals — Berlin, Vienna, Paris — to meet with government ministers and influential Jewish figures on behalf of Russian Jewry. These trips also brought him into contact with Western Orthodox leaders (particularly in Germany) and established relationships that his son the Rashab would continue.

The Maharash died on 13 Tishrei 5643 (1882) in Lubavitch, in the immediate aftermath of the wave of pogroms (1881) that had shaken Russian Jewry to its foundations and triggered mass emigration. His son, the young Rashab — just 21 years old — inherited leadership under deeply traumatic circumstances.

## The Rebbe

**Full name:** Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn  
**Born:** 2 Iyar 5594 (1834), Lubavitch  
**Died:** 13 Tishrei 5643 (October 1882), Lubavitch  
**Buried:** Lubavitch, Belarus  
**Rebbe years:** 1866–1882 (16 years)  
**Famous teaching:** "L'Chatchila Ariber" — "from the outset, go over"  
**Acronym:** Maharash (Moreinu HaRav Shmuel)  
**Chabad-wiki:** [[entities/rebbe-maharash|rebbe-maharash.md]], [CW] E030-rebbe-maharash, P0009-shmuel-maharash

The Maharash was the seventh and youngest son of the Tzemach Tzedek. His older brothers had established courts at Kopust, Niezhin, Liadi, and elsewhere — a fragmentation the Maharash could not reverse but navigated diplomatically. His mother Rebbetzin Rivkah played an important role in the community during his leadership and long afterward.

## Theological Legacy

### "L'Chatchila Ariber" — The Maharash's Maxim
The Maharash is best remembered in Chabad culture for this single aphorism: "The world says, if you can't go under, go around. I say: from the outset, go over." This phrase — *l'chatchila ariber* in Yiddish — encapsulates an attitude toward life's obstacles that Chabad adopted as a cultural norm. Rather than accommodation, workaround, or incremental compromise, the ideal is direct confrontation and transcendence.

This teaching is applied at multiple levels: personal spiritual work (don't manage your animal soul, transcend it); communal challenges (don't accommodate anti-Semitic restrictions, fight them); and the broader question of Jewish life in diaspora (don't assimilate, maintain Jewish distinctiveness at a higher level).

### Chassidic Discourses
The Maharash produced a substantial body of Chassidic discourses, collected in his *Likkutei Torah* and other works. His style was distinctive — more accessible than his father's encyclopedic works, yet still deeply philosophical. He emphasized the concepts of *ratzo v'shov* (running and returning) in divine service — the alternating states of spiritual ascent and grounding in practical life.

### The Diplomat-Rebbe
The Maharash cemented the tradition, begun by the Tzemach Tzedek, of the Rebbe as international advocate. His travels to Berlin, Vienna, and Paris — meeting with Bismarck's ministers, Rothschild, and other Jewish communal leaders — established Chabad's engagement with the wider world. Unlike the Baal Shem Tov's era, when the Rebbe's world was the shtetl and the forest, the Maharash moved through capitals and salons, representing Russian Jewry to those with power.

### The Pogrom Context
The Maharash died weeks after the great wave of pogroms of 1881, which shook the foundations of Russian Jewish life. His era thus ends at a historical inflection point: the beginning of mass emigration, the radicalization of Jewish youth (toward Zionism, socialism, and other ideologies), and the end of any realistic hope for Jewish civic emancipation within the Czarist system. His son the Rashab would have to respond to this transformed world.
