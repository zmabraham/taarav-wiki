# Panevezys (Ponevezh)

> The Lithuanian city whose great yeshiva was destroyed by the Holocaust yet reborn in Israel — carrying its name and its learning to the modern world.

![Panevezys city view](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/29/Pnv2_by_Augustas_Didzgalvis.jpg/800px-Pnv2_by_Augustas_Didzgalvis.jpg)
*Panevezys, Lithuania's fifth-largest city, on the banks of the Nevezis River*

## Overview

Panevezys (Lithuanian: Panevėžys; Yiddish/Hebrew: פּאָנעװעזש, *Ponevezh*) is a city in northeastern Lithuania, situated along the Nevezis River. Lithuania's fifth-largest city, it has a current population of approximately 85,000. The city was formally granted building rights by Grand Duke Alexander Jagiellon on September 7, 1503, though settlement in the area predates this charter. Its name derives from the Lithuanian words meaning "above the Nevezis."

Throughout its history, Panevezys served as a multicultural hub of the eastern Baltic region, home to Lithuanian, Jewish, Polish, Russian, German, and other communities. By the early twentieth century it had become a significant commercial and cultural center of the Aukstaitija region — the northern highlands of Lithuania — with bustling markets, artisan workshops, and a vigorous Jewish communal and intellectual life.

Today Panevezys is known as an industrial city, home to optical and electronic manufacturing. Its old Jewish quarter and most of its pre-war heritage were erased by the Nazi occupation, leaving behind memorials and memories where a living civilization once stood.

## The Jewish Community of Ponevezh

The Jewish community of Ponevezh was one of the most vibrant in Lithuania, a country that had earned the title *Lita* (Lithuania) as the heartland of traditional rabbinic learning. The Jews of Ponevezh spoke Yiddish, maintained a rich network of synagogues, study houses (*batei midrash*), schools, charitable organizations, and political movements. By the 1923 Lithuanian census, the city's Jewish population stood at 6,845 — representing approximately 36% of the total urban population of 19,147.

Economically, the Jews of Ponevezh were involved in trade, crafts, small manufacturing, and the liberal professions. The city's market economy depended heavily on Jewish merchants and artisans. Politically, the interwar period saw Ponevezh's Jews engaged with a full spectrum of Jewish political life — Bundists, Zionists of various factions, Agudat Israel, and communal traditionalists all competed for communal leadership.

Ponevezh was a city of Torah. Multiple synagogues, shtieblakh (small prayer houses), and batei midrash were active. The city's rabbinical tradition was anchored in the Litvish (Lithuanian Orthodox) approach — rigorous Talmudic scholarship, rational analysis, and a Mitnagdic (non-Hasidic) orientation that had historically defined Lithuanian Jewish intellectual culture in contrast to the Hasidic movements of Poland, Ukraine, and Galicia.

## The Ponevezh Yeshiva

The institution that made Ponevezh world-famous in the Jewish world was its yeshiva. The Ponevezh Yeshiva was established in 1919 by Rabbi Yitzhak Yaakov Rabinovich and became one of the leading Lithuanian (Litvish) yeshivas of the interwar period, training hundreds of students in the demanding Lithuanian method of Talmudic analysis.

After Rabbi Rabinovich's death, the institution passed to Rabbi Yosef Shlomo Kahaneman (1886–1969), who became its towering figure. Born in 1886, Kahaneman had served as rabbi of Ponevezh and built the yeshiva into a flagship of Lithuanian Orthodoxy. When he traveled to Mandatory Palestine in 1940 on a fundraising trip, the outbreak of war prevented his return. He alone of the yeshiva's leadership survived.

From Palestine — and later Israel — Rabbi Kahaneman rebuilt the Ponevezh Yeshiva in Bnei Brak beginning in 1944, creating what became one of the most significant haredi educational institutions in the world, with over three thousand students at its peak. The main study hall features a sixteenth-century Italian wooden Torah ark, and the institution's graduates have spread across the haredi world carrying the Ponevezh tradition of rigorous Talmudic learning.

The Ponevezh Yeshiva of Bnei Brak operates to this day as a living memorial to the destroyed Jewish civilization of Lithuania — a civilization whose epicenter in Ponevezh itself was annihilated.

## The Holocaust

The Nazi occupation of Lithuania beginning in June 1941 brought immediate and catastrophic destruction to the Jewish community of Panevezys. The German Army and their Lithuanian collaborators moved with extraordinary speed to isolate, concentrate, and murder the Jewish population.

In August 1941, a mass massacre at the Pajuostė Forest outside the city resulted in the execution of 7,523 Jews — men, women, and children — in a single operation. This figure represented the overwhelming bulk of the Jewish community. By the time the occupation ended, nearly the entire Jewish population of Ponevezh had been murdered. The vibrant community of centuries was extinguished within weeks of the German arrival.

The mass grave at Pajuostė today stands as a memorial site, and Lithuania has grappled over the decades with how to commemorate the Jewish victims and address the role of Lithuanian collaborators in the killings.

## Significance in Chabad History

While Ponevezh was a fundamentally Litvish (Mitnagdic) city, its significance for the broader Chabad story lies in several dimensions:

First, the general Lithuanian Jewish world from which Chabad emerged was in constant dialogue — sometimes tense, sometimes creative — with the Mitnagdic tradition centered in cities like Ponevezh. The great dispute between Hasidism and Mitnagdim that erupted in the eighteenth century gradually moderated over time, and by the twentieth century, Lithuanian and Hasidic Jews lived in proximity across the region.

Second, Chabad had a presence in interwar Lithuania, including emissaries and chasidim in various Lithuanian cities. The Rayatz (Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn) maintained connections with Lithuanian Jewry throughout his leadership.

Third, the destruction of Lithuanian Jewry — including Ponevezh — forms a central chapter of the catastrophe that Chabad, like all of world Jewry, mourns. The rebuilding of Jewish institutional life in Israel and America in the post-Holocaust decades, which Chabad participated in and helped lead, was in part a response to the obliteration of the great centers of European Jewish learning, of which Ponevezh was one of the most distinguished.

In the contemporary period, Chabad operates in the city of Panevezys, serving the small Jewish community that has reconstituted itself in post-Soviet Lithuania.
