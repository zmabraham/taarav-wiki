# Minneapolis

> The City of Lakes — flour milling capital of the world, home to a resilient Jewish community that built institutions in the face of extraordinary hostility.

![Minneapolis skyline looking south](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/31/Minneapolis_Skyline_looking_south.jpg/800px-Minneapolis_Skyline_looking_south.jpg)
*The Minneapolis skyline, reflecting the city's transformation from frontier mill town to major American metropolis*

## Overview

Minneapolis is the largest city in Minnesota, with a population of 429,954 (2020 census) and a metropolitan area of approximately 3.69 million people — one of the fifteen largest metro areas in the United States. Situated on the western bank of the Mississippi River at Saint Anthony Falls — the river's only natural waterfall — Minneapolis shares its metropolitan area with its twin city of Saint Paul, the state capital, together forming the Twin Cities region.

The Dakota people originally inhabited the land along the Mississippi, but were displaced through a series of treaties between 1805 and 1858 and violently removed following the Dakota War of 1862. European American settlement accelerated rapidly thereafter. The name Minneapolis combines the Dakota word *mni* (water) with the Greek *polis* (city), coined in the 1850s to reflect the city's position among lakes and rivers.

Minneapolis grew explosively in the second half of the nineteenth century on two primary industries: flour milling and lumber. By 1884, Minneapolis had the highest value of flour milling of any city in the world, processing the wheat of the vast northern plains through the enormous mills at Saint Anthony Falls. The city gave birth to companies that shaped American food culture — Pillsbury and General Mills both trace their origins to Minneapolis milling. By 1899 Minneapolis also dominated global lumber markets, stripping the great northern Minnesota pine forests.

The twentieth century brought economic diversification. Target Corporation, Cargill (the world's largest privately held company by revenue), Honeywell, and numerous other major corporations established Minneapolis as a major American business center. The University of Minnesota, founded in 1851, grew into one of the largest public universities in the United States, and its medical, scientific, and engineering research programs have made Minneapolis a center of biomedical innovation.

## Jewish Community History

Jews arrived in Minneapolis alongside the city's general growth, beginning in the 1860s and 1870s. The earliest Jewish settlers were primarily German Jews of the Reform tradition — merchants, professionals, and entrepreneurs who arrived with the broader wave of German immigration to the American Midwest. They established Minneapolis's first synagogues and communal institutions in the late nineteenth century.

Beginning in the 1880s and accelerating through the early 1900s, the Jewish community was transformed by mass immigration from Eastern Europe — the great wave of Ashkenazic Jews fleeing poverty and persecution in the Russian Empire, Romania, and Galicia. These immigrants, primarily Yiddish-speaking and Orthodox in background, settled in their own neighborhoods on Minneapolis's North Side, creating a dense, vibrant Jewish cultural world of synagogues, Yiddish theater, labor unions, Zionist organizations, mutual aid societies, and political movements. The North Side became the heart of Minneapolis Jewish life for generations.

The community produced major Jewish communal and cultural institutions. Minneapolis's Jewish community was large enough to support multiple daily Yiddish newspapers, an active Jewish labor movement, and all the major ideological factions of American Jewish life — Reform, Conservative, and Orthodox synagogues; Zionist and non-Zionist political organizations; socialist-oriented Bundists and religious conservatives.

However, Minneapolis also earned a deeply troubling distinction. Journalist Carey McWilliams, writing in 1946, characterized Minneapolis as "the antisemitic capital of the United States" — the most antisemitic major city in America during the period from the end of World War I in 1918 through 1950. The charge was not hyperbolic. Jews faced systematic exclusion from major industries, law firms, medical practices, and social clubs. Restrictive covenants barred Jews from purchasing homes in desirable neighborhoods. The Silver Shirts, a domestic fascist organization modeled on Nazi movements, held regular meetings in Minneapolis beginning in 1936. Antisemitic violence was not uncommon.

The turning point came through the work of Hubert H. Humphrey, elected mayor of Minneapolis in 1945. Humphrey made combating antisemitism and racial discrimination central to his administration, helping establish the country's first municipal fair employment practices commission and creating a human-relations council. His work in Minneapolis became a national model and launched his career — he went on to serve as U.S. Senator, Vice President, and 1968 Democratic presidential nominee.

The post-war decades saw Minneapolis's Jewish community thrive. Jews integrated into the broader professional and civic life of the city, and the overt antisemitism of the earlier era largely retreated. The community built new suburban synagogues, Jewish day schools, Jewish Federation infrastructure, and a broad network of social service organizations. St. Louis Park, a western suburb, became a major center of Jewish Minneapolis life, home to multiple synagogues and a significant Jewish population that produced, among others, the Coen Brothers filmmakers.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad established its presence in Minneapolis as part of the movement's systematic expansion across the United States following the Lubavitcher Rebbe's arrival in America in 1940. The Twin Cities region today hosts multiple Chabad houses serving diverse Jewish communities — university students at the University of Minnesota, suburban families, young professionals, and the traditional community.

The University of Minnesota campus, with its large Jewish student population, has been a particular focus of Chabad outreach. Chabad at the University of Minnesota operates a student center providing Shabbat hospitality, Jewish programming, and pastoral support for students far from home. This campus-focused work follows the Rebbe's strong emphasis on reaching Jewish youth on university campuses across America.

The Minneapolis Chabad community participates in the broader Minnesota and Upper Midwest Chabad network, coordinating holiday programming, Jewish education, and community-building activities across the region.
