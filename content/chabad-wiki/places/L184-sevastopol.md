# Sevastopol

> The "Venerable City" — fortress of the Black Sea Fleet, twice besieged, never surrendered in spirit.

*Panoramic view of Sevastopol and its celebrated natural harbor*

## Overview

Sevastopol (Севастополь) is a major port city at the southwestern tip of the Crimean Peninsula, commanding one of the finest natural harbors on the Black Sea. Its name derives from the Greek: *sebastós* (venerable, august) combined with *pólis* (city) — a name coined at the time of its founding to honor Empress Catherine II of Russia. With a population of approximately 547,000, it stands as Crimea's largest city and has served since its founding as the primary base of Russia's Black Sea Fleet.

The city was founded in June 1783 when Catherine II formally annexed Crimea from the Ottoman Empire. Scottish Rear Admiral Thomas MacKenzie, serving in Russian naval command, oversaw the initial establishment of a naval station at the site. The natural harbor — a drowned river valley providing deep anchorage protected from the open sea — made the location ideal for naval operations, and Sevastopol rapidly developed as both a military fortress and a civilian settlement.

Ancient history underlies the modern city: the ruins of Chersonesos (Херсонес), a major Greek colony established in the fifth century BCE, lie within the city's modern boundaries. This site, where Prince Vladimir of Kyiv reportedly accepted Christian baptism in 988 CE — an event that initiated the Christianization of Kievan Rus — gives Sevastopol extraordinary religious and historical significance for Orthodox Slavic civilization.

Since 2014, Sevastopol has been under Russian administration following the annexation of Crimea, though the international community continues to regard the territory as part of Ukraine.

## The Sieges of Sevastopol

Sevastopol's identity is inseparable from the two great sieges it endured, which made it one of the most celebrated military sites in modern history.

**The Crimean War Siege (1854–1855):** When Britain, France, Sardinia, and the Ottoman Empire declared war on Russia to contest control of the Black Sea and the Bosphorus straits, Allied forces landed in Crimea and advanced on Sevastopol. The city's garrison, reinforced by sailors from the scuttled Russian fleet whose guns were repositioned as shore batteries, held out for eleven months against Allied bombardment and assault. The defense became a symbol of Russian patriotism and military valor; figures including Leo Tolstoy, who served there and wrote his *Sevastopol Sketches*, and the naval engineer Eduard Totleben, who designed the earthwork defenses, achieved national fame. The city finally fell in September 1855 after the French captured the key Malakhov bastion.

**The Second World War Siege (1941–1942):** German forces besieged Sevastopol for 250 days, beginning in the autumn of 1941. The city's garrison — supplemented by Black Sea Fleet sailors, civilian militia, and Soviet regulars — resisted intensive aerial and artillery bombardment in one of the war's most prolonged urban defenses. The Germans brought the massive "Dora" gun, the largest artillery piece ever deployed in combat, to assist in reducing Sevastopol's fortifications. The city finally fell in July 1942 after running out of ammunition and reinforcements. Soviet forces liberated it on May 9, 1944 — a date of additional symbolism as the day that became Victory Day — and Sevastopol received the designation "Hero City" (Gorod-Geroy) in recognition of its defenders.

## Jewish Community History

The Jewish presence in Crimea predates the Russian conquest by centuries. Crimea was home to several distinct Jewish communities: the mainstream Ashkenazic and Sephardic Jews; the Krymchaks (Romaniote Jews who had lived in Crimea since antiquity, speaking a Judeo-Crimean Tatar language); and the Karaites (a sect rejecting rabbinic tradition, centered in Chufut-Kale near Bakhchisaray and in Yevpatoriya).

In Sevastopol specifically, the Jewish community grew alongside the city itself from the late eighteenth century. The naval city attracted Jewish merchants, contractors, and craftsmen who serviced the fleet and the growing civilian population. Jewish tradespeople were involved in supplying the naval station, and Jewish merchants operated in the city's commercial district.

During the tsarist period, Crimea had a complex relationship with Pale of Settlement regulations. While formally part of the Pale (Jews were permitted to reside in Crimea), Sevastopol's military character — it was a restricted city for much of the tsarist era — created additional complications for Jewish settlement. Nevertheless, a functioning Jewish community existed, maintaining synagogues and communal institutions.

The Holocaust devastated Crimea's Jewish communities with particular speed and totality. German forces occupied Sevastopol following the 250-day siege in July 1942. The Einsatzgruppe D, which had been operating across Crimea since the German takeover of the peninsula's inland towns in 1941, murdered the Jewish communities of Crimea with extraordinary efficiency. The Krymchaks, who did not resemble the stereotypical Jewish appearance that some German commanders had used as an excuse to spare Karaites, were nearly entirely exterminated. Of Crimea's estimated 60,000 Jews at the time of the German invasion, the overwhelming majority were murdered.

The post-war and post-Soviet Jewish community of Sevastopol has been small but persistent. A synagogue and Chabad house have operated in the city, serving the remaining and returning Jewish community. The political status of Sevastopol after 2014 has complicated the relationship between the local Jewish community and Ukrainian-based Jewish organizations.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad's presence in Crimea draws on both the region's ancient Jewish roots and the movement's post-Soviet expansion. During the Soviet period, Chabad activists operated underground networks across the peninsula. In the post-Soviet era, Chabad established rabbinates and community centers in Simferopol (Crimea's capital) and in Sevastopol.

The Sevastopol Chabad house operates as a center of Jewish life for the city's Jewish community, providing Shabbat services, holiday celebrations, Jewish education, and charitable programs. The movement's shluchim in Crimea operate in the complex political environment created by the 2014 annexation, maintaining connections with both the Russian-based Federation of Jewish Communities and the broader global Chabad network.
