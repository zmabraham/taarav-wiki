# Springfield, Massachusetts

> The "City of Firsts" on the Connecticut River — the original Springfield of the New World, birthplace of basketball and the American dictionary, with a proud and deep Jewish communal history

![Springfield Massachusetts skyline](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/Springfield%2C_MA.jpg/250px-Springfield%2C_MA.jpg)
*Springfield, Massachusetts, the Pioneer Valley city on the Connecticut River, home since colonial times and a center of Jewish life in western New England*

## Overview

Springfield, Massachusetts is the third most populous city in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts, with a 2020 population of approximately 155,929. It is situated on the eastern bank of the Connecticut River in Hampden County, in the region known as the Pioneer Valley. Springfield holds the distinction of being the first city named Springfield in the New World: it was founded in 1636 by William Pynchon, an English Puritan merchant, who originally called the settlement Agawam Plantation — borrowing the name of the Agawam band of Native Americans who lived in the area — before renaming it Springfield in 1641 after his hometown of Springfield in Essex, England.

The city lies at the confluence of the Connecticut River and three of its tributaries, in a geographic position that made it a natural crossroads for commerce and settlement in colonial and early American New England. Springfield served as an important military and manufacturing center from its earliest years: George Washington designated it as the site of the Springfield Armory in 1794, and the Armory became one of the most important centers of American weapons manufacturing through the Civil War and beyond. The Springfield rifle — a series of standard military rifles produced at the Armory — became synonymous with American military might. The Armory is now a National Historic Site.

Springfield has earned the nickname "The City of Firsts" for its remarkable concentration of American innovations. These include: the first American dictionary, produced by Noah Webster in Springfield in 1806 (the precursor to what became Merriam-Webster); the first successful gasoline-powered automobile in the United States, produced by the Duryea brothers in Springfield in 1893; and the invention of basketball by James Naismith at the International YMCA Training School (now Springfield College) in December 1891. The Naismith Memorial Basketball Hall of Fame is located in Springfield, celebrating the sport that was invented on its streets.

The city occupies 33 square miles at approximately 42 degrees north latitude and sits at the junction of Interstate highways that connect it to Boston (90 miles east), Hartford and New York to the south, Albany to the west, and Vermont to the north. This geographic centrality within New England made Springfield an important industrial and commercial center through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, with a diverse manufacturing base that included paper, firearms, machinery, and later motorcycles (the Indian Motorcycle Company was founded in Springfield in 1901).

## Jewish Community History

Jewish life in Springfield has a history stretching back to at least the mid-nineteenth century, when the city's growth as an industrial and commercial center attracted Jewish merchants and craftsmen from Germany, and later from Eastern Europe. The German-Jewish immigrants of the 1840s and 1850s tended to integrate into the commercial middle class, establishing businesses and participating in civic life. The larger wave of Eastern European Jewish immigration — primarily from Russia, Poland, the Austro-Hungarian Empire, and Romania — that arrived between the 1880s and the early 1920s created the foundations of the recognizably ethnic, Yiddish-speaking Jewish community that would define Springfield's Jewish life through much of the twentieth century.

By the early twentieth century, Springfield's Jewish community had established synagogues representing the full spectrum of European Jewish religious practice. Orthodox congregations — including Congregation Kesser Israel, one of the city's oldest — served immigrants who maintained traditional observance. Conservative and Reform congregations developed as American-born generations sought a modified religious expression. Landsmanshaftn (mutual aid societies organized by town of origin), Zionist organizations, labor unions with significant Jewish membership, Yiddish cultural societies, and Jewish fraternal orders like B'nai B'rith all contributed to a rich communal infrastructure.

The Jewish neighborhoods of Springfield concentrated in the North End and later the Forest Park area, which developed as a residential destination for upwardly mobile Jewish families in the mid-twentieth century. Jewish-owned businesses — department stores, retail shops, professional offices — were a prominent feature of downtown Springfield's commercial life.

Like many northeastern American cities, Springfield experienced significant Jewish suburbanization from the 1950s onward, as families moved to neighboring communities such as Longmeadow, West Springfield, Wilbraham, and other Pioneer Valley suburbs. The Jewish Federation of Western Massachusetts has served as the umbrella organization coordinating Jewish philanthropic and communal activity across the region. The federation has remained a vital institution despite demographic shifts, coordinating local services, Israel advocacy, Holocaust remembrance, and cultural programming.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad-Lubavitch established a presence in Springfield and the Pioneer Valley as part of its expansion across New England and eventually across every major Jewish community in the United States. **Chabad of Springfield** serves the Jewish community of western Massachusetts, offering Shabbat programming, holiday events, Jewish educational classes, and pastoral support to affiliated and unaffiliated Jews alike.

The Pioneer Valley's Jewish community — distributed across Springfield, Northampton, Amherst, Holyoke, and the Five College area (which encompasses the University of Massachusetts Amherst, Amherst College, Smith College, Mount Holyoke College, and Hampshire College) — presents both opportunities and challenges for Jewish outreach. The large student population at the Five College consortium, many of them Jewish, represents a natural constituency for Chabad campus outreach. Chabad houses and programming for college students in the Pioneer Valley extend Chabad's reach into one of New England's most vibrant academic communities.

The Lubavitcher Rebbe's consistent emphasis on reaching every Jewish person, regardless of background or affiliation, resonated particularly in cities like Springfield where many Jews had drifted from institutional Jewish life as demographics shifted and traditional community structures weakened. Chabad's model of unconditional Jewish hospitality and outreach without membership requirements has found a receptive audience in the diverse Jewish population of western Massachusetts.

Springfield also lies on the route between New York and Boston, making it a natural stopping point for Jewish travelers and a community with ties to both the major East Coast metropolitan Jewish communities. Chabad's international network of welcoming houses makes this geographic in-between position an asset rather than a limitation.
