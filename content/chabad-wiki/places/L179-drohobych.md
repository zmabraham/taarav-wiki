# Drohobych

> An oil-rich Galician city whose once-vibrant Jewish community was nearly annihilated in the Holocaust — birthplace of Bruno Schulz and a crossroads of empires

![Drohobych historic city center](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/Casco_hist%C3%B3rico_de_Drogobych.jpg/250px-Casco_hist%C3%B3rico_de_Drogobych.jpg)
*The historic center of Drohobych, shaped by centuries of Galician history under Polish, Habsburg, and later Soviet and Ukrainian rule*

## Overview

Drohobych (Ukrainian: Дрогобич; Polish: Drohobycz; Yiddish: דראָהאָביטש, *Drahobish*) is a city in Lviv Oblast in western Ukraine, situated in the foothills of the Carpathian Mountains roughly 100 kilometers south of Lviv. Its population today stands at approximately 73,000. First mentioned in the municipal records of Lviv in 1387, Drohobych developed from a modest trading post into a city of regional economic importance, owing largely to the exploitation of its natural resources — particularly salt and, later, petroleum.

The city's strategic location between the eastern Galician plateau and the Carpathian foothills placed it at a cultural and commercial crossroads for centuries. It passed through the hands of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Habsburg Empire (from 1772, following the First Partition of Poland), the short-lived West Ukrainian People's Republic, interwar Poland, Soviet occupation (1939–1941), Nazi German occupation (1941–1944), and finally Soviet and then independent Ukrainian rule. Each era left its mark on the city's architecture, demographics, and collective memory.

The mid-nineteenth century brought an economic revolution to Drohobych. The region around the nearby town of Boryslav was recognized as one of Europe's largest centers of oil extraction, transforming Drohobych into an industrial boomtown. The oil rush drew laborers, engineers, merchants, and investors from across the Habsburg Empire, and the Jewish community — long established in the city — played a central role in commerce, finance, and the liberal professions during this period of rapid modernization. The city also produced one of Ukraine's first doctors of medicine: Yuriy Drohobych (c. 1450–1494), who served as rector of the University of Bologna in 1481–1482 and is considered a Renaissance pioneer of Ukrainian intellectual history.

## Jewish Community History

Jews were present in Drohobych from at least the sixteenth century, and by the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries they formed one of the largest and most culturally active communities in eastern Galicia. On the eve of World War II, approximately 15,000 Jews lived in Drohobych, comprising roughly 40 percent of the city's total population. The community sustained a rich network of institutions: synagogues, batei midrash, Zionist organizations of every political stripe, Bundist and socialist circles, Hebrew and Yiddish schools, libraries, and charitable societies.

The interwar period saw Drohobych's Jewish community engage vigorously with both Zionist idealism and the broader currents of Polish and European Jewish cultural life. The city produced writers, artists, intellectuals, and community leaders of note. Hebrew and Yiddish journalism flourished alongside Polish-language cultural production by Jewish authors. The community's professional class included physicians, lawyers, engineers, and academics who participated fully in the civic life of the Polish republic while also maintaining distinctively Jewish communal structures.

The German invasion of the Soviet-occupied zone of Poland in June 1941 — Operation Barbarossa — brought catastrophe. Beginning almost immediately, German forces and local collaborators carried out mass killings. A ghetto was formally established in Drohobych in October 1942, confining approximately 10,000 Jews in conditions of systematic starvation, forced labor, and terror. Deportations to the Belzec extermination camp proceeded in waves throughout 1942 and into 1943. By June 1943 the ghetto had been liquidated. Of the approximately 15,000 Jews who had lived in Drohobych before the war, only around 800 survived to see liberation. The Drohobych Jewish cemetery and the memory of those murdered remain as testimony to what was lost.

The most internationally famous victim of the Drohobych Holocaust was **Bruno Schulz** (1892–1942), a Polish-Jewish writer, graphic artist, and drawing teacher who spent nearly his entire life in the city. His two collections of short stories — *Cinnamon Shops* (1933) and *Sanatorium Under the Sign of the Hourglass* (1937) — are considered masterpieces of twentieth-century world literature, weaving Drohobych's streets, market squares, and characters into a dreamlike mythological universe. Schulz was shot dead on a Drohobych street on November 19, 1942, by Gestapo officer Karl Gunther in a cynical act of revenge between two Nazi officers competing over their Jewish "proteges." He was carrying bread when he was killed. Schulz's fate became emblematic of the destruction of Galician Jewish civilization. Today an international literary prize bears his name, and commemorative plaques in Drohobych honor his memory.

## Significance in Chabad History

Drohobych, situated at the heart of eastern Galicia, lay within the broad geographic zone where Hasidism spread rapidly in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The region was home to numerous Hasidic dynasties and courts, and Drohobych's Jewish community participated in the vibrant Hasidic life of Galicia. While the city was not itself a seat of a major Chabad dynasty — Chabad's primary centers were further east and north, in White Russia and Ukraine — the movement's influence reached Galician communities through itinerant teachers, printed texts, and the movement of families across the Pale of Settlement.

The city and its region appear in the broader map of Chabad outreach as the movement expanded its reach into Galician territory during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The Lubavitcher Rebbes maintained correspondence with communities throughout Galicia, and Chabad-oriented Jews lived in many Galician towns including those in the Drohobych district.

The annihilation of Drohobych's Jewish community during the Holocaust extinguished whatever Hasidic and Chabad-connected networks had existed in the city and its surroundings. In the post-war and post-Soviet period, Chabad's worldwide outreach mission has led to renewed Jewish presence and programming in the Lviv Oblast region, with Chabad of Lviv serving Jewish travelers, students, and the small remaining Jewish communities throughout western Ukraine.
