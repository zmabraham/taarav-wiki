# Denver

> The Mile High City — where the clean Rocky Mountain air drew tuberculosis patients from the Jewish immigrant world, planting a community that flourished long after the sanatoriums closed.

## Overview

Denver is the capital and most populous city of Colorado, situated at exactly one mile (5,280 feet) above sea level on the South Platte River at the eastern foot of the Rocky Mountains. With a population of 715,522 (2020 census) and a metropolitan area of approximately 3.1 million, Denver is the nineteenth-largest city in the United States and the commercial, cultural, and governmental center of the Mountain West region.

The city was founded in November 1858, when General William Larimer staked a town claim at the confluence of Cherry Creek and the South Platte River during the Pike's Peak Gold Rush. Named "Denver City" to honor Kansas Territorial Governor James W. Denver in the hope of winning political favor (a strategy that reportedly failed, as Denver had already resigned his governorship), the city was incorporated in 1861 and became Colorado's permanent state capital in 1881 following the territory's admission to the Union in 1876.

Denver's physical setting is extraordinary. The Great Plains meet the Rocky Mountains at the city's western edge, and on clear days the skyline of the mountains — including peaks exceeding 14,000 feet — is visible from downtown. The city receives approximately 3,100 hours of sunshine annually, and its semi-arid climate, with generally low humidity and cold but often sunny winters, gave Denver its early reputation as a health resort. An average annual snowfall of 49 to 53 inches punctuates what is otherwise a relatively mild climate for a northern city.

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, Denver evolved from a mining-era boomtown into a diversified economy anchored by energy industries, federal government employment (the region hosts numerous national laboratories and military installations), technology, finance, and tourism. Eight Fortune 500 companies are headquartered in the Denver metropolitan area.

## Jewish Community History

The Jewish history of Denver is one of the most distinctive in American Jewish life, shaped by a unique combination of the Gold Rush, the tuberculosis epidemic, and the extraordinary character of the immigrant generation.

**The Pioneer Generation:** Jews arrived in Denver almost as soon as the city itself was founded. By 1859 — barely a year after the first settlement — approximately a dozen Jewish pioneers, primarily of German and Central European origin, had established themselves in the nascent town. Four of these early settlers — Hyman and Fred Salomon, Leopold Mayer, and Abraham Jacobs — served on the Denver City Council and are believed to have conducted the first religious service of any kind in Denver history, in September 1859. The community formalized quickly: the Hebrew Burial and Prayer Society was established in 1860, B'nai B'rith arrived in 1872, and Colorado's first synagogue, Temple Emanuel, opened its doors in 1874. In 1889, Wolfe Londoner became Denver's first (and to date only) Jewish mayor.

**The Tuberculosis Migration:** The most distinctive chapter of Denver Jewish history unfolded between the 1880s and the 1930s, driven by the tuberculosis epidemic. Tuberculosis — then called consumption — was devastating the crowded tenements of New York's Lower East Side and other immigrant neighborhoods. Doctors widely believed that dry mountain air offered the best hope for recovery, and Denver's combination of altitude, sunshine, and low humidity made it "The World's Sanitarium."

For the Jewish immigrant community, this medical geography had profound consequences. Thousands of Eastern European Jewish immigrants suffering from tuberculosis or accompanying sick family members made their way to Denver, often with only one-way tickets and little money. The Jewish community of Denver responded with remarkable institutional creativity. Two major organizations emerged to serve this population:

- **The National Jewish Hospital for Consumptives (1899):** Founded with support from Reform Jewish philanthropy, NJH operated on a model of rigorous admission criteria and high-quality care. Its motto, *"None may enter who can pay; none can pay who enter,"* expressed its commitment to free care for the indigent. The institution ultimately became National Jewish Health, which exists to this day as a world-leading respiratory and immune disease research and treatment center.

- **The Jewish Consumptives' Relief Society (1904):** Founded by Eastern European Jewish immigrants who felt unwelcome at the more German-Jewish and Reform-oriented NJH, the JCRS offered more accessible care with fewer admission restrictions, and provided a Yiddish-speaking, culturally familiar environment for Ashkenazic patients. The class and cultural tensions between German-Jewish and Eastern European Jewish communities played out directly in the competition between these institutions.

Frances Wisebart Jacobs was the central figure in founding both institutions and became such a revered philanthropist that she appears in the Colorado State Capitol's stained-glass rotunda — one of only sixteen pioneers honored there and the sole woman depicted.

**Golda Meir in Denver:** One of the most historically significant episodes of Denver Jewish history involves the young Golda Mabovitz, who would later become Golda Meir, Prime Minister of Israel. In 1913, at age fifteen, Golda came to Denver's West Side with her sister Sheyna, who had contracted tuberculosis and sought treatment. Golda lived in Denver from 1913 to 1914. During those months, she was immersed in the politically charged world of Denver's Eastern European Jewish immigrants — Zionist meetings, socialist debates, labor organizing discussions — and she met her future husband, Morris Meyerson, in Denver's Jewish circles. In her autobiography, Meir reflected that *"those talk-filled nights in Denver played a considerable role"* in shaping the convictions that would define her political life.

**Antisemitism and the KKK:** Denver's Jewish community also faced severe hostility. In 1905, a Jewish immigrant named Jacob Weisskind was fatally beaten by a mob claiming to "avenge the blood of Christ." Less than two years later, two more Jewish immigrants were murdered by Christian gang members. The 1920s brought the Ku Klux Klan's dramatic rise in Colorado: the Klan achieved extraordinary political power, electing Denver's mayor, the governor of Colorado, and controlling state government at nearly every level. While the primary KKK targets in Colorado were Catholics, the organization's virulent antisemitism created an atmosphere of fear and intimidation for Denver's Jewish community.

**Modern Community:** Despite these challenges, Denver's Jewish community flourished and grew. Today the Denver metropolitan area is home to approximately 85,000 Jews (2007 Jewish Federation estimate), making it one of the ten largest Jewish communities in the United States. The West Side, once the heart of Eastern European Jewish immigrant life, still maintains a traditional Haredi community following Lithuanian Jewish practice. The broader metro area supports 25 active synagogues and multiple Jewish educational institutions.

Notable Denver Jews include U.S. Senator Michael Bennet, former Secretary of State Madeleine Albright (who grew up in Denver), Governor Jared Polis (Colorado's first openly gay governor and first Jewish governor), and Ruth Handler, the creator of the Barbie doll.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad established its presence in Denver as part of the movement's systematic expansion across the United States. Today the Denver metropolitan area hosts multiple Chabad houses and centers, serving diverse segments of the community.

The University of Denver and Colorado State University campuses have been particular focuses of Chabad outreach, consistent with the Lubavitcher Rebbe's emphasis on reaching Jewish university students. The Denver Chabad community also serves the broader metro population, with houses in Denver proper, Boulder, Fort Collins, and surrounding communities.

Denver's large and historically distinctive Jewish community — shaped by the tuberculosis migration, the pioneer experience, and the subsequent growth of a Mountain West Jewish identity — provides a rich context for Chabad's work. The community's mix of longtime Denver families, newcomers drawn by Colorado's quality of life, and students at the region's many universities creates varied pastoral and educational needs that Chabad's shluchim address.
