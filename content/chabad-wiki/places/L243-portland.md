# Portland, Oregon

> The Rose City of the Pacific Northwest — home to the oldest Jewish congregation west of the Rockies and a thriving, progressive Jewish community of over 75,000

![Portland Oregon city view](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fc/Portland%2C_Oregon_Collage.jpg/250px-Portland%2C_Oregon_Collage.jpg)
*Portland, Oregon — the Rose City at the confluence of the Willamette and Columbia Rivers, home to one of the oldest Jewish communities in the American West*

## Overview

Portland is the most populous city in the state of Oregon and the twenty-eighth largest in the United States, with a 2020 population of approximately 652,000. It is situated at the confluence of the Willamette and Columbia Rivers in northwestern Oregon, and serves as the county seat of Multnomah County. The city was established in 1845 and incorporated on February 8, 1851. According to local legend, Portland was named after Portland, Maine, following a coin toss between two founders — Asa Lovejoy of Boston and Francis W. Pettygrove of Portland, Maine — each of whom wanted to name the new settlement after his own hometown.

Before European settlement, the area was inhabited by the Multnomah and Clackamas bands of the Chinook people, whose presence along the rivers provided both sustenance and trade networks across the Pacific Northwest. The landscape was dramatically shaped by the ancient Missoula Floods — catastrophic glacial outburst floods from the ice-age Lake Missoula — which carved the distinctive Columbia River Gorge and deposited the soils of the Willamette Valley.

Portland developed as a commercial hub in the mid-nineteenth century, serving as a supply point for settlers heading to the interior of Oregon, miners, and later as an exporter of agricultural produce from the fertile Willamette Valley. The city's location — with Pacific Ocean access via the Columbia River and proximity to the abundant timber resources of the Pacific Northwest — made it a natural center of trade and industry. The completion of the transcontinental railroad connections to Portland in the 1880s accelerated growth further.

Today Portland is known for its liberal political culture, its commitment to sustainability and urban planning, its famous food scene, and its reputation as a center of independent music, arts, and culture. It is called the "Rose City" — an official designation since 2003 — for its ideal climate for cultivating roses, and "Bridgetown" for the numerous historic spans crossing the Willamette River through downtown. Its Mediterranean-influenced climate features mild, rainy winters and warm, dry summers, with a record-breaking heat event in June 2021, when the city recorded an all-time high of 116 degrees Fahrenheit during a catastrophic heat dome.

## Jewish Community History

Jewish settlement in Portland began remarkably early for the Pacific Northwest. The community traces its institutional origins to 1858, when **Congregation Beth Israel** was founded — while Oregon was still a territory, even before it achieved statehood. Beth Israel built its first synagogue in 1859, making it one of the very first Jewish congregations formally established west of the Rocky Mountains. This early presence reflects the broader pattern of Jewish merchants and entrepreneurs following the Gold Rush and subsequent commercial opportunities into the far western territories of the United States.

**Congregation Beth Israel** today remains a landmark institution of American Jewish life. Its current building, a magnificent Neo-Byzantine structure at 1931 NW Flanders Street, was constructed between 1926 and 1928. Architects Morris H. Whitehouse, Herman Brookman, and Harry A. Herzog designed the building in conscious homage to the Alte Synagoge (Steelerstrasse Synagogue) in Essen, Germany — itself considered the first modern synagogue in Germany before it was destroyed during Kristallnacht in November 1938. The Portland building was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1979 and is considered one of the finest examples of Neo-Byzantine architecture on the West Coast. Beth Israel remains an active Reform congregation.

The Jewish community that developed in Portland through the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries was distinctively western in character: somewhat less anchored to the dense urban kehillot (organized communities) of the East Coast, more dispersed geographically, and notable for a pattern of civic engagement and acculturation that reflected both the opportunities and the social pressures of a frontier and then a western American city. Zionist organizations, Yiddish cultural groups, socialist and labor movements, Orthodox shuls, and Reform congregations all found their Portland expressions.

By the early twenty-first century, the Portland metropolitan area was home to approximately **75,500 Jews**, according to the 2022–2023 Greater Portland Jewish Community Study. This substantial population supports a rich ecosystem of synagogues across denominations, day schools, the Oregon Jewish Museum, and a range of cultural and social service organizations. Portland's Jewish community has been noted for its progressive political engagement, its involvement in environmental and social justice causes, and its creative cultural contributions.

World War II brought tragedy alongside growth: Portland's Jewish community mourned servicemen lost in the war, raised funds for relief, and in the postwar period became involved in resettlement of Holocaust survivors and the nascent State of Israel.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad-Lubavitch established a presence in Oregon as part of its national and global outreach expansion. **Chabad of Oregon** operates multiple centers serving the Portland metropolitan area and the broader state of Oregon, including Chabad houses in Portland itself, on the campuses of Oregon's universities, and in communities across the state.

The Chabad house model — an open, welcoming home offering Shabbat hospitality, Jewish education, holiday celebrations, and individual counseling without denominational barriers — has proven particularly resonant in a city like Portland, where many Jewish residents are unaffiliated with traditional congregations but retain Jewish identity and interest. Portland's culture of independent-mindedness and its relatively young, mobile professional population make the Chabad model of low-barrier, high-warmth outreach especially effective.

Chabad's presence in Portland also reflects the Lubavitcher Rebbe's vision of reaching every Jew wherever they are located — including in the Pacific Northwest cities that might seem remote from the traditional centers of American Jewish life. The Rebbe consistently urged his emissaries to go to the most far-flung corners of the world, and Oregon was certainly included in that mandate. The result has been a growing Chabad infrastructure in the Rose City, providing Jewish services and community for students, travelers, young professionals, and long-term residents alike.
