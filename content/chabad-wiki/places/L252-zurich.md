# Zurich

> Switzerland's largest city and financial capital — a Jewish community that survived centuries of expulsion to become one of Europe's most vibrant

![Zurich Old Town and Lake](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a8/Zurich_-_Grossm%C3%BCnster_from_the_lake.jpg/800px-Zurich_-_Grossm%C3%BCnster_from_the_lake.jpg)
*Zurich's iconic Grossmünster towers over the Limmat River and Lake Zurich — a city whose Jewish community endured centuries of expulsion before emerging as one of Switzerland's most dynamic Jewish centers*

## Overview

Zurich is Switzerland's largest city and the capital of the Canton of Zurich, situated at the northwestern tip of Lake Zurich at an elevation of 408 meters above sea level. The Limmat River flows through the heart of the city on its way from the lake to the Rhine, dividing the Old Town and giving Zurich its characteristic dual-bank character. With a population of approximately 436,000 in the city and 2.1 million in the greater metropolitan region, Zurich punches far above its weight in global financial and cultural influence.

The city has ancient roots — Roman Turicum was established as a customs post along the Limmat, and the first major medieval period saw Zurich achieve the status of imperial immediacy (direct subordination to the Holy Roman Emperor) in 1218. The city became one of the intellectual powerhouses of the Protestant Reformation when Huldrych Zwingli began preaching at the Grossmünster in 1519, making Zurich a center of Reformed Christianity and thus a significant force in shaping European religious history.

In the modern era, Zurich has emerged as one of the world's premier financial centers, home to UBS, Credit Suisse (until its 2023 acquisition by UBS), the Swiss Stock Exchange, and the Zurich Insurance Group. The city consistently ranks at or near the top of global quality-of-life indices, valued for its cleanliness, efficiency, cultural richness, and natural beauty. Zurich's two universities — the University of Zurich and the ETH (Swiss Federal Institute of Technology) — are among Europe's finest research institutions, giving the city an intellectual dimension that complements its financial prominence. Both Zurich Airport and Zurich's main railway station are the largest and busiest in Switzerland.

## Jewish Community History

Jews are first documented in Zurich records in 1273, during the early period of the city's growth as a commercial and political center. This early medieval community was destroyed in 1349, when Zurich's Jews were massacred during the Black Death pogrom — one of countless such atrocities across the German-speaking lands, where Jewish communities were blamed for the plague. A second community formed in the late 14th century but was expelled in 1423, and Jews were banned from settling in Zurich Canton for over four centuries. This extraordinarily long absence — from 1423 until the mid-19th century — is a defining fact of Zurich's Jewish history.

The reinstatement of Jewish settlement rights came through a remarkable external pressure: the United States. American diplomats, representing interests that included significant American Jewish merchants with Swiss business ties, pressed Switzerland to extend settlement rights to Jewish citizens of countries with most-favored-nation status. The pressure was effective. Jews were permitted to settle in the Swiss cantons beginning in the 1850s, and the full legal equality that was absent from the 1848 Swiss constitution finally came with the 1866 referendum that granted settlement rights and the 1874 constitution that strengthened civil protections.

Swiss Jews had in the meantime been confined to two small villages — Endingen and Lengnau in the canton of Aargau — which served as de facto reservations for the entire Jewish population of the country from the early 17th century onward. Swiss Jews were banished from the cantons in the 1620s and restricted to these two villages; this restriction persisted until 1850. The character of Swiss Jewry — Ashkenazic, largely German-speaking, deeply acculturated to German-Swiss bourgeois norms — was shaped by this prolonged isolation.

Once emancipation arrived, Zurich's Jewish community grew rapidly. The Israelitische Cultusgemeinde Zürich (ICZ) was formally established in 1862, and the main synagogue on Löwenstrasse was completed in 1884. This handsome Moorish-style building in the Enge district remains the ICZ's principal place of worship and one of the most beautiful synagogues in Switzerland. By the early 20th century, Zurich hosted a significant and distinguished Jewish community of academics, businesspeople, writers, and professionals who had been drawn by the city's universities, financial sector, and cosmopolitan culture.

Zurich played a notable role during World War II as a neutral haven. Switzerland's neutrality allowed Jewish refugees from across Nazi-controlled Europe to seek entry, though Swiss authorities adopted a shameful restrictive policy toward refugees — it was the Swiss who first proposed stamping German Jewish passports with the letter "J" to facilitate their identification and exclusion. Nonetheless, thousands of Jews did reach Switzerland during the war, and Zurich's Jewish community played an important role in relief and rescue efforts.

After the war, the community was strengthened by survivors and refugees who remained. Today approximately one-third of Switzerland's 20,000 Jews live in Zurich, making it the largest Swiss Jewish community. The ICZ counts approximately 2,500 formal members, with many more Jews living in the city without formal affiliation. The community maintains multiple synagogues, Jewish schools, and cultural organizations. In 2005–2008, the Canton of Zurich formally recognized Jewish communities as legal entities for the first time — a belated acknowledgment of their legitimate civic status.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad-Lubavitch has established an active and growing presence in Zurich. As Switzerland's largest city and financial capital, Zurich attracts Jewish businesspeople, bankers, and investors from around the world, making it a natural center for Chabad's hospitality work. The Zurich Chabad House serves both the local community and the constant flow of Jewish travelers.

Zurich's proximity to Basel — where the First Zionist Congress was held in 1897 — gives the broader Swiss Jewish world a significant place in the history of modern Jewish politics. Though Chabad's relationship to political Zionism was complex (the Rebbe had specific and nuanced views on Zionist ideology while strongly supporting the Land and People of Israel), the concentration of Jewish institutional life in the Zurich-Basel corridor made Switzerland significant in global Jewish affairs throughout the 20th century.

Chabad's Swiss presence, centered in Zurich, has also been notable for its engagement with the broader academic and professional world that the city represents. ETH Zurich and the University of Zurich attract Jewish students and faculty from around the world, and Chabad's campus programming complements the institutional ICZ community.

## Notable Jewish Landmarks

- **Synagoge Zürich Löwenstrasse**: The main ICZ synagogue, completed in 1884; a Moorish Revival building in Zurich-Enge, housing Switzerland's largest formal Jewish congregation. A cantonal heritage landmark.
- **ICZ Library**: Recognized as a Swiss national heritage site in 2009; houses over 50,000 volumes in Yiddish, Hebrew, and German, including rare 16th–18th century theological works. Its collection was significantly enriched after World War II when Hannah Arendt transferred materials rescued from the destroyed Breslau Rabbinical Seminary.
- **Israelitische Religionsgesellschaft Zürich**: A second Orthodox congregation, historically distinct from the ICZ.
- **Liberal Jewish Community**: Zurich also hosts a liberal/progressive Jewish congregation.
- **Jewish cemeteries of Zurich**: The Unterer Friesenberg (established 1866) and Oberer Friesenberg (opened 1952). Notable burials include conductor Otto Klemperer and author Felix Salten (creator of Bambi).
- **Chabad House Zurich**: Serving the community and Jewish travelers in Switzerland's financial hub.

## Key Historical Events

- **1273** — First documented Jewish presence in Zurich
- **1349** — Jewish community massacred during the Black Death pogrom
- **Late 14th century** — Second community forms
- **1423** — Jews expelled from Zurich; ban persists for over four centuries
- **1620s** — Swiss Jews confined to the villages of Endingen and Lengnau, Canton Aargau
- **1848** — Swiss Federal Constitution enacted; Jewish settlement rights NOT included
- **1850** — Jews permitted to settle in Swiss cantons again for the first time since 1423
- **1862** — ICZ (Israelitische Cultusgemeinde Zürich) formally established
- **1866** — Swiss referendum grants Jews full settlement rights
- **1884** — Main ICZ synagogue completed on Löwenstrasse
- **1874** — Revised Federal Constitution strengthens Jewish civil protections
- **1897** — First Zionist Congress held in Basel (approximately 85 km from Zurich); attended by 208 delegates from 17 countries; World Zionist Organization founded; Basel Program adopted
- **1930s–1945** — Switzerland maintains neutrality; Swiss authorities controversially restrict Jewish refugees; Zurich community involved in relief efforts
- **1999** — Ruth Dreifuss becomes the first Jewish president of the Swiss Federal Council
- **2005–2008** — Canton of Zurich formally recognizes Jewish communities as legal entities

## Notable Figures Associated

- **Ruth Dreifuss (b. 1940)** — Swiss politician; became the first Jewish president of Switzerland in 1999
- **Otto Klemperer (1885–1973)** — Renowned German-Jewish conductor; buried in Zurich's Jewish cemetery
- **Felix Salten (1869–1945)** — Austrian Jewish author of Bambi; buried in Zurich
- **Hannah Arendt** — Though not resident in Zurich, she passed through Switzerland and facilitated the transfer of the Breslau Rabbinical Seminary library to the ICZ
- **Albert Einstein** — Though not Jewish in communal terms, Einstein studied and worked at ETH Zurich and the University of Zurich; his presence enriches the city's Jewish intellectual associations
- **Theodor Herzl** — While based in Vienna, Herzl organized the First Zionist Congress in neighboring Basel and frequently engaged with Swiss Jewish circles
