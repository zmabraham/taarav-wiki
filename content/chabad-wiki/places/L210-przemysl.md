# Przemysl

> An ancient fortress city on the San River — one of the oldest cities in southeastern Poland, with a Jewish community reaching back centuries and nearly erased in the Holocaust

![Przemysl Market Square](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Przemysl_Rynek.JPG/800px-Przemysl_Rynek.JPG)
*The Rynek (Market Square) of Przemysl, heart of one of southeastern Poland's most historic cities*

## Overview

Przemysl (Polish: Przemyśl) is one of the oldest cities in Poland, tracing its origins to at least the eighth century when it served as a fortified settlement of the Lendians, a West Slavic tribe. It ranks as the second-oldest city (after Kraków) in southeastern Poland, and its long history reflects the contested character of the borderland region it occupies — a place where Polish, Ukrainian, Ruthenian, Jewish, and Armenian cultures coexisted, competed, and intermingled across many centuries.

The city sits astride the San River, a natural defensive barrier that made Przemysl strategically vital. Mieszko I of Poland annexed the Lendian territory around 970–980, drawing the region into the orbit of the nascent Polish state, though it subsequently changed hands multiple times between Poland and Kievan Rus' before being definitively incorporated into the Polish Crown in the fourteenth century. Under Polish and then Habsburg rule, the city developed as an important administrative and commercial center, attracting a remarkably diverse population including a notable Armenian merchant community that maintained its own cathedral — still standing today.

Following the First Partition of Poland in 1772, Przemysl became part of the Austrian Empire and was incorporated into the newly created Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria. Between 1888 and 1914, the Austro-Hungarian military transformed Przemysl into one of the great fortresses of Europe: 44 individual forts arranged in a 45-kilometer defensive ring made it the third-largest fortress in Europe, after Antwerp and Verdun. This fortress was the site of the famous Siege of Przemysl (1914–1915), one of the longest sieges of World War I, in which an Austro-Hungarian garrison of over 100,000 men was eventually compelled to surrender to the Russian army after months of encirclement. The fortress ruins and earthworks remain visible in the landscape surrounding the city. Today Przemysl has a population of approximately 55,000.

## Jewish Community History

Jewish settlement in Przemysl dates to at least the fifteenth century, with the community growing substantially through the early modern period. By the eighteenth century Jews constituted a significant and economically active portion of the city's population, engaged in commerce, crafts, and money-lending. The 1910 census recorded approximately 16,062 Jewish residents — 29.7% of the city's total population — making Przemysl one of the major Jewish communities of Galicia.

During the Austrian period, Przemysl's Jews enjoyed the legal emancipation granted by the Habsburg reforms of 1867 and developed a vibrant communal infrastructure: multiple synagogues of various rites, a significant yeshiva, Jewish schools, newspapers, and a full complement of Zionist, Bundist, and Orthodox political organizations. The Galician Jewish community of Przemysl was known for its mixture of traditional Hasidic life — with followers of various Galician courts — alongside a substantial modern, educated bourgeoisie that had taken full advantage of Austrian educational and professional opportunities.

The city's position on the San River, which after World War I became the border between reconstituted Poland and the Soviet Union, gave it particular sensitivity. The interwar period saw the community navigate Polish economic nationalism and rising antisemitism while maintaining a rich internal cultural life.

The German occupation began in September 1939, but in an unusual twist, Soviet forces also occupied part of the city — the San River formed the demarcation line between the German and Soviet zones under the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact. The German zone included most of the Jewish population, who immediately faced persecution, forced labor, and confiscation. When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, the entire city fell under German control and the systematic murder began. A ghetto was established in July 1942 and approximately 22,000 Jews were confined within it. By September 1943, almost all of Przemysl's Jews had been sent to the Auschwitz or Belzec extermination camps. Approximately 415 Jews survived the war, many of them saved by Polish rescuers at enormous personal risk — a number Yad Vashem has documented carefully.

In 2022, Ukraine's president conferred the honorary "Rescuer City" title on Przemysl in recognition of the city's massive humanitarian assistance to Ukrainian refugees fleeing the Russian invasion — a historical irony that the city once contested between empires now served as a gateway of refuge.

## Significance in Chabad History

Przemysl, as a significant Galician city with a large and religiously diverse Jewish population, intersected with Chabad history primarily through the movement of people and ideas across the Galician landscape. The city's position near the Austrian-Russian border — and after World War I near the Polish-Soviet border — made it a crossing point for Jews moving between the two great Jewish demographic centers of eastern Europe. Chabad families and emissaries operating between Belorussia and Galicia would have passed through or settled in Przemysl. The Rayatz (Sixth Rebbe) maintained contacts and networks throughout Galician Jewish life, and a city of Przemysl's scale and religious diversity would have been part of that network. The total destruction of the community in the Holocaust ended what had been a centuries-old Jewish presence in one of Galicia's most historically significant cities.
