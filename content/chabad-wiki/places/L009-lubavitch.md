# Lubavitch

> The capital city of Chabad for 102 years (1813-1915)

## Overview

Lubavitch (Лубавічы in Belarusian, לובאוויטש in Yiddish) was a small village in the Mogilev Governorate of White Russia (modern-day Belarus) that served as the headquarters of the Chabad-Lubavitch movement for over a century.

## Name Meaning

The name "Lubavitch" derives from the Russian word "Lyubavichi" (любавичи), meaning "city of love" or "beloved." This name became synonymous with the Chabad Hasidic movement.

## Significance in Chabad History

### Capital of Chabad (1813-1915)

- **1813:** Rabbi Dovber Schneuri (the Mitteler Rebbe) settled in Lubavitch
- **Became the spiritual center** of Chabad Hasidism for 102 years
- **Home to multiple Rebbes:**
  - Mitteler Rebbe (Rabbi Dovber)
  - Tzemach Tzedek (Rabbi Menachem Mendel)
  - Rebbe Maharash (Rabbi Shmuel)
  - Rashab (Rabbi Shalom Dovber)

### Village Life

- **Population:** Approximately 2,000 residents (doubled to 4,000 during market fairs)
- **Economy:** Agricultural region with seasonal yarmarka (market fairs)
- **Jewish Community:** Vibrant center of Torah learning and Chasidic life
- **Yeshiva Tomchei Temimim:** Founded nearby in 1897

### Key Events

- **1875:** Marriage of Rashab and Rebbetzin Shterna Sara
- **1880:** Birth of Yosef Yitzchak (the Rayatz) in Lubavitch
- **1891:** First arrest of young Yosef Yitzchak at age 11
- **1915:** World War I forced evacuation; Chabad leadership relocated to Rostov

### Notable Features

- **The Rebbe's Court:** Central synagogue and study hall
- **Jewish Cemetery:** Final resting place of earlier Rebbes
- **Market Square:** Center of village commerce during fairs
- **Surrounding Villages:** Agricultural communities that supported the yeshiva

## Final Departure (1915)

The outbreak of World War I and the German advance on Russia forced the evacuation of Lubavitch. The Rashab and his family relocated to Rostov-on-Don, marking the end of Lubavitch's 102-year period as Chabad's capital.

## Legacy

Despite its small size, Lubavitch became one of the most famous Jewish place names in history, known worldwide as the namesake of the Chabad-Lubavitch movement. The village exemplified how a small, devoted community could become a beacon of Jewish spiritual leadership.
