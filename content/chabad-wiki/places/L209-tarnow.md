# Tarnow

> The "Pearl of the Renaissance" in Galicia — a city where Jews once comprised nearly half the population, leaving behind a lone bimah standing in ruins as testament to a vanished world

![Tarnow Town Hall](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3b/Tarnow_ratusz_ffolas_03.jpg/800px-Tarnow_ratusz_ffolas_03.jpg)
*Tarnow's Renaissance town hall (ratusz), the architectural centerpiece of the Pearl of Galicia*

## Overview

Tarnow (Polish: Tarnów) is a major city in southern Poland's Lesser Poland Voivodeship, situated in the heart of the historical region of Galicia approximately 80 kilometers east of Kraków. The city received its municipal charter — based on Magdeburg law — on 7 March 1330, when King Władysław I the Elbow-high formally established it as a town. But it was under the patronage of the powerful Tarnowski magnate family during the sixteenth century that the city achieved its greatest cultural flowering, earning the sobriquet "the Pearl of the Renaissance" through magnificent architecture, vibrant trade, and courtly patronage of the arts.

The city's extraordinary Renaissance heritage survives in its old town, anchored by the magnificent Town Hall (ratusz) whose slender tower dominates the market square. Surrounding the square are arcaded townhouses, medieval defensive walls, and a cathedral dating to the fourteenth century. Tarnow became part of Austria following the First Partition of Poland in 1772, entering the Galician period that would define its Jewish community's experience for over a century and a half. Austrian Galicia brought, paradoxically, both Habsburg bureaucratic order and a relatively permissive environment for Jewish commercial and religious life — culminating in full legal emancipation under the Austrian constitutional reforms of 1867.

Tarnow holds a grim distinction in the history of the Holocaust: on 14 June 1940, the first transport of Polish political prisoners departed from the Tarnow rail station to Auschwitz, which was then being newly established as a concentration camp. A commemorative plaque marks the station. The city today has a population of approximately 101,000.

## Jewish Community History

Jews have been recorded in Tarnow from at least the mid-fifteenth century, making the community one of the older in Galicia. Under the Tarnowski lords and their successors, the community occupied a recognized legal position, contributing substantially to commerce, particularly in textiles, leather goods, and long-distance trade. During the Austrian period, the community grew rapidly in both size and cultural ambition.

By the early twentieth century, Tarnow had approximately 25,000 Jewish residents, constituting roughly half of the city's total population — one of the highest proportions of any major Galician city. The community encompassed the full range of Jewish ideological and religious life: Hasidic courts (including followers associated with Chabad and with the Galician courts of Belz, Sanz, and Ropshitz), Orthodox traditionalists, Zionists of various orientations, members of the socialist Bund, Hebrew-language educators, and Yiddish cultural activists. The city maintained multiple synagogues, the most architecturally celebrated of which was the Old Synagogue, whose origins date to the sixteenth century. It was also the center of thriving Jewish publishing, journalism, and communal institutions.

The Nazi occupation began on 8 September 1939, and persecution moved immediately. On 9 September, German units burned down most of the city's synagogues and conscripted Jews for forced labor. A ghetto was formally established in 1942. The mass deportations commenced in June 1942: during the first major Aktion, approximately 3,000 Jews were shot on the streets and in the Jewish cemetery, while a further 7,000 were taken to forests outside the city and murdered there. Additional waves of deportation through 1942 and into 1943 sent the overwhelming majority of victims to the Belzec extermination camp.

By 1943, Tarnow was declared Judenrein. When approximately 700 survivors returned in 1945, many quickly left the city again, unable to reconstitute a community in a place so saturated with loss. What remains of the Old Synagogue today is the bimah — the raised platform from which the Torah was read — standing exposed in an outdoor memorial space. The bimah's survival amid the ruins makes it one of the most haunting Holocaust monuments in Poland: a single element of liturgical furniture outlasting the building and the entire community that gave it purpose.

Some Tarnow Poles risked their lives to shelter Jews despite Nazi penalties that mandated death for any Pole who aided a Jew. Several Polish rescuers were captured and murdered by the Germans. Their names are recorded at Yad Vashem as Righteous Among the Nations.

## Significance in Chabad History

Tarnow's position as a major Galician city made it an important point of contact in the network of Chabad emissaries who moved through Galicia during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Galicia had its own distinct Hasidic geography — dominated by local courts — but Chabad maintained a presence in the region, particularly among families with roots in Belorussia and Lithuania who had resettled in Galician cities. As a regional center with rail connections east toward Lwów and west toward Kraków, Tarnow served as a transit point for Chabad movement throughout Galicia. The Rayatz (Sixth Rebbe) and his emissaries traversed the Galician rail network extensively during the interwar years, and Tarnow — as a significant station on the route between Kraków and the east — would have figured in that geography. The destruction of the Tarnow Jewish community during the Holocaust eliminated one of the significant Jewish population concentrations of southern Poland.
