# Rzeszów

> Capital of the Subcarpathian Voivodeship and once a city where Jews formed over a third of the population — a major Galician transit hub whose Jewish community was almost entirely destroyed in the Holocaust

![Rzeszów panoramic view](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fd/Panorama_Rzeszowa.jpg/120px-Panorama_Rzeszowa.jpg)
*Rzeszów, the thriving capital of southeastern Poland, former home to one of Galicia's largest Jewish communities*

## Overview

Rzeszów (Yiddish: ריישע, *Reyshe*; German: Reichshof) is the capital of the Subcarpathian (Podkarpackie) Voivodeship in southeastern Poland, situated on the Wisłok River. With a population of approximately 198,000, it is southeastern Poland's largest city and ranks as Poland's fifteenth-largest city overall. It received its municipal charter in 1354 from King Casimir III the Great, the Polish monarch renowned for his hospitality toward Jewish settlement and for issuing the Statute of Kalisz (1264) that established Jewish legal protections in Poland.

Rzeszów developed as a significant commercial and administrative center at the intersection of major trade routes connecting the Polish interior with the Hungarian lands, the Baltic with the Adriatic, and the eastern territories with central Europe. Its position made it a natural transit hub: goods, merchants, armies, and refugees all passed through Rzeszów at various points in its history. The city was part of the Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria under the Habsburg Empire from 1772 until 1918, a period during which it developed a substantial urban infrastructure, rail connections, and a more diversified economic base.

After Polish independence in 1918, Rzeszów served as the seat of a county within the Lwów Voivodeship. The interwar period saw the city grow modestly, with its Jewish community playing an active role in commercial and cultural life. The city was occupied by Germany in September 1939 following the invasion of Poland, and it was under German rule that the Jewish population suffered near-total annihilation. After liberation by Soviet forces in August 1944, Rzeszów became the capital of a newly created voivodeship under communist Poland, and in the post-communist period it has emerged as a dynamic regional capital with an expanding technology sector and one of Poland's busiest provincial airports.

## Jewish Community History

Jews had lived in Rzeszów since at least the seventeenth century, and by the twentieth century the community had grown into one of the most substantial Jewish populations in eastern Galicia. In the years before World War II, approximately 14,000 Jews lived in Rzeszów — well over one-third of the city's total population. This proportion placed Rzeszów in the ranks of those Polish cities where Jewish life was deeply embedded in the urban fabric: Jewish merchants dominated many commercial sectors, Jewish craftsmen were ubiquitous in the trades, and Jewish cultural and political organizations — Zionist parties, the Bund, Orthodox Agudah, and liberal associations — competed vigorously for communal loyalty.

The Jewish quarter of Rzeszów centered on the old town, where synagogues, a beth midrash, and communal institutions clustered. The Old Synagogue of Rzeszów, an impressive Renaissance-period structure, reflected both the antiquity and the prosperity of the Jewish presence in the city. Jewish schools educated children in Hebrew, Yiddish, and Polish. Libraries, newspapers, and theatrical troupes in multiple languages served the varied cultural tastes of a community that ranged from the ultra-Orthodox to the secular left.

Following the German occupation in September 1939, the situation of Rzeszów's Jews deteriorated rapidly. German authorities imposed forced labor, confiscated property, and subjected Jews to arbitrary violence and humiliation. In 1942, as the Nazis implemented the Final Solution across occupied Poland, a ghetto was established in Rzeszów. Mass deportations to the Belzec extermination camp followed. By the end of the German occupation, approximately 20,000 Jews — including those brought to Rzeszów from surrounding communities — had been murdered. Survivors numbered only in the hundreds. Of those who returned from Soviet-occupied territories, "almost all of them subsequently left Rzeszów and Poland," according to accounts of the postwar period, as they encountered continued hostility and found their former homes and livelihoods irrecoverably altered.

The memory of Rzeszów's Jewish community is preserved in a Jewish cemetery, remnants of communal buildings, a growing body of memorial literature by survivors and their descendants, and the work of Polish scholars and local activists committed to recovering and transmitting the history of Jewish life in the region.

## Significance in Chabad History

Rzeszów's importance in the context of Chabad history derives from its position as the major transit and administrative hub of southeastern Galicia. As Chabad emissaries, families, and texts moved across eastern Europe in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, Rzeszów served as a natural waypoint along routes connecting the Lubavitch heartland in White Russia and Ukraine with Galician communities and beyond.

The city appears in the narrative of Chabad expansion into Galicia, a region that had its own flourishing Hasidic traditions — including major dynasties such as those of Belz, Ropshitz (Ropczyce), Zanz (Sanz/Nowy Sącz), and others — into which Chabad-Lubavitch gradually extended its outreach. Ropczyce (Ropshitz), the seat of Rabbi Naftali Tzvi Horowitz of Ropshitz, is located near Rzeszów, indicating the density of Hasidic religious culture in the region. Chabad Hasidim maintained communities and connections throughout this landscape.

The Holocaust's destruction of Rzeszów's Jewish community severed these networks. In the post-war period, as Chabad rebuilt its outreach infrastructure across Europe, southeastern Poland — including the Rzeszów area — became a focus of memorial visits, Holocaust remembrance programs, and the renewal of Jewish life for the small numbers of Jews who remain or have returned to the region.
