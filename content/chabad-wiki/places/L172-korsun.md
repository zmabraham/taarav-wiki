# Korsun-Shevchenkivskyi

> An ancient Ukrainian city on the Ros River — fortress of Yaroslav the Wise, site of Cossack uprising, and theater of one of WWII's most dramatic encirclement battles

![Korsun-Shevchenkivskyi city view](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/71/Korsuno-002.jpg/250px-Korsuno-002.jpg)
*Korsun-Shevchenkivskyi, a city whose layered history spans Kievan Rus fortifications, Cossack rebellions, and the crucible of World War II*

## Overview

Korsun-Shevchenkivskyi (Ukrainian: Корсунь-Шевченківський) is a city in Cherkasy Oblast in central Ukraine, located on the Ros River approximately 130 kilometers south of Kyiv. Its current population is approximately 17,000. The city's compound name reflects its dual heritage: the ancient toponym Korsun — derived from Chersonesus, the Greek colonial city on the Crimean coast from which it may have taken its name or its early Varangian-Christian cultural associations — joined to the surname of Taras Shevchenko, Ukraine's national poet, in whose honor the city was renamed following World War II.

The site's strategic importance is ancient. In 1032, the Kievan Rus prince Yaroslav the Wise ordered a fortress built here to protect Kyiv against raids from nomadic peoples of the steppe — the Pechenegs and later the Cumans — who regularly threatened the settled populations of the Dnieper basin. This defensive function placed Korsun in the frontline of the great clash between the sedentary civilization of Kievan Rus and the nomadic world of the Eurasian steppe, a tension that defined Ukrainian history for centuries.

The city lies in a landscape of gentle hills, river valleys, and the broad agricultural plains of central Ukraine known as the forest-steppe zone. Its location on the Ros River, a right-bank tributary of the Dnieper, gave it both defensive advantages — the river served as a natural moat — and commercial ones, as waterways were the highways of pre-modern Ukraine.

## Jewish Community History

Like most towns and cities in the Right-Bank Ukraine (the territories west of the Dnieper that were under Polish-Lithuanian and later Russian rule), Korsun hosted a Jewish community from the early modern period. The region entered Russian Imperial control in 1793 with the Second Partition of Poland, after which Korsun's Jews became subject to the restrictions of the Pale of Settlement — the zone within which Jewish residence was legally permitted in the Russian Empire.

Jewish communities in the Cherkasy region typically engaged in trade, artisanship, and the various middleman economic roles characteristic of the Pale. Jewish innkeepers, millers, merchants, and craftsmen were woven into the economic fabric of towns like Korsun. The community would have maintained its own synagogue, ritual bath (mikveh), burial society (chevra kadisha), and educational institutions (cheder and perhaps a yeshiva).

The catastrophic Khmelnytsky Uprising of 1648–1657 — during which Cossack forces under Bohdan Khmelnytsky slaughtered enormous numbers of Jews throughout Ukraine in one of the worst pre-Holocaust massacres in Jewish history — devastated communities throughout the region, including the Korsun area. The Battle of Korsun in May 1648 was one of Khmelnytsky's first great military victories, with Polish forces routed near the city. The Jewish communities of the region bore terrible losses in the violence that accompanied and followed this battle.

The Holocaust reached Korsun in 1941 following the German advance into Soviet-occupied Ukraine. Jewish communities throughout the Cherkasy region were targeted by SS Einsatzgruppen mobile killing units and local collaborators. The Jewish population of Korsun and the surrounding district was murdered during the German occupation, which lasted until the Red Army's liberation of the area in late 1943 and early 1944.

## The Battle of Korsun-Cherkassy Pocket (1944)

The name Korsun is inseparable in military history from one of the most dramatic battles of World War II's Eastern Front. Between January 24 and February 16, 1944, Soviet forces of the 1st and 2nd Ukrainian Fronts — approximately 336,700 troops — encircled a substantial German force of Army Group South near Korsun and Cherkassy. This operation, part of the broader Soviet Dnieper-Carpathian Offensive, aimed to replicate the success of Stalingrad by trapping and annihilating German forces in a *kessel* (cauldron).

The trapped German contingent numbered between 60,000 and 100,000 men. Soviet supreme commander Georgy Zhukov reportedly assured Stalin: "There is no need to worry, Comrade Stalin. The encircled enemy will not escape." Despite these assurances, the Germans mounted a desperate breakout in February 1944. The attempted rescue by Field Marshal Erich von Manstein's armored forces from outside the pocket, combined with the breakout from within, allowed a significant portion of the trapped forces to escape, though at enormous cost. Harrowing scenes unfolded at the Gniloy Tikich River, where soldiers and horses drowned in the icy waters during the chaotic flight.

Soviet casualties numbered approximately 80,000 (with nearly 25,000 killed or missing); German losses remain disputed but were severe in equipment and personnel. The battle demonstrated the growing sophistication of Soviet operational art while also revealing the tenacity of German defensive capability even in desperate circumstances. The city was liberated by the Red Army on February 14, 1944, and subsequently renamed Korsun-Shevchenkivskyi in honor of Taras Shevchenko, who was born in the Cherkasy region and whose poetry became a touchstone of Ukrainian national identity.

## Significance in Chabad History

Korsun lies within the historic heartland of Ukrainian Hasidism. The Ros River valley and the Cherkasy region were part of the broad landscape where the Baal Shem Tov (Rabbi Israel ben Eliezer, c. 1698–1760) and his immediate disciples established the Hasidic movement in the eighteenth century. The surrounding region of Podolia and central Ukraine was saturated with Hasidic courts, teachers, and communities. Chabad, as one of the most organized and intellectually articulated streams of Hasidism, had adherents and networks throughout central Ukraine.

The Cherkasy region in particular, including Korsun, appears in the broader geography of Chabad activity as the movement expanded from its White Russian heartland southward and eastward into Ukraine during the nineteenth century. Chabad Hasidim operated in many of the towns and cities of the region, and the Lubavitcher Rebbes maintained awareness of and correspondence with these communities.

The Soviet period suppressed all Jewish and religious life in Korsun as everywhere else in the USSR. The Holocaust's destruction of the Jewish community meant that by the postwar period, organized Jewish life in Korsun had effectively ceased. In the post-Soviet era, Chabad emissaries working across Ukraine have extended outreach to the Cherkasy Oblast region, working with whatever Jewish population remains or visits the area.
