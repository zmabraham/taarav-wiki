# Radom

> A royal Polish city that was home to one of the largest Jewish communities in central Poland — nearly annihilated in the Holocaust

![Radom city montage](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7e/Radom_montage1.png/600px-Radom_montage1.png)
*Radom, a major city in Masovian Voivodeship, Poland — landmarks, churches, and streets*

## Overview

Radom is a historic Polish city located approximately 100 kilometers south of Warsaw in the Masovian Voivodeship. Its documented history begins as early as 1155, when Pope Adrian IV referenced "a village near Radom, called Sławno." The city rose to genuine prominence during the medieval and early modern periods when it served as a royal residence and administrative center strategically situated between the regions of Lesser Poland and Mazovia.

In 1350, King Casimir III the Great established Radom's "New Town," fortifying it with defensive walls and gates and laying out its characteristic grid street pattern. The city achieved lasting constitutional significance in 1505, when the Nihil Novi Act — one of the earliest formulations of parliamentary privilege in European history — was adopted by the Polish Sejm meeting at Radom Castle. This act established that no new laws could be passed without the consent of the Polish nobility, making Radom a landmark site in the history of European constitutionalism.

By the late nineteenth century Radom had grown into a substantial industrial center, with tanning, metalworking, and arms manufacturing. It was also a city of significant political turbulence: the 1905 Revolution produced intense labor unrest here, and the city's Jewish community was deeply embedded in the various socialist and nationalist currents of the era. Today, with a population of approximately 207,000, Radom remains the second-largest city in the Masovian Voivodeship and the fifteenth-largest in Poland.

## Jewish Community History

Jewish settlement in Radom dates to the medieval period, though it expanded dramatically in the early modern centuries as Poland's legal framework became more hospitable to Jewish commerce and communal life. By the 1897 Russian Imperial Census, Jews numbered approximately 11,200 — nearly 39% of the city's total population of 28,700 — making Radom one of the most heavily Jewish cities in central Poland. The community maintained a rich religious and cultural life, with multiple synagogues, yeshivot, charitable societies, and Zionist organizations. Jewish merchants and artisans were central to the city's economic fabric, particularly in the textile and leather trades.

The Great Synagogue of Radom, built in the nineteenth century, served as the spiritual center of the community. Alongside it stood numerous smaller shtieblakh (prayer houses) associated with different Hasidic courts and ideological movements. The interwar period saw the community divided among Zionists, Bundists, Agudath Israel traditionalists, and various Hasidic affiliations — a rich tapestry of Jewish political and religious life that would be entirely extinguished within a few years.

When the Germans occupied Radom in September 1939, they immediately began targeting the Jewish population with forced labor, property confiscation, and systematic humiliation. In March 1941, the Nazi authorities established the Radom Ghetto, dividing the Jewish population between a large ghetto in the city center and a smaller one in the Glinice suburb. By the time the ghetto gates were sealed on 7 April 1941, approximately 33,000 Jews were confined within them — some 27,000 in the main ghetto and roughly 5,000 in Glinice. German-allotted rations amounted to as little as 100 grams of bread per day, though conditions were reportedly somewhat less catastrophic than in several other contemporary ghettos.

The systematic murder began in earnest in August 1942. Between August 1942 and June 1944, approximately 30,000–32,000 of Radom's Jews were deported, the vast majority to the Treblinka extermination camp, with others sent to Auschwitz. Only a few hundred Radom Jews survived the war. At massacre sites in the Firlej district and at Kosów, the Germans murdered approximately 15,000 and 1,500 people respectively. The city was declared Judenrein — empty of Jews — by 1944.

A rare note of courage amid the catastrophe: Dr. Jerzy Borysowicz, director of the local mental hospital, secretly provided medical care to ghetto residents, including the future Warsaw Ghetto Uprising leader Mordechai Anielewicz. Borysowicz was posthumously recognized as Righteous Among the Nations in 1984.

## Significance in Chabad History

Radom served as one of the many Polish urban centers through which Chabad Hasidic emissaries and Lubavitcher families moved during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. As a large city in the western reaches of the territory where Chabad operated, it functioned as both a way-station and a destination for Chabad families navigating the pressures of Tsarist Russia, World War I displacement, and interwar migration. The city's sizable and religiously diverse Jewish community made it a site of ongoing ideological competition between Chabad, other Hasidic courts, and the Haskalah (Enlightenment) movement. The Rayatz (Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, the Sixth Rebbe) passed through and had connections to central Polish cities including Radom during his extensive travels in the 1920s and 1930s. The destruction of Radom's Jewish community in the Holocaust eliminated what had been a significant node in the broader network of central-Polish Jewry with which Chabad maintained ties.
