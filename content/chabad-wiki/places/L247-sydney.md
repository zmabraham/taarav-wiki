# Sydney

> Australia's largest city and home to the oldest Jewish congregation in the Southern Hemisphere

![Sydney Opera House and Harbour Bridge](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Sydney_Australia._%28i%29.jpg/800px-Sydney_Australia._%28i%29.jpg)
*Sydney Harbour, with the Opera House and Harbour Bridge, defines the skyline of Australia's largest city — a metropolis whose Jewish community dates to the very founding of the colony in 1788*

## Overview

Sydney is Australia's most populous city and the capital of New South Wales, situated on the southeastern coast of Australia along one of the world's most celebrated natural harbors — Port Jackson. With a population of approximately 5.6 million, it is the largest city in the Southern Hemisphere by most measures and one of the great cosmopolitan cities of the Asia-Pacific world. The Sydney metropolitan area sprawls across approximately 12,368 square kilometers, from the Blue Mountains to the west to the Tasman Sea to the east, and from the Hawkesbury River in the north to the Woronora Plateau in the south.

The city was established on January 26, 1788, when the British First Fleet arrived at Port Jackson and Governor Arthur Phillip formally proclaimed the Colony of New South Wales. Sydney's founding as a penal colony gives it an unusual historical character — unlike most great cities, it began not as a trading post, religious settlement, or military installation, but as a place of forced transportation. This origin shaped a culture of pragmatism, egalitarianism (at least among the settler population), and eventually striking openness to immigrant communities from around the world.

Today Sydney is Australia's financial and cultural capital in fact if not in name (that designation belongs to Canberra), home to major banks, the Australian Securities Exchange, world-class universities, and a vibrant arts scene. Its iconic landmarks — the Sydney Opera House (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), the Sydney Harbour Bridge, and Bondi Beach — are among the most recognized images in the world. The city's Jewish community, one of the oldest in Australia, is concentrated primarily in the Eastern Suburbs and the Upper North Shore, and numbers approximately 50,000 — the second largest Jewish community in Australia after Melbourne.

## Jewish Community History

Sydney's Jewish community is older than the city itself, in the sense that Jews were among the convicts transported on the First Fleet that arrived in January 1788. By some estimates, eight to fourteen Jews arrived on those first ships, making them among the very first European settlers in the continent. These men and women were not transported for Jewish offenses but were simply part of the broader population of convicts drawn from Britain's underclass — a remarkable and poignant beginning to Australian Jewish history.

As the colony grew and free settlers began to arrive in significant numbers in the early 19th century, a more organized Jewish community took shape. A burial society was established in 1817, and the York Street Synagogue (predecessor to the Great Synagogue) was consecrated in 1844. The Great Synagogue itself, on Elizabeth Street in the CBD, was completed in 1878 and remains one of the most magnificent Gothic Revival synagogues in the world. It houses the oldest Jewish congregation in Australia and serves as a landmark of both Jewish and Australian architectural heritage.

Jewish immigration to Sydney increased substantially in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, as Eastern European Jews fled pogroms and persecution. A second, transformative wave arrived in the 1930s and 1940s — refugees and survivors fleeing Nazi Germany, Austria, Hungary, and Poland. This cohort, many of them highly educated professionals, transformed the character of Sydney Jewry, bringing Central and Eastern European sophistication, Zionist ideology in its various forms, and a burning commitment to communal memory and Holocaust education.

A further significant wave of Jewish immigration came from South Africa in the latter decades of the 20th century, as apartheid-era tensions and post-apartheid uncertainty drove many South African Jews, a disproportionate number of whom had Lithuanian (Litvak) roots, to seek new homes. Sydney and Melbourne received the bulk of this migration. Today over two-thirds of Sydney's Jewish population traces its origins to South Africa, Hungary, the former Soviet Union, or Israel, reflecting the community's extraordinary diversity of origins.

The community is concentrated primarily in two geographic clusters: the Eastern Suburbs (from Vaucluse through Bondi to Darlinghurst, the traditional heartland of Sydney Jewry) and the Upper North Shore (from Chatswood to St Ives, where many more recent arrivals settled). The suburb of Bondi has long had a particularly strong Jewish character, with multiple synagogues, Jewish schools, and kosher shops.

The Sydney Jewish Museum, opened in 1992 in the Maccabean Hall in Darlinghurst, stands as a landmark institution for Holocaust education and the documentation of Australian Jewish heritage. It is one of the foremost Holocaust museums in the Asia-Pacific region.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad-Lubavitch has established a substantial and growing presence in Sydney. Among the most significant Chabad institutions in Australia is Yeshiva College, a day school and synagogue complex in Bondi that has served as the anchor of Lubavitch life in Sydney for decades. The Chabad Yeshiva in Sydney draws students from across Australia, New Zealand, and the broader Asia-Pacific region.

The Rebbe (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson) recognized Australia as a continent requiring dedicated Chabad attention, and shluchim were sent to Sydney in the early decades of the post-World War II expansion. Chabad Houses have proliferated across Sydney's Jewish neighborhoods and university campuses, offering Shabbat hospitality, Jewish education, and holiday programming. Particularly notable is Chabad's presence in Bondi, where it serves both the local Jewish community and the large number of Israeli travelers, backpackers, and short-term residents who make Bondi their base.

Sydney's Chabad community also benefits from proximity to New Zealand and other Pacific island nations, making it a regional hub for Lubavitch outreach across the southern Pacific. The city's international airport and its role as Australia's primary gateway make it a natural center for Chabad's hospitality networks for Jewish travelers.

## Notable Jewish Landmarks

- **The Great Synagogue, Elizabeth Street**: Completed in 1878 in a magnificent Gothic Revival style, this is the oldest Jewish congregation in Australia and one of the finest synagogue buildings in the Southern Hemisphere. A heritage-listed structure and active congregation.
- **Sydney Jewish Museum, Darlinghurst**: Opened 1992; one of the premier Holocaust education and commemoration institutions in the Asia-Pacific region, documenting the experiences of Holocaust survivors who rebuilt their lives in Australia.
- **Chabad Yeshiva College, Bondi**: The flagship Lubavitch institution in Sydney, combining a day school, synagogue, and Chabad House. A center of Jewish life for the entire eastern suburbs community.
- **Hakoah Club, Bondi**: Described as "Sydney Jewry's main social and cultural meeting point"; a hub of Jewish sports, culture, and community life.
- **Moriah College**: Described as the largest Jewish day school in Australia; a major institution for Jewish education in Sydney.
- **Temple Emanuel, Woollahra**: Established 1938; the main Progressive Jewish congregation in Sydney.
- **Multiple synagogues in Bondi, Bondi Beach, and surrounding suburbs**: The Eastern Suburbs constitute the densest concentration of Jewish religious institutions in Australia.

## Key Historical Events

- **January 1788** — Jews arrive with the First Fleet; Australia's Jewish community begins at the moment of the colony's founding
- **1817** — First Jewish burial society established in Sydney
- **1844** — York Street Synagogue consecrated — Australia's first purpose-built synagogue
- **1878** — Great Synagogue completed on Elizabeth Street; becomes the spiritual heart of Sydney Jewry
- **1930s–1940s** — Major waves of refugees arrive from Nazi-occupied Europe, transforming the community
- **1948** — Australian Jewish community strongly supports Israeli independence; many Sydney Jews serve as volunteers
- **1967** — Six-Day War; surge of Zionist identification in Sydney community
- **1980s–2000s** — Large South African Jewish immigration; community demographics shift significantly
- **1992** — Sydney Jewish Museum opens in Darlinghurst
- **Ongoing** — Chabad Houses established across university campuses and suburbs throughout metropolitan Sydney

## Notable Figures Associated

- **John Monash (1865–1931)** — Though from Melbourne, Monash is the most celebrated Australian Jew; general who led Australian forces at the Western Front in WWI
- **Isaac Isaacs (1855–1948)** — First Australian-born Governor-General of Australia; born in Melbourne but representative of the broader Australian Jewish community's engagement with civic life
- **Moriah College alumni** — Generations of Sydney Jewish leaders in law, medicine, business, and the arts
- **Numerous Holocaust survivor families** — Whose testimony and documentation forms the core of the Sydney Jewish Museum
