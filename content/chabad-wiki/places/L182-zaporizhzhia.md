# Zaporizhzhia

> The legendary capital of the Zaporozhian Cossacks — a Dnieper River city whose very name means "beyond the rapids," where Cossack freedom and Jewish survival existed in uneasy proximity for centuries

## Overview

Zaporizhzhia (Ukrainian: Запоріжжя; formerly Aleksandrovsk) is a major industrial city in southeastern Ukraine situated on the Dnieper River, approximately 80 kilometers southeast of Dnipro. Its name — meaning "beyond the rapids" in Ukrainian — refers to its location downstream from the great Dnieper Rapids that once made navigation through this stretch of the river perilous. Those rapids, which shaped the entire strategic and cultural geography of the region for centuries, were submerged beneath the reservoir created by the Dnieper Hydroelectric Station (DniproHES), built between 1927 and 1932 as one of the flagship projects of Soviet industrialization.

The city's origins as a formal settlement date to 1770, when it was founded as a Russian fortress — then called Aleksandrovsk — to protect Russian imperial territories from Crimean Tatar raids. But the region's historical significance far predates this administrative founding, rooted in the Zaporizhzhian Sich: the famous fortified encampment of the Zaporozhian Cossacks that gave the city and the surrounding region their identity, mythology, and name.

The Zaporizhzhian Sich, established on Khortytsia Island in the Dnieper in 1552 by Prince Dmytro Vyshnevetsky, functioned for over two centuries as a semi-autonomous Cossack republic. At its height around 1650, it sheltered a population of approximately 100,000 people. The Sich operated as a stratocratic democracy — led by a Kish otaman elected by the Sich Rada (council) — and its population was remarkably cosmopolitan, encompassing Ukrainians, Moldavians, Tatars, Poles, Lithuanians, Jews, Russians, and many other peoples. In 1775, Catherine the Great ordered General Peter Tekeli to surround and dismantle the Sich, ending over two centuries of Cossack autonomy. Approximately 5,000 Cossacks fled to the Danube Delta and established a successor community under Ottoman protection. Today, the Zaporizka Sich historical-cultural museum on Khortytsia Island contains a reconstruction of the original Cossack stronghold and gives visitors a sense of military camp life in the Sich era.

The Soviet-era DniproHES dam transformed Zaporizhzhia into a major industrial center, and by the mid-twentieth century the city hosted steel mills, aluminum plants, and the Zaporizhzhia Automobile Building Plant (ZAZ) — maker of the iconic compact cars that became ubiquitous across the Soviet Union. Today, Zaporizhzhia is home to the largest nuclear power plant in Europe, which has been a source of severe international anxiety since Russia's seizure of the facility following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

## Jewish Community History

The Jewish presence in the Zaporizhzhia region reflects the complex and often violent relationship between Ukrainian Cossack culture and Jewish communities across the early modern period. The Zaporizhzhian Cossacks were, at various times, both protectors and persecutors of Jews. The 1648 Khmelnytsky Uprising — launched by Hetman Bohdan Khmelnytsky from the Zaporozhian Sich — produced one of the greatest catastrophes in Jewish history prior to the Holocaust, with Cossack forces massacring tens of thousands of Jews across Ukraine and Poland. Yet Jews were simultaneously documented as members of the Sich community itself, participating in trade and sometimes in military service.

By the 1897 census, Yiddish was the native language of 27.8% of Aleksandrovsk's residents — a remarkable proportion that testifies to a large and established Jewish community in the city. This figure declined sharply under Soviet pressure: to 9.7% by 1926 and to 0.1% by 1989. The Soviet period brought modernization, urbanization, and official atheism — all forces that accelerated assimilation and eroded traditional Jewish life. The Jewish community that had given Aleksandrovsk/Zaporizhzhia a Yiddish-speaking majority of nearly 30% at the century's start was transformed beyond recognition by its close.

During World War II, the German occupation of Zaporizhzhia from October 1941 to October 1943 brought catastrophic violence against the Jewish community. The Einsatzgruppen and local collaborators carried out mass shootings that killed the vast majority of Jews who had not been evacuated eastward ahead of the German advance. The community, already diminished by Soviet assimilation, was effectively destroyed.

The post-Soviet period saw a revival of organized Jewish life in Zaporizhzhia under Chabad leadership, with the establishment of a Chabad-affiliated community center, synagogue, and educational programs serving the remnant Jewish population.

## Significance in Chabad History

Zaporizhzhia's significance in Chabad history operates on two levels: historical and contemporary. Historically, the city lies within the Ukrainian heartland where early Hasidism took root — the Baal Shem Tov's base of Medzhybizh is not far to the northwest, and the movement's first generation spread through exactly this region. Chabad's own foundations were laid further north in Belorussia, but the Ukrainian Jewish communities of the Dnieper basin were deeply implicated in the early Hasidic world. Zaporizhzhia's large Jewish population in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries meant that Chabad emissaries and teachers operated here as part of the broader effort to maintain Jewish life in the face of Haskalah, Zionism, and ultimately Soviet atheism.

In the contemporary period, Zaporizhzhia is home to an active Chabad community that, before 2022, was growing steadily. The Chabad House in Zaporizhzhia served as a center for Jewish education, holiday observance, and community programming. The Russian invasion and ongoing conflict have dramatically disrupted Jewish life in the city.

- **Region/Country:** Zaporizhzhia Oblast, southeastern Ukraine
