# Nemirov

> A Ukrainian town forever marked by the 1648 Khmelnytsky massacre — and later consecrated in Jewish memory as the birthplace and burial place of Nathan of Breslov, the preserver of Rebbe Nachman's teachings

## Overview

Nemirov (Ukrainian: Немирів; Polish: Niemirów; also transliterated Nemyriv) is a historic town in Vinnytsia Oblast in west-central Ukraine, situated in the historical region of Podolia. Though modest in its present scale — with a population of approximately 10,000 — Nemirov carries an outsized weight in the history of eastern European Jewry, having been the site of one of the most catastrophic mass murders of Jews in the pre-modern era and, centuries later, the birthplace of one of the most influential figures in Hasidic history.

The settlement traces its origins to the end of the fourteenth century, with the first written mention of the town under its modern name appearing in 1506. Like much of Podolia, Nemirov was positioned at the intersection of Polish, Ukrainian, and Ottoman spheres of influence, frequently changing hands as these powers contested the region. Its castle and defensive earthworks (the remains of Cossack-era vallations can still be seen) testify to its strategic importance. The town also developed a reputation as a minor spa and resort destination in the nineteenth century, when Polish and Ukrainian nobility patronized its mineral springs and established country estates in the vicinity, including the grand Scherbatova Palace.

## Jewish Community History

Jews had been present in Nemirov for generations before the catastrophe of 1648, functioning as merchants, artisans, tax farmers (arendators), and essential intermediaries in the economic life of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. The arendator system — by which Jewish families leased income-producing properties (mills, taverns, estates) from Polish nobility and managed them in exchange for a share of the revenues — was widespread throughout Podolia and made Jews both economically indispensable and socially exposed.

### The 1648 Massacre

On 10 June 1648, Nemirov became the site of one of the most shocking atrocities of the Khmelnytsky Uprising. Bohdan Khmelnytsky's Cossack rebellion against Polish rule had ignited a general uprising across Ukraine, and Jews — targeted both as agents of the Polish nobility and as a convenient scapegoat — bore catastrophic losses. At Nemirov, the situation was particularly grim: the non-Jewish townspeople opened the gates of the town to the Cossacks and actively betrayed the Jewish community to them. In the massacre that followed, an estimated several thousand Jews were killed.

The event was so shocking that the Council of Four Lands — the supreme governing body of Polish Jewry — formally designated 20 Sivan (the Hebrew date of the massacre) as a day of fasting and mourning for the entire Jewish people. This date was observed as a fast day in Jewish communities for generations, and elegies (kinnot) were composed memorializing the Nemirov martyrs. The massacre at Nemirov became a symbol of the broader catastrophe of 1648–1649, in which historians estimate that between 100,000 and 200,000 Jews perished across Ukraine and Poland.

### Recovery and Later Community

The Jewish community of Nemirov eventually rebuilt after 1648, recovering through the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries to become once again a substantial presence in the town's economic and social life. As Podolia passed from Polish to Ottoman to Russian control across the eighteenth century, Nemirov's Jews navigated each new political dispensation.

The late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries saw the town become part of the explosion of Hasidism that transformed Ukrainian Jewish life. Nemirov's connections to the Breslov Hasidic movement were particularly significant (see below). The community maintained synagogues, study houses, and the full infrastructure of traditional Jewish life through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

During World War II, the German-allied forces and eventually the Wehrmacht brought the community to its end. On 24 November 1941, an Einsatzgruppen unit massacred 2,680 Jews in pits dug at the Polish cemetery — a single day of organized murder that destroyed a community that had rebuilt itself across three centuries since Khmelnytsky.

## Significance in Breslov and Hasidic History

Nemirov occupies a unique position in Hasidic history through its connection to **Rabbi Nathan of Breslov** (Reb Noson; full name Nathan Sternhartz, 1780–1844), who was born in Nemirov on 22 January 1780. Nathan's father, Rabbi Naphtali Hertz Sternhartz, was a Talmudic scholar and businessman in the town.

Nathan encountered Rebbe Nachman of Breslov in 1802 and became his most devoted disciple — the foremost recorder, editor, and publisher of the Rebbe's teachings. When Rebbe Nachman died in 1810, it was Nathan who ensured that his Torah, stories, and sayings were not lost: he published Likutey Moharan (the Rebbe's collected teachings), Sippurey Maasiyot (the famous allegorical tales), and numerous other works. He also organized the Breslov tradition of annual Rosh Hashana pilgrimage to Uman, where Rebbe Nachman is buried — a tradition maintained to this day by tens of thousands of followers annually.

Nathan is buried in the town of Breslov (Bratslav), on the Bug River, in the old Jewish cemetery. However, his connection to Nemirov — as his birthplace — gives the town special significance in Breslov memory. During Nathan's house arrest in 1834, the Russian authorities exiled him back to Nemirov, the city of his birth, adding a poignant biographical connection between the man and the town.

## Significance in Chabad History

While Nemirov is most closely associated with the Breslov Hasidic movement rather than with Chabad, its broader significance in the history of Ukrainian Jewry and early Hasidism makes it relevant to the Chabad story as well. The Baal Shem Tov — the founder of Hasidism from whom both Chabad and Breslov trace their spiritual lineage — operated in exactly this Podolian milieu, and Nemirov was part of the geographic landscape through which the Besht traveled and taught. The traumatic memory of 1648 shaped the entire worldview of Ukrainian Jewish communities, including those communities from which Chabad drew its earliest adherents. The movement of ideas, teachers, and families across Podolia meant that Chabad and Breslov followers were often neighbors in the same towns, their teachings circulating in the same batei midrash (study houses).
