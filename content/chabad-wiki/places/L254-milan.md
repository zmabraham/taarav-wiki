# Milan

> Italy's economic capital and northern gateway for a Jewish community with roots in late antiquity

![Milan Cathedral (Duomo)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Milan_Cathedral_from_Piazza_del_Duomo.jpg/800px-Milan_Cathedral_from_Piazza_del_Duomo.jpg)
*Milan's Duomo cathedral, the third-largest church in the world, presides over Piazza del Duomo at the heart of Italy's most dynamic modern city — a city whose Jewish community reaches back nearly two millennia*

## Overview

Milan is Italy's second-most populous city and, by most measures, its economic and financial capital. Located in the Po Valley of northern Italy, in the Lombardy region, Milan is home to approximately 1.36 million residents in the city proper and over 6.5 million in its metropolitan area — the fourth-largest metropolitan area in the European Union. The city accounts for approximately 20 percent of Italy's entire GDP, a staggering concentration of economic power that reflects Milan's role as the headquarters of Italy's major banks, insurance companies, publishing houses, and fashion conglomerates.

Founded around 590 BCE by Celtic tribes under the name Mediolanum, Milan was conquered by the Romans in 222 BCE and subsequently became one of the most important cities of the Western Roman Empire. For a brief period in the late 4th century it served as the imperial capital. The city's strategic position at the center of the Po plain, controlling the Alpine passes connecting Italy to France, Switzerland, and the Germanies, gave it enduring commercial and military significance throughout the medieval and early modern periods. Under the Sforza family during the Renaissance, Milan became a major cultural center, drawing Leonardo da Vinci among many others.

Milan later fell under Spanish and then Austrian rule before joining unified Italy in 1861, after which it rapidly industrialized to become the engine of Italian modernity. Today it is internationally recognized as a global center of fashion (home to the "Fashion Quadrilateral"), finance, publishing, and design, while its Gothic Duomo, the Brera art district, La Scala opera house, and Leonardo's Last Supper remain among Europe's most celebrated cultural attractions.

## Jewish Community History

Jewish settlement in Milan dates to late antiquity. Archaeological and documentary evidence indicates that Jews migrated to Milan from Alexandria during the late Roman period, establishing a presence in what was then one of the empire's most important cities. Jewish communities in Lombardy are documented throughout the medieval period, though they faced the same cycles of toleration and persecution that characterized Jewish life across Europe, with expulsions and readmissions tied to the changing political fortunes of the region's rulers.

The late 15th and early 16th centuries brought Sephardic refugees to northern Italy following the 1492 expulsion from Spain and the 1497 forced conversion in Portugal. Some of these refugees established themselves in Milan and the surrounding Lombard cities, though Milan's proximity to Spanish imperial control during the same period made conditions uncertain. Jews gradually moved northward from southern Italy through the 16th century, establishing communities in the Po Valley cities.

Milan's modern Jewish community took more definite shape during the 19th century, particularly following the Risorgimento (Italian unification) and the legal emancipation that came with it. The construction of the Central Synagogue of Milan (Sinagoga Centrale) in 1892 on Via Guastalla was a landmark moment — a grand Moorish-style building that announced the community's permanence and respectability in the newly unified Italian state. This synagogue, one of the most beautiful in Italy, continues to serve as the spiritual center of Milan's Jewish life.

By the early 20th century, Milan's Jewish community had grown to several thousand, drawn by the city's economic dynamism and liberal atmosphere. Many Jews played significant roles in Milan's commercial, publishing, and intellectual life. The Corriere della Sera, Italy's most prestigious newspaper (headquartered in Milan), had notable Jewish contributors and editors.

The Holocaust devastated Italian Jewry, and Milan's community was not spared. Following the German occupation of Italy in September 1943, the persecution of Jews intensified dramatically. Jews were arrested in Milan and throughout Lombardy, concentrated, and deported primarily to Auschwitz-Birkenau. Thousands of Italian Jews perished in the Shoah, and Milan's community lost many of its members. Among the most famous victims was Primo Levi, the chemist and writer from Turin, whose experiences in Auschwitz and subsequent literary testimony in If This Is a Man made him one of the defining voices of Holocaust literature — a testament to the cultural richness of northern Italian Jewry.

After liberation in 1945, the community rebuilt. A significant wave of Jewish refugees and Displaced Persons passed through Milan in the postwar years, as the city's international connections made it a transit point for Jews making their way to Israel (particularly during the years 1945–1948, when Italy served as a key staging ground for Aliyah Bet, the clandestine immigration to Palestine). Today Milan's Jewish community numbers approximately 7,000–9,000 — among the largest in Italy, second only to Rome — and maintains a range of institutions including schools, cultural organizations, and a community newspaper.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad-Lubavitch has established a notable presence in Milan. As Italy's economic capital and one of its major transportation hubs, Milan receives constant flows of Jewish businesspeople, tourists, and students, making it a natural base for Chabad's hospitality and outreach work. The city's Chabad House offers Shabbat dinners, holiday programming, and support services for the Jewish community.

Milan is also significant as one of the cities the Rebbe (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson) specifically targeted for shlichus as part of rebuilding post-Holocaust European Jewish life. The Italian Jewish community, with its unique Italian rite and deep historical roots, received particular attention from Chabad emissaries committed to strengthening Jewish identity across the spectrum of observance.

The presence of major Chabad institutions in Milan also reflects the city's role in Italian Jewish communal life — Milan competes with Rome as the center of Italian Jewry, and in some respects surpasses Rome as an economic and social hub. Chabad's Milanese presence participates in this communal dynamism, offering Chassidic teaching and practice alongside the existing Italian Orthodox and other communal structures.

## Notable Jewish Landmarks

- **Sinagoga Centrale di Milano (Central Synagogue)**: Built in 1892 on Via Guastalla in a Moorish-eclectic style; the spiritual heart of Milan's Jewish community. A heritage building of architectural distinction.
- **Sinagoga di Via Eupili**: A smaller historic congregation serving part of the Milan community.
- **Centro Ebraico (Jewish Community Center)**: The administrative and cultural hub of Milan's organized Jewish community.
- **CDEC (Centro di Documentazione Ebraica Contemporanea)**: Founded in Milan in 1955, this is Italy's premier center for documentation and research on contemporary Jewish history, especially the Holocaust. Archives of irreplaceable significance.
- **Jewish Museum of Milan**: Documents the history and culture of Italian Jewry.
- **Chabad House Milan**: Serves Jewish travelers, students, and community members with Shabbat hospitality and Jewish programming.

## Key Historical Events

- **Late antiquity** — Jews settle in Milan, migrating from Alexandria and southern Italy
- **Medieval period** — Jewish communities established in Lombardy; periodic expulsions and readmissions
- **1492–1497** — Sephardic refugees from Spain and Portugal arrive in northern Italy; some settle in Milan
- **1861** — Italian unification; Jews granted full civil rights throughout the Kingdom of Italy
- **1892** — Central Synagogue completed on Via Guastalla; community establishes formal institutional presence
- **Early 20th century** — Community grows to several thousand; Jews active in Milanese commerce, publishing, and intellectual life
- **1938** — Fascist racial laws passed; Jews excluded from public life, professions, and schools
- **September 1943** — German occupation; systematic persecution and deportation begins
- **1943–1945** — Thousands of Lombard Jews deported to Auschwitz-Birkenau; community devastated
- **1945–1948** — Milan serves as key transit point for Jewish survivors emigrating to Palestine (Aliyah Bet)
- **1955** — CDEC founded in Milan for Holocaust documentation and research
- **Post-1945 to present** — Community rebuilds; Chabad establishes presence; community numbers approximately 7,000–9,000

- **Carlo Erba** — 19th-century Milanese industrialist of Jewish heritage; founder of the pharmaceutical firm Carlo Erba
- **Vittorio Dan Segre** — Italian Jewish diplomat and author associated with Milanese intellectual life
