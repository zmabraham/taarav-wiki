# Zamosc

> A perfectly preserved Renaissance "ideal city" that sheltered one of Poland's most cultured Jewish communities — birthplace of Rosa Luxemburg and I. L. Peretz

![Zamosc Synagogue](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/76/Jewish_Synagogue_-_Built_1620_-_Zamosc_-_Poland_%289224544954%29.jpg/800px-Jewish_Synagogue_-_Built_1620_-_Zamosc_-_Poland_%289224544954%29.jpg)
*The Renaissance-Mannerist synagogue of Zamosc, completed 1618, one of Poland's finest surviving historic Jewish structures*

## Overview

Zamosc (Polish: Zamość) is a city of extraordinary architectural distinction in southeastern Poland's Lublin Voivodeship, situated approximately 90 kilometers east of Lublin near the border with Ukraine. Founded in 1580 by Jan Zamoyski — Grand Chancellor and Grand Hetman of the Polish Crown — the city was conceived from the outset as a utopian Renaissance ideal: a perfectly planned urban environment combining commercial vitality, cultural aspiration, and military defensibility. Zamoyski commissioned the Italian architect Bernardo Morando to design the city on what contemporaries described as an "anthropomorphic" layout, where streets and squares functioned as the city's organs and the surrounding bastions served as its defensive limbs.

The Old Town of Zamosc was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1992, recognized as "a unique example of a Renaissance town in Central Europe, intact and unchanged." Its centerpiece is the Great Market Square (Rynek Wielki), a perfect 100-by-100-meter space enclosed by magnificent arcaded tenement houses. The Cathedral of the Resurrection, built between 1587 and 1598, anchors the skyline and preserves original Renaissance decorative schemes alongside later Baroque and Rococo additions. Zamosc was envisioned as a cosmopolitan trading hub, and it succeeded: within a generation of its founding it had attracted merchants, artisans, and scholars from across Poland, the Ottoman Empire, Italy, and the Jewish diaspora.

Today the city has a population of approximately 62,000 and continues to function as a regional administrative and cultural center in southeastern Poland.

## Jewish Community History

Jan Zamoyski permitted Jewish settlement from 1588, just eight years after the city's founding — a relatively early and explicit invitation that reflected his pragmatic commercial instincts. The earliest Jewish settlers included both Ashkenazi merchants from surrounding Polish towns and a small Sephardic community that had made its way northward from the Ottoman world. The Sephardim maintained their own distinct congregation for several decades before economic pressures led them to merge with the larger Ashkenazi community by the 1620s.

The community grew steadily through the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, eventually occupying a substantial share of the city's commercial life. The magnificent synagogue — completed in 1618, just thirty years after the first Jewish settlers arrived — testifies to the community's early prosperity and self-confidence. Built in the Renaissance-Mannerist style that unified the whole city, it features richly decorated barrel vaults, an elaborate aron kodesh (Torah ark), and stonework of exceptional quality. The building was included in the UNESCO World Heritage designation as an inseparable part of the Old Town's architectural unity. During the Holocaust, the Nazis converted it into a carpentry workshop; it was restored beginning in 2005 and again more extensively in 2009–2010 with funding from the European Economic Area, Norway Grants, and the World Monuments Fund. It now serves as a Jewish museum and cultural center.

By 1921, the Jewish population had reached approximately 9,383 residents, constituting nearly half (49.3%) of the city's total population. The interwar community supported newspapers, schools, political parties ranging from Bundists to Zionists, and a flourishing literary culture.

Zamosc was the birthplace of two figures of international significance: **Rosa Luxemburg** (1871–1919), the Marxist theorist and revolutionary who became one of the most consequential political thinkers of the twentieth century, and **Isaac Leib Peretz** (1852–1915), one of the three classical masters of Yiddish literature alongside Sholem Aleichem and Mendele Mocher Sforim. Peretz maintained a strong attachment to Zamosc in his writing, evoking the culture and tensions of the small-town Polish-Jewish world with unsurpassed insight. His stories and poems became foundational texts for secular Yiddish culture worldwide.

When the Germans occupied Zamosc in September 1939, Luftwaffe bombardments in the opening days of the campaign killed more than 250 civilians. A ghetto was established almost immediately. By November 1942 it had been fully liquidated: approximately 12,500 Jews were deported, the vast majority to the Belzec extermination camp. In addition, more than 8,000 people of all backgrounds were murdered at the Zamosc Rotunda — a Gestapo prison and execution site that stands today as a memorial.

## Significance in Chabad History

Zamosc's position at the southeastern edge of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth placed it within the geographic orbit of Chabad's early expansion. The surrounding Lublin region was one of the cradles of Polish Hasidism, and as Chabad emissaries fanned out from Belorussia into Poland during the nineteenth century, the large and commercially active Jewish community of Zamosc was a natural destination. The city's tradition of cultural openness — it had long hosted multiple Jewish ideological currents — meant that Chabad teachings circulated alongside those of other Hasidic movements and of the Haskalah. The Rayatz (Sixth Rebbe) had contacts throughout the southeastern Polish Jewish world, and Zamosc — as a major city in the Lublin-Lwów corridor — figured in the geography of Chabad outreach and travel during the early twentieth century. The total destruction of the Jewish community in the Holocaust severed those ties permanently.
