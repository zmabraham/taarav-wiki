# Rome

> Home to the oldest continuous Jewish community in Europe — more than 2,100 years of unbroken presence

![Great Synagogue of Rome](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/47/Synagogue_Rome.jpg/800px-Synagogue_Rome.jpg)
*The Great Synagogue of Rome (Tempio Maggiore), completed in 1904, rises above the Tiber River near the site of the former Roman Jewish Ghetto with its distinctive square aluminum dome — the only one of its kind in the city*

## Overview

Rome is the capital of Italy and one of the great historical cities of the world, with a continuous urban presence stretching back more than 2,700 years. Located in the Lazio region along the Tiber River, it serves as the seat of the Italian government and the Vatican, and functions as the cultural and religious center of the Catholic world. With a population of approximately 2.8 million in the city proper and over 4 million in the metropolitan area, Rome is Italy's largest city and one of Europe's most visited capitals.

The city's extraordinary layering of history — Etruscan, Republican Roman, Imperial, medieval, Renaissance, Baroque, and modern — makes it unique among European capitals. The Roman Forum, the Colosseum, the Pantheon, the Vatican Museums, and the Sistine Chapel draw tens of millions of visitors each year. Yet beneath the city's headline landmarks lies a quieter and equally ancient story: the unbroken presence of Jewish life in Rome for over two millennia, making it home to the oldest continuous Jewish community in Europe, and among the oldest in the entire world.

The Tiber Island district, adjacent to the historic Trastevere neighborhood, was long the center of Jewish life and remains intimately associated with Roman Jewish history. Today the community numbers approximately 15,000 and is concentrated throughout the city, with a particularly rich cultural footprint in the old Ghetto area near the Theater of Marcellus.

## Jewish Community History

The Jewish community of Rome traces its origins to at least 161 BCE, when Judah Maccabee sent envoys to Rome to seek alliance against the Seleucid Greeks. This diplomatic mission represents the first documented Jewish contact with the city, though a permanent community likely developed shortly thereafter. By the 1st century BCE, Jewish life in Rome was substantial enough that Julius Caesar extended legal protections and privileges to the community, including property rights and exemption from military service — a remarkable accommodation in the ancient world.

The First Jewish-Roman War (66–73 CE) transformed Rome's Jewish population dramatically. The destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple brought tens of thousands of Jewish captives to Rome as slaves, many of whom were later freed and absorbed into the existing community. The Arch of Titus, still standing in the Roman Forum, depicts the spoils of the Temple — the menorah, the show-bread table, and the silver trumpets — being carried in triumph through Rome's streets. Funerary inscriptions from Jewish catacombs of the 2nd through 4th centuries reveal a community deeply committed to Torah observance, with individuals honored as "lovers of the Law" and "teachers of the Law."

During the Middle Ages, Rome's Jews benefited from a unique status as residents of the Papal States — one of the few medieval European domains that never expelled its Jewish population entirely. This was not because of tolerance, but because Church theology held that Jews must be preserved as witnesses to Christian truth, albeit in a degraded state. The medieval community produced significant scholarship: Rabbi Nathan ben Jehiel authored the Arukh in the 11th century, a foundational dictionary of the Talmud that drew on ten languages and remains a scholarly reference to this day.

The late 15th century brought new waves of Sephardic and other Jewish refugees to Rome following the 1492 expulsion from Spain and the 1497 forced conversion in Portugal, enriching the community with Iberian traditions, liturgy, and culture.

The Roman Jewish Ghetto, established in 1555 by Pope Paul IV's bull Cum nimis absurdum, confined Rome's Jews — approximately 2,000 at the time — to a flood-prone area of less than three hectares in Rione Sant'Angelo. Jews were required to wear identifying yellow badges, forbidden from most professions, compelled to attend conversionary sermons on Saturdays, and subjected to humiliating public ceremonies during Carnival. Despite these severe constraints, the community maintained vibrant intellectual and religious life, developed the distinctive Giudeo-Romanesco dialect (a blend of medieval Italian and Hebrew), and produced a remarkably cohesive cultural identity. As the population grew to over 3,500, residents constructed ever-higher buildings, creating the densely vertical character of ghetto architecture.

The Ghetto's walls were torn down in 1870 following Rome's capture by the Kingdom of Italy and unification. Jews were granted full civil rights, and within a generation they began making major contributions to Italian art, science, academia, politics, and business. The early 20th century brought further flourishing, symbolized by the completion of the Great Synagogue in 1904.

The Nazi occupation beginning in September 1943 brought catastrophe. On October 16, 1943, SS troops raided the Ghetto and surrounding neighborhoods, rounding up more than 1,000 Jews — the vast majority of whom were gassed at Auschwitz within days. Over the nine months of occupation, approximately 1,800 Roman Jews were deported; very few survived. Yet over 10,000 others survived through remarkable networks of rescuers — Catholic institutions, private individuals, and others who hid Jewish families at great personal risk.

## Significance in Chabad History

Rome's significance in Chabad history lies primarily in its role as a gateway city — both historically and in the modern era of global shlichus. As the capital of Italy and the seat of the Vatican, Rome has been a destination for Chabad emissaries engaged in outreach, diplomatic connections, and the service of Jewish travelers and businesspeople.

The Rebbe (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson) devoted particular attention to Italian Jewry and encouraged shluchim to establish themselves across Italy. Rome, as the country's capital, became an important base of operations. Chabad of Rome provides services to the estimated 15,000 local Jews as well as the tens of thousands of Jewish tourists who visit the Eternal City each year.

Rome also figures in the broader context of Chabad's engagement with Italian Jewry's unique history. The Roman Jewish community's "Italian rite" (Nusach Italki), the oldest surviving synagogue liturgy in the world, represents a living connection to pre-expulsion Jewish practice. Chabad's respect for indigenous Jewish traditions while bringing Lubavitch teaching and practice creates a distinctive dynamic in Rome.

## Notable Jewish Landmarks

- **Tempio Maggiore (Great Synagogue of Rome)**: Built 1901–1904 in a Historicist-Art Nouveau style blending Assyrian, Babylonian, and Greco-Roman elements. Its distinctive square aluminum dome — the only square dome in Rome — is visible from across the city. Houses the Jewish Museum of Rome and serves as an active Orthodox congregation.
- **Roman Jewish Ghetto (Rione Sant'Angelo)**: The historic quarter established by Pope Paul IV in 1555 and abolished in 1888. Today a vibrant neighborhood of restaurants, boutiques, and historic buildings, centered on the Portico d'Ottavia.
- **Jewish Museum of Rome (Museo Ebraico di Roma)**: Located within the Great Synagogue complex; documents 2,100+ years of Roman Jewish life.
- **Jewish Catacombs**: Ancient underground burial sites in several locations (Villa Torlonia, Vigna Randanini) preserving some of the oldest Jewish inscriptions in the Western world.
- **Synagoga Catalana and other historic synagogues**: Several smaller historic congregation spaces preserved within the former Ghetto.
- **Portico d'Ottavia**: Ancient Roman gate incorporated into the medieval Ghetto's fabric; the area around it remains the heart of the old Jewish quarter.
