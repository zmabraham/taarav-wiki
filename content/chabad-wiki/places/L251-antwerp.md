# Antwerp

> Europe's diamond capital and home to one of the largest Haredi Jewish communities outside Israel

![Antwerp Central Station](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/92/Antwerpen_Centraal_station.jpg/800px-Antwerpen_Centraal_station.jpg)
*Antwerp's magnificent Central Station, often called the most beautiful railway station in the world, stands at the edge of the diamond district and the heart of Jewish Antwerp*

## Overview

Antwerp is Belgium's most populous municipality and the capital of Antwerp Province, situated in the Flanders region approximately 40 kilometers north of Brussels. The city straddles the eastern bank of the Scheldt River, whose mouth opens onto the North Sea via the Westerschelde estuary, giving Antwerp one of Europe's most strategically significant seaports — the Port of Antwerp ranks second on the continent, behind only Rotterdam. With a population of approximately 565,000, Antwerp is a city of extraordinary cultural layering, home to some 180 nationalities and a remarkable depth of religious and ethnic communities.

During the 16th century, Antwerp experienced its "Golden Age," becoming the center of the entire international economy. Portuguese traders established major shipping networks here, and the city's bourses pioneered modern financial instruments. The Spanish Fury of 1576 — in which mercenary troops massacred approximately 8,000 residents — dealt a devastating blow, as did the subsequent siege of 1585, which led to a mass exodus of Protestant merchants northward and contributed to Amsterdam's rise. Yet Antwerp recovered and rebuilt, retaining its position as a major Atlantic port and commercial hub well into the modern era.

Today Antwerp is globally recognized for two things above all: the world's diamond trade and its extraordinary concentration of Haredi Jewish life. The city's historic center, with its Gothic Cathedral of Our Lady, Baroque Grote Markt, and ornate Central Station, draws tourists from across Europe, while the diamond district along Pelikaanstraat remains one of the most commercially intense Jewish neighborhoods anywhere in the world. More than 80 percent of Antwerp's Jewish population works in the diamond trade, and Yiddish was historically the primary language of the diamond exchange.

## Jewish Community History

Jewish presence in Antwerp stretches back nearly eight centuries. The first documented evidence appears in a 1261 will of Duke Henry III, and medieval records show periodic Jewish settlement punctuated by the persecutions that swept across Europe. The community was expelled and readmitted multiple times in the medieval and early modern periods. Sephardic Jews fleeing the Iberian Peninsula in the late 15th and early 16th centuries introduced significant new population and commercial acumen — converso merchants from Portugal played a central role in Antwerp's 16th-century golden age of commerce, and even in 1348, when Jews across Europe were accused of well-poisoning during the Black Death, Antwerp's community managed partial survival.

Modern Jewish Antwerp as we know it took shape primarily in the 19th and early 20th centuries, as Ashkenazic Jews from Eastern Europe — fleeing pogroms and poverty in Russia, Poland, and Galicia — arrived in large numbers, drawn by the diamond industry and the relative tolerance of Belgian society. By the 1930s, Antwerp's Jewish population had grown to approximately 35,000, making it one of the most significant Jewish communities in Western Europe.

The Holocaust devastated the community with particular severity. Of Antwerp's approximately 35,000 Jews, roughly 65 percent perished in the Shoah — a significantly higher death rate than in Brussels (approximately 35%), partly due to Antwerp's proximity to Germany and the collaboration of some local authorities in deportations. Jews were primarily deported to Auschwitz-Birkenau. At liberation in 1944, only between 800 and 1,200 Jews remained in the city.

Yet Antwerp's Jewish community made one of the most remarkable recoveries in postwar Europe. The city's mayor actively encouraged surviving Jews and displaced persons to return, recognizing the economic importance of the diamond community. Holocaust survivors, many of them Hasidic, returned or arrived as refugees and rebuilt the diamond trade. Several key trading exchanges were founded or reconstituted by Hasidic diamantaires, including the Diamond Club of Antwerp and the Beurs voor Diamanthandel. No business is conducted on Saturdays at these exchanges, an accommodation to Shabbat observance embedded in the institution's very structure. Today Antwerp's Jewish population numbers approximately 20,000–25,000, of whom roughly 20,000 identify as Haredi and approximately 10,000 as Hasidic — making it one of the largest concentrations of Hasidic Jews in the world outside Israel and the United States.

The community's vitality is visible in every dimension of daily life. Antwerp maintains multiple Jewish day schools, a notable kosher restaurant scene, numerous synagogues, yeshivos, and batei din (religious courts). The neighborhood around Pelikaanstraat is effectively a self-contained Orthodox Jewish world, where Yiddish remains a primary spoken language among many residents.

## Significance in Chabad History

Chabad-Lubavitch maintains a significant and active presence in Antwerp. Among the major Hasidic dynasties represented in the city — including Belz, Satmar, and the locally rooted Pshevorsk dynasty — Chabad has established multiple institutions serving the broader Jewish community as well as Jewish travelers passing through this major European transit hub.

Antwerp's position as the world's diamond capital has long made it a node in international Chabad activity. Jewish diamond traders from Brooklyn, Israel, and elsewhere who travel to Antwerp for business interact with local Chabad houses and synagogues. Chabad shluchim (emissaries) in Antwerp serve both the permanent community and the constant flow of Jewish businesspeople.

The presence of a large, deeply traditional Jewish population in Antwerp also creates a unique environment for Chabad outreach — distinct from most European cities, where Jewish life is sparse and assimilated. Here, Chabad operates within a thriving Orthodox community rather than serving as its sole anchor, focusing on programs for youth, travelers, and Jews from less observant backgrounds who live alongside or near the Haredi neighborhoods. The Rebbe (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson) encouraged his shluchim to establish themselves in Antwerp as part of the broader post-Holocaust mission to rebuild European Jewish life.

## Notable Jewish Landmarks

- **Machsike Hadass Synagogue**: The flagship synagogue of the ultra-Orthodox Machsike Hadass community, one of Antwerp's three main Jewish religious councils and a centerpiece of Haredi communal life.
- **Shomre Hadass Community**: The main Orthodox umbrella community, with its own synagogue and institutions.
- **Portugees Israëlitische Gemeenschap**: The historic Sephardic congregation tracing its roots to converso merchants who made Antwerp's Golden Age possible.
- **Pelikaanstraat Diamond District**: The commercial and spiritual heart of Jewish Antwerp, where diamond trading houses, kosher shops, and synagogues stand side by side and Yiddish is commonly heard.
- **Beurs voor Diamanthandel**: One of the world's premier diamond exchanges, founded by Hasidic diamantaires and still deeply embedded in Orthodox Jewish business culture.
- **Joods Museum van Belgie (Jewish Museum of Belgium)**: A major institution documenting the history of Belgian Jewry.

## Key Historical Events

- **1261** — First documented Jewish presence in Antwerp, in the will of Duke Henry III
- **1348** — Jews accused of well-poisoning during the Black Death; community persecuted
- **1492–1497** — Sephardic refugees from Spain and Portugal arrive in large numbers, transforming the community's character
- **1576** — Spanish Fury devastates the city, disrupting all communities including Jewish residents
- **19th–early 20th century** — Mass Ashkenazic immigration from Eastern Europe; diamond industry becomes central to Jewish economic life
- **1930s** — Jewish population reaches approximately 35,000; one of Western Europe's largest communities
- **1940–1944** — Nazi occupation; 65% of Antwerp's Jews perish in the Holocaust, primarily at Auschwitz-Birkenau
- **1944** — Liberation; only 800–1,200 Jews remain; communal reconstruction begins under municipal encouragement
- **Post-1945** — Diamond industry rebuilt largely by Hasidic Holocaust survivors; Antwerp re-emerges as a global Jewish center
- **2014** — Terrorist attack on the Joods Museum van Belgie in Brussels kills four people, underscoring ongoing antisemitic threats to Belgian Jewry
