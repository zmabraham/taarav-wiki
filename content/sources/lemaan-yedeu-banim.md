## Overview

This book is a personal memoir by an unnamed Chabad Chassid, narrating his life from the 1920s through the post-WWII period. The title comes from Psalm 78:6 — "So that the children shall know" — indicating the memoir's purpose: to transmit the experiences of the Soviet-era underground Jewish life to future generations.

The narrative is structured in five parallel strands (told simultaneously), organized thematically:

1. **Section Aleph: 5674–5698 (1914–1938)** — Childhood and yeshiva years
2. **Section Bet: Moscow** — Underground life and marriage in Moscow
3. **Section Gimel: Samarkand** — Wartime years in Samarkand (Central Asia)
4. **Section Dalet: Paris, Summer 5707 (1947)** — Refugee period in post-war Paris
5. **Section Hey: America** — Life in Brooklyn/New York from 5708 (1948) onward

## Key Themes

### Underground Yeshiva Life (1920s–1930s)
The narrator studied in Tomchei Temimim (Chabad yeshiva) in various locations under Soviet pressure:
- **Vitebsk** (winter 5689/1929) — yeshiva operated secretly
- **Mogilev** — yeshiva in hiding
- **Kherson** (winter 5690/1930) — yeshiva relocated

The memoirs describe how the Yevsektsia (Jewish Section of Soviet Communist Party) systematically shut down religious institutions. Many fellow students "turned" (became irreligious) under pressure; the narrator sees this as the most painful aspect of Soviet persecution.

### Marriage in Moscow (5700/1940)
The narrator met his wife through R. Zusha Paz of Moscow. Her father R. Zusman Pradkin of Shumyatz was a shochet (ritual slaughterer) and de facto rav of his town. The wedding (tena'im) took place in Nisan 5700. The family background: wife's grandfather R. Moshe Leib Paz of Klimovitch was a shochet, mohel, and Chabad Chassid.

### Samarkand (5701–5707 / 1941–1947)
During World War II, the narrator fled east to Samarkand (Uzbekistan, Central Asia) with his family. The section covers:
- Escaping Moscow as Germans approached
- Advice from R. Dovber Kavalyaker (Levertov) in Moscow to leave immediately (Kavalyaker was later arrested by NKVD and exiled)
- Wartime life in Samarkand with the Chassidic community
- Birth of son Gershon (15 Kislev 5708) in Paris
- Escape west through Lemberg (Lvov)

### Post-War Escape ("Bricha")
The narrator describes the "Bricha" (underground escape network) — a clandestine organization of young Israel-oriented men who coordinated the flight of thousands of Jewish refugees across Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria, and Germany to DP camps. The Joint Distribution Committee (JDC/Joint) and the Va'ad HaHatzala (American relief committee) provided coordination and funding.

### Paris Refugee Period (Summer 1947)
The narrator arrived in Paris before Shavuot 5707 (1947). Was housed in the "La Chapelle" hotel, then moved to a mansion in "Sevre" (suburb) rented by the Joint for refugees. Worked as a shochet at the Pletzel (Jewish quarter market in Paris) together with R. Betzalel Vilshansky.

The Lishka (Office for Refugees) founded by the Previous Rebbe (Rayatz) was active in Paris: Director R. Binyamin Gorodetsky, Secretary R. Refael Vilshansky. This organization helped coordinate emigration.

The narrator received a "professor" visa (permanent immigrant visa) from Telshe Yeshiva (through the efforts of Freydel, wife of R. Chaim Stein, mashgiach ruchani of Telshe). This allowed him to settle permanently in America.

### America: Brooklyn (1948 onward)
Settled in Brooklyn, worked as a linotype typesetter for the Geertz Brothers printing house (770 Eastern Parkway area). Set type for major Chabad publications:
- **Kitzurim veHaaros al HaTanya** by the Tzemach Tzedek (first printed 5708/1948)
- **Or HaTorah al Shemos** (the Tzemach Tzedek)
- **Mafteach Maamarei Admur Maharshab**
- Kuntresim (pamphlets) for each Yom Tov

Also worked at "Modern Linatype" shop of R. Yechiel Shapira.

### The Rebbe (Menachem Mendel Schneersohn)
Multiple interactions with the Rebbe (referred to as "the Rebbe shlit"a" throughout) are recorded. The Rebbe personally directed the narrator in typesetting work, handing him manuscripts to set. The narrator describes receiving smiles (panim shokhakot) from the Rebbe on completing significant projects.
