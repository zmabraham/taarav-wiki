# Rashab's Opposition to Zionism (1900–1915)

**Date**: Approximately 1900–1915 (correspondence and position articulated throughout this period)  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]], Theodor Herzl  
**Location**: Lubavitch, Russia

---

## Overview

The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab's]] opposition to political Zionism was one of the most principled and theologically grounded stands of his leadership. At a time when many Jewish leaders were embracing Zionism as a response to rising antisemitism and the plight of Russian Jewry, the Rashab articulated a fierce critique that combined theological objections with practical concerns. His 1900 letter to Theodor Herzl, his pamphlet _Or LaYesharim_ (Light for the Upright), and his ongoing position shaped Chabad's relationship to Zionism for generations to come.

---

## Historical Context: The Rise of Zionism

The late 19th century saw the emergence of political Zionism as a organized movement:

- **1881–1882**: Pogroms in southern Russia sparked mass emigration and early Zionist settlement in Palestine
- **1896**: Theodor Herzl published _Der Judenstaat_ (The Jewish State), arguing for a political solution to the "Jewish question"
- **1897**: The First Zionist Congress in Basel established the World Zionist Organization
- **Early 1900s**: The movement gained momentum among both secular and some religious Jews

Many religious leaders initially supported settlement in Palestine (_aliyah_) for purely religious reasons. However, political Zionism — which sought to establish a Jewish state through secular political means — was deeply controversial.

---

## The Letter to Theodor Herzl (1900)

In **1900**, Theodor Herzl wrote to the Rashab seeking his support for the Zionist movement. Herzl, recognizing the Rashab's moral authority among Russian Jewry, hoped to win the endorsement of one of Hasidism's most respected leaders.

The Rashab's response was unambiguous and sharp. He wrote to Herzl that he could not support a movement that:

1. **Sought to replace Torah with nationalism** — The Zionist movement's secular foundations were incompatible with Jewish religious life
2. **Promised false redemption** — Political solutions could not accomplish what only divine redemption could achieve
3. **Endangered Jewish communities** — Zionist activism risked provoking governments without offering real protection

The letter, which became widely known in Jewish circles, was an early and clear statement of what would become the Chabad position on Zionism: support for Jewish settlement in Palestine for religious reasons, but principled opposition to secular political Zionism as an ideology and movement.

---

## Or LaYesharim (1900)

Following his correspondence with Herzl, the Rashab published **_Or LaYesharim_** (Light for the Upright) in **1900** — a pamphlet that systematically articulated his theological objections to Zionism. The work was directed toward "the upright" — those sincere Jews who might be drawn to Zionism out of genuine concern for Jewish suffering, without fully understanding its religious implications.

### Key Arguments in Or LaYesharim

**1. The Theological Argument: Redemption vs. Politics**

The Rashab argued that the Jewish people's connection to the Land of Israel is fundamentally religious, not national. The Land cannot be "redeemed" through political means, settlement, or diplomatic maneuvering. Only the Messiah — through divine intervention — can accomplish true redemption. Human political efforts, however well-intentioned, cannot replace the messianic promise.

**2. The Spiritual Danger of Secular Nationalism**

Zionism, in the Rashab's analysis, represented a new form of _avodah zarah_ (idolatry) — substituting nationalism for Torah. By making the Land itself rather than divine service the center of Jewish identity, Zionism inverted Jewish priorities. The Rashab warned that this would lead to spiritual assimilation, even as it promised national revival.

**3. The Practical Objection: The Secular Character of the Movement**

Even if one granted that settlement in Palestine was desirable, the Rashab argued that the actual Zionist movement — led by secular, often anti-religious figures — could not be trusted to protect Jewish religious interests. The movement's culture, values, and leadership were fundamentally opposed to Torah Judaism. Supporting it meant empowering those who would dismantle Jewish religious life.

**4. The False Promise**

The Rashab warned that Zionism offered false hope to suffering Jewish communities. Political solutions could not address the spiritual condition of the Jewish people, and could even exacerbate external dangers by provoking governments and alienating allies.

---

## Personal Encounters and Influence

The Rashab's position had practical effects beyond his writings. When Zionist activists sought to gain support in Hasidic communities, the Rashab's influence was a significant barrier. His students and followers — particularly the graduates of [[events/tomchei-temimim-founding|Tomchei Temimim yeshiva]] — were trained to understand and articulate this position.

This educational work produced a generation of Chabad activists who maintained consistent opposition to secular Zionism, even as many other religious groups eventually accommodated themselves to the movement.

---

## The Distinction: Zionism vs. Living in Palestine

It is critical to understand what the Rashab opposed — and what he did not oppose:

**What He Opposed:**

- Political Zionism as an ideological movement
- Secular Jewish nationalism that replaced Torah
- The Zionist Organization and its leadership
- The idea that a Jewish state could be achieved without divine redemption

**What He Did Not Oppose:**

- Individual Jews moving to Palestine for religious reasons
- Religious communities in Palestine (in fact, Chabad had communities in [[events/hebron-community-1823|Hebron]] and [[events/toras-emes-hebron-1911|supported yeshivos there]])
- The religious obligation to live in the Land when possible
- Prayer for the rebuilding of Jerusalem and the Temple

The Rashab himself maintained and supported religious institutions in Palestine, including the [[events/toras-emes-hebron-1911|Toras Emes yeshiva in Hebron]]. His opposition was specifically to secular political Zionism, not to Jewish presence in the Land for religious reasons.

---

## Impact on Chabad's Position

The Rashab's articulation of Chabad's anti-Zionist position became normative for the movement. Subsequent Rebbes — including the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] and the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]] — maintained variations of this position, though with different emphases and accommodations to historical reality.

**The Rayatz** maintained his father's basic theological critique, though he navigated complex relationships with Zionist and later Israeli institutions. **The seventh Rebbe** developed a more nuanced position — emphasizing support for the safety and security of Jews in Israel while maintaining reservations about secular Zionist ideology and certain state policies.

What remained constant was the Chabad principle that Jewish identity is fundamentally religious, not national — that the Land is holy because of the Torah, not the reverse.

---

## Historical Irony

In a historical irony, the Rashab's critique of Zionism's secular foundations anticipated many tensions that would emerge after 1948. The relationship between religion and state in Israel, the status of non-Orthodox Jewish movements, the question of "who is a Jew," and the ongoing debate between religious and secular Israelis — all reflect the fundamental tension the Rashab identified: can a Jewish state be Jewish without Torah?

That Chabad would eventually play a major role in Israel — through [[events/kfar-chabad-founding|Kfar Chabad]], educational institutions, and the outreach of shluchim — while maintaining a complex relationship to Zionist ideology, reflects the ongoing relevance of the Rashab's framework.
