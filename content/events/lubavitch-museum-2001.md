# Lubavitch Museum Opening (2001)

**Establishment of a museum and memorial site at the historic location of the original Lubavitch Chabad center in present-day Russia**

## Background

After decades of Soviet rule, the town of Lubavitch (present-day Russia, Smolensk Oblast) retained little visible trace of its former role as the world center of Chabad Chassidism. The buildings of the original Lubavitch courtyard — the Rebbe's residence, the synagogue, the yeshiva — had been destroyed in the 1921 fire and were never rebuilt during the Soviet era.

With the fall of the Soviet Union (1991), Jewish heritage preservation became possible in the former Soviet space. Chabad organizations worked to restore historic sites connected to the movement.

## What Happened

In 2001, a museum was opened at the historic site of the Lubavitch Chabad center. The opening marked the first formal recognition of Lubavitch's role in Jewish and specifically Chabad history in the post-Soviet era. The museum provided a physical memorial to the century during which Lubavitch served as the Chabad capital (approximately 1813–1915/1921).

## Significance

The 2001 museum opening represents the closing of a circle: after approximately 80 years of Soviet suppression, during which the physical legacy of Lubavitch was essentially erased, the post-Soviet period allowed for at least a memorial recognition of the site's historical importance. For Chabad Chassidim, Lubavitch remains a deeply significant location — the "ir v'em b'Yisrael" (city and mother in Israel) where the movement was established and where generations of Chassidim traveled to receive guidance from the Rebbes.
