# The Tefillin Campaign (1967)

**Date**: June 1967 (launched during the Six Day War)  
**Initiated by**: [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|The Seventh Rebbe]]

---

## Overview

In June 1967, as Israel fought the Six Day War, the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Lubavitcher Rebbe]] launched an unprecedented public initiative: a campaign to encourage every Jewish man to put on **tefillin** (phylacteries). What began as a wartime spiritual mobilization became a permanent institution — the first of the Rebbe's famous **Mitzvah Campaigns** (_mivtzo'im_) — and fundamentally changed how Chabad engaged with unaffiliated Jews worldwide.

---

## Background: The Six Day War

In late May 1967, Egypt's President Nasser closed the Straits of Tiran, massed troops in the Sinai, and called for Israel's destruction. Syria and Jordan joined the alliance. The Jewish world feared a second Holocaust.

In the weeks before the war, the Rebbe delivered a series of urgent talks. His message was distinctive: while the world debated military strategy, the Rebbe spoke about spiritual protection. He cited the Talmudic passage (Berachot 6a): "And all the nations of the world will see that the Name of God is called upon you, and they will fear you" — which the Talmud interprets as referring to the tefillin worn on the head. The Rebbe argued that the physical act of putting on tefillin provided genuine spiritual protection for the Jewish people and the Land of Israel.

---

## The Campaign Launch

During and immediately after the Six Day War (June 5–10, 1967), the Rebbe called upon his students and followers to go out to the streets — and particularly to the Western Wall, newly accessible after the war — and help Jewish men put on tefillin. Many of these men had never worn tefillin before, or had not done so since their bar mitzvah.

The initial response was extraordinary. Soldiers at the Western Wall, civilians in Israeli cities, and Jews in communities worldwide participated. Photographs of soldiers putting on tefillin at the Kotel became iconic images of the era.

---

## The Campaign Becomes Permanent

What could have been a one-time wartime effort became a permanent institution. The Rebbe declared that the tefillin campaign should continue indefinitely. Key developments:

### Tefillin Stands

Chabad students and volunteers set up **tefillin stands** (often simple folding tables) in high-traffic public locations — street corners, shopping centers, university campuses, airports, business districts. They would approach Jewish men with the question: "Are you Jewish? Would you like to put on tefillin?"

This practice — approaching strangers in public to offer a religious act — was revolutionary. No Jewish organization had done anything like it at scale. It was initially met with skepticism and even hostility from parts of the Orthodox establishment, who questioned whether it was appropriate to perform a mitzvah so casually, and from secular Jews who found the public solicitation intrusive.

The Rebbe's response was firm: every single time a Jewish man puts on tefillin, even once, it has infinite spiritual value. The act need not be preceded by extensive study or commitment. The mitzvah itself is the point.

### Mitzvah Tanks

In the 1970s, the campaign evolved further with the introduction of **Mitzvah Tanks** — converted RVs and vans equipped with tefillin, Shabbat candles, and Jewish literature. These mobile units could reach areas where fixed stands were impractical. Mitzvah Tanks became a visual symbol of Chabad's outreach and operated in major cities worldwide.

---

## The Rebbe's Rationale

The Rebbe articulated several reasons for the campaign:

1. **Spiritual protection**: Tefillin provide a tangible spiritual shield for the Jewish people, especially in times of danger
2. **The value of a single mitzvah**: Even one act of tefillin-wearing has cosmic significance, regardless of the person's overall level of observance
3. **Awakening the soul**: The physical act of putting on tefillin awakens the divine soul (_nefesh ha'elokit_) within every Jew
4. **Jewish unity**: The campaign expressed the unity of the Jewish people — every Jew, regardless of background, performs the same mitzvah
5. **The power of action**: In Chabad philosophy, _ma'aseh_ (action) is paramount — a single physical deed can accomplish what contemplation alone cannot

---

## Impact

The Tefillin Campaign had far-reaching consequences:

- **Tens of thousands** of Jewish men who had never worn tefillin — or had not done so in decades — began doing so, many for the first time since their bar mitzvah
- The campaign established the model for all subsequent Chabad outreach: direct, personal, public, and focused on specific mitzvos
- It launched the broader **Mitzvah Campaigns** framework, which eventually included ten campaigns: tefillin, mezuzah, Torah study, tzedakah, Jewish books in the home, Shabbat candles, kashrut, family purity, Jewish education, and love of fellow Jews
- The "Are you Jewish?" question became a cultural phenomenon — recognized (and sometimes parodied) far beyond Jewish circles
- It fundamentally shifted Chabad's institutional identity from an insular Chassidic community to a global outreach movement

---

## Connection to Shlichus

The Tefillin Campaign was both a product of and catalyst for the [[concepts/shlichus|shlichus]] (emissary) system. While the Rebbe had been sending shluchim to communities since the 1950s, the tefillin campaign gave every Chabad student and layperson a concrete, simple outreach activity. It democratized the concept of shlichus — you did not need to move to a distant city to be an emissary; you could be one on your local street corner.

---

## Legacy

The Tefillin Campaign continues to operate worldwide. On any given Friday afternoon, Chabad volunteers can be found with tefillin at street corners, college campuses, and public spaces in hundreds of cities. The Mitzvah Tanks still roll through Manhattan, Tel Aviv, London, Paris, and countless other cities. The campaign's model — approaching Jews with a specific, concrete, non-judgmental offer of a mitzvah — became the template for all of Chabad's outreach work and arguably the most visible expression of the movement in the public sphere.
