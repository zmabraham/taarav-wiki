# The Great Escape — Detailed Account (1946–1947)

**Date**: Late 1946 – early 1947  
**Route**: Soviet Central Asia → Lvov → Poland → Germany/Austria → Western Europe → Palestine/America  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], [[entities/people/binyamin-gorodetsky|Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky]], the **Ramash** (future seventh Rebbe) in Paris

---

## Overview

The **Great Escape (_HaBricha HaGedolah_)** was the organized exodus of approximately **1,000 Chabad chassidim** from the Soviet Union in 1946–1947, exploiting a brief window created by the **Soviet-Polish repatriation agreement**. This page provides expanded detail on the operation, including specific routes, organizers, dangers, and the role of the **Ramash** (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn, the future seventh Rebbe) in coordinating from Paris.

For a comprehensive overview, see [[events/great-escape|The Great Escape (1946–1947) — Overview]].

---

## The Repatriation Agreement Window

### Soviet-Polish Agreement (1945–1946)

In **1945–1946**, the Soviet Union and the new Polish communist government signed an agreement allowing **ethnic Poles** who had been stranded in the Soviet Union during World War II to repatriate to Poland.

**The Loophole**:

- Anyone who could demonstrate Polish citizenship or origin could leave
- Most Chabad chassidim were Russian, Belarusian, or Ukrainian — not Polish
- This meant they needed forged Polish documents

**The Window Was Brief**:

- The agreement was open for a limited time
- By late 1947, the window was closing
- Those who didn't escape would remain trapped for decades

**Why This Worked**:

- The Soviet Union wanted to reduce its population burden after the war
- Poland wanted to increase its population
- The agreement served both governments' interests

## The Operation Structure

### Organization Centers

**Lvov (Lemberg)**:

- A **rabbinical court of 23** (_beis din_) was convened in Lvov, the Ukrainian city near the Polish border
- **Purpose**: Set halachic guidelines, pool resources, coordinate logistics, establish contacts
- **Rabbi Yaakov Landau**: Named as one of the key organizers in Lvov

**Samarkand, Tashkent, Bukhara**:

- Major Chabad communities in Soviet Central Asia
- Chassidim had gathered there during and after the war
- These were the departure points for groups heading west

**Paris**:

- **The Ramash** (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn, future seventh Rebbe) coordinated from Paris
- He communicated with the underground network through coded messages
- He arranged reception and onward travel for those who escaped

### Forged Documents Operation

**The Need**:

- Each family needed Polish birth certificates, identity papers, and travel permits
- Soviet border guards were trained to detect forgeries
- A mistake could mean arrest for the entire network

**The Counterfeiters**:

- Skilled craftsmen within the community created forged documents
- Some were professionals who had worked in printing or document preparation
- Others learned forgery skills out of necessity

**Document Types**:

- Polish birth certificates showing birth in Poland
- Polish identity cards (_Kennkarte_ equivalent)
- Soviet internal travel permits (to reach the border)
- Sometimes, entirely fictional identities were created

**Quality Control**:

- Documents were reviewed by multiple people to catch errors
- Test runs were conducted to see if border officials would accept certain types of papers
- The quality improved over time as the operation learned

## The Route: Step by Step

### Phase 1: Central Asia to Lvov

**Starting Points**: Samarkand, Tashkent, Bukhara, and other cities in Soviet Central Asia

**Journey**:

- Travel by train across thousands of miles of Soviet territory
- Passes through Moscow, Kiev, and other major cities
- Required internal travel permits

**Dangers**:

- Document checks at train stations
- Police patrols looking for suspicious travelers
- Informants who might report groups of Jews

**Duration**: Several days to a week, depending on connections

### Phase 2: Gathering in Lvov

**Lvov as Staging Area**:

- Families would gather in Lvov, awaiting their crossing opportunity
- Hidden in safe houses, often in crowded conditions
- The beis din coordinated groups and timing

**Preparations**:

- Final document checks
- Coordination with border guards (some were bribed)
- Prayer and spiritual preparation

**Tension**:

- Every day in Lvov increased the risk of discovery
- Soviet authorities were aware of unauthorized departures
- Raids were always a possibility

### Phase 3: The Border Crossing

**Primary Crossing Point**: Lvov area, Soviet-Polish border

**Methods**:

- **Crossing as Polish Repatriates**: Groups disguised as families returning to Poland
- **Bribed Guards**: Some border guards were paid to look the other way
- **Night Crossings**: Some crossed at night in unguarded areas

**Transport**:

- Often in **cattle cars** — the same type of railroad cars used for wartime deportations
- Packed with families and possessions
- Conditions were humiliating and dangerous

**Dangers**:

- Arrest for carrying forged documents
- Interrogation and torture
- Imprisonment or exile to Siberia
- Some groups were stopped and some members arrested

### Phase 4: Poland to Western Europe

**Poland as Transit Point**:

- Once across the border, escapees were in Poland
- Poland was unstable under its new communist government
- The goal was to reach the American occupation zone in Germany

**Route**:

- Poland → Germany (American zone) → **Displaced Persons camps** (Feldafing, Landsberg, etc.)
- Alternatively: Poland → Austria → American/French zones
- Some went directly to France

**Chabad Reception**:

- **[[entities/people/binyamin-gorodetsky|Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky]]** and other representatives met refugees
- [[events/chabad-in-displaced-persons-camps|Chabad DP camps]] provided temporary housing and support
- Spiritual and material assistance was provided

## The Ramash's Role from Paris

### Coordination from Afar

**Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn** (the Ramash, later the seventh Rebbe) was living in **Paris** in 1946–1947. His role was pivotal:

**Communication**:

- Coded correspondence with the underground network in the Soviet Union
- Messages passed through trusted couriers traveling between Paris and Eastern Europe
- Communication was slow and dangerous

**Financial Coordination**:

- The Rayatz in Brooklyn sent funds
- The Ramash distributed money in Europe to support the operation
- Funds covered bribes, travel costs, and support in DP camps

**Reception and Onward Travel**:

- The Ramash coordinated reception of refugees in Western Europe
- He arranged their onward movement to Palestine or America
- He ensured that escapees had what they needed

### Spiritual Leadership

Even from afar, the Ramash provided spiritual guidance:

- He advised on halachic questions arising during the escape
- He gave encouragement to those undertaking the dangerous journey
- His presence in Paris provided a connection to the Rebbe (the Rayatz)

## The Bricha Organization

### What Was Bricha?

**Bricha** ("Escape" or "Flight" in Hebrew) was the organized movement that helped Jewish survivors escape Eastern Europe after World War II:

- Operated from 1944–1948
- Helped approximately 250,000 Jews leave Eastern Europe
- Worked with Zionist organizations, the Jewish Brigade, and other groups

### Cooperation with Chabad

Chabad worked alongside Bricha:

- Bricha provided **logistical expertise** and knowledge of escape routes
- Chabad provided **funds** and used its organizational networks
- The cooperation was pragmatic — both wanted to get Jews out of the Soviet sphere

### Route Knowledge

Bricha had developed expertise on:

- Which border crossings were safest
- Which guards could be bribed
- How to navigate the chaotic postwar landscape
- Chabad benefited from this knowledge

## Dangers and Casualties

### Not Everyone Escaped

The operation was not without cost:

**Arrests**:

- Some groups were caught at the border
- Individuals with forged papers were arrested
- Some were imprisoned or sent to Siberia

**Failures**:

- Not all attempts to cross succeeded
- Some families were turned back and had to try again later
- Some never got another opportunity

**Those Left Behind**:

- Hundreds of Chabad chassidim remained in the Soviet Union
- They continued underground religious life for another generation
- Many did not emigrate until the 1970s–1990s

### Specific Dangers

**Document Fraud**:

- Possession of forged documents was a serious crime
- Discovery meant arrest and likely imprisonment

**Border Guards**:

- Some guards were honest and would arrest escapees
- Others were corrupt but could not be trusted entirely
- Bribery was always risky

**Informants**:

- The Soviet government encouraged citizens to report suspicious activity
- An informants could expose entire networks

**Physical Danger**:

- Travel in postwar Europe was hazardous
- Infrastructure was destroyed
- Criminal elements preyed on refugees

## The Az Yashir Moment (December 2, 1946)

### The Event

On **December 2, 1946**, a large group of chassidim was attempting to cross the Soviet-Polish border. In New York, the Rayatz called for the recitation of **Az Yashir** (_Shirat HaYam_, the Song of the Sea from Exodus) at precisely the time the crossing was occurring.

The chassidim later learned that their border crossing succeeded at that exact moment.

### Spiritual Significance

This was understood as:

- **Divine providence**: The Rebbe's spiritual awareness transcended physical distance
- **Protection**: God's protection of those attempting the dangerous crossing
- **Connection**: The bond between the Rebbe and his chassidim, even across continents

The story was retold for generations as an example of the **tzaddik's** spiritual connection to his community.

## Aftermath and Dispersion

### Destinations

Once across the border and through Western Europe, the escapees dispersed:

**Palestine/Israel** (1947–1949):

- Many went to Palestine, arriving before or during Israel's War of Independence
- Some fought in the 1948 war
- Many became the founding population of [[events/kfar-chabad-founding|Kfar Chabad]] (1949)

**America**:

- Some joined the Rayatz in Brooklyn
- Strengthened the nascent American Chabad community
- Brought with them the living traditions of Soviet Chabad

**Western Europe**:

- Some remained in France, England, or elsewhere
- Established Chabad communities in these countries
- Served as waystations for future escapees

### Demographic Impact

The escape of approximately 1,000 chassidim was demographically significant:

- This was a critical mass for rebuilding
- They represented the surviving remnant of Soviet Chabad
- They carried with them the traditions, texts, and practices of the underground

---

## Historical Significance

The Great Escape was the last organized Jewish escape from the Soviet Union before the Iron Curtain sealed completely:

- Those who escaped in 1946–1947 were the lucky ones
- Those who remained would be trapped for decades
- Many would not see freedom until the Soviet Jewry movement of the 1970s–1990s

The operation demonstrated:

- **Chabad's organizational capacity** even under the most difficult conditions
- **Mesirus nefesh** (self-sacrifice) for Torah and Jewish life
- **The centrality of the Rebbe's leadership** — even from afar
- **Community resilience** in the face of persecution
