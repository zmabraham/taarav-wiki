# Arrest and Liberation of the Rayatz (1927)

**Date**: Arrested: 15 Sivan 5687 (June 15, 1927); Liberated: 12–13 Tammuz 5687 (July 13, 1927)  
**Location**: Leningrad (St. Petersburg), Soviet Russia → Kostroma (exile) → Liberation  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (Rayatz)]]

---

## Overview

The arrest of the [[entities/people/rayatz|sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe]] by the Soviet GPU (secret police) in June 1927 — and his extraordinary liberation less than a month later — is one of the most dramatic events in modern Jewish history. Sentenced initially to death, then to ten years in the notorious Solovki prison camp, and finally commuted to three years' exile in Kostroma, the Rayatz was released on **12–13 Tammuz 5687** following intense international pressure. His liberation is celebrated annually as **Yud-Beis Tammuz** — one of the major Chabad holidays — and is understood as the third in a pattern of Chabad Rebbe arrests followed by miraculous liberations.

---

## Background: The Yevsektsiya's War on Religion

Since the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917, the Soviet regime had waged systematic war against all forms of religion. For the Jewish community, the primary instrument was the **Yevsektsiya** (Jewish Section of the Communist Party) — an organization of Jewish communists tasked with destroying traditional Jewish life.

The [[events/yevsektsiya-campaign|Yevsektsiya's campaign]] targeted:

- **Synagogues**: forced closures, confiscation of buildings
- **Jewish education**: shutdown of cheders (elementary schools) and yeshivos
- **Ritual life**: bans on circumcision, kosher slaughter, mikvah
- **Rabbis and religious leaders**: arrests, exile, show trials

The Rayatz, who had assumed Chabad leadership upon his father's death in 1920, defied the regime systematically. From his base in Leningrad, he directed an underground network of:

- Clandestine **cheders** in dozens of cities
- Secret **yeshivos** operating in shifting locations
- Underground **mikvaos** (ritual baths)
- Illegal **religious courts** (batei din)
- Fundraising for **Palestine** (illegal under Soviet law)
- Correspondence with **foreign Jewish leaders** (considered espionage)

The GPU monitored these activities closely. By 1927, they had compiled extensive files on the Rayatz's underground network.

## The Arrest

### 3:00 AM, 15 Sivan 5687 (June 15, 1927)

GPU agents arrived at the Rayatz's home at **22 Machovaya Street, Leningrad** at approximately 3:00 in the morning. They presented an arrest warrant and began searching the premises for incriminating documents.

### "I Recognize Only One Authority"

The Rayatz's response became legendary. When confronted by the GPU agents, he declared: **"I recognize only one authority — the Almighty."** He refused to be intimidated, maintained his dignity throughout the arrest, and continued his religious practices even in custody.

One agent reportedly pulled a revolver and pointed it at the Rebbe, saying: "This little toy has made many a man change his mind." The Rayatz replied: "That little toy can intimidate only the kind of man who has many gods and only one world. Because I have only one God and two worlds, I am not impressed by your little toy."

### The Charges

The Rayatz was charged with three primary offenses:

1. **Operating illegal religious schools** (underground cheders and yeshivos)
2. **Collecting funds for Palestine** (illegal foreign fundraising)
3. **Maintaining correspondence with foreign agents** (letters to Jewish leaders abroad)

Each charge carried severe penalties under Soviet law.

---

## Imprisonment and Sentencing

### Initial Sentence: Death

The Rayatz was initially sentenced to **death by firing squad** — the standard GPU sentence for counter-revolutionary activity. The sentence reflected the severity with which the regime viewed organized religious resistance.

### Commutation to Solovki

Following initial interventions — the details of which are debated by historians — the death sentence was commuted to **ten years in the Solovki labor camp**, a notoriously brutal island prison in the White Sea where many political prisoners died of cold, starvation, and forced labor.

### Further Commutation to Exile

Additional pressure resulted in a further commutation: **three years of internal exile in Kostroma**, a city northeast of Moscow. This was a dramatic reduction — exile in a provincial city versus a death camp — though it still meant separation from his community and restriction of his activities.

---

## International Pressure

The Rayatz's liberation was the result of an extraordinary international campaign:

### Jewish Communities Worldwide

News of the arrest spread rapidly through Jewish communities in Europe, America, and Palestine. Protests, petitions, and diplomatic appeals poured into Moscow from:

- **American Jewish organizations**: major communal bodies intervened through diplomatic channels
- **European Jewish leaders**: particularly in Latvia, Germany, and Poland
- **Palestinian Jewish institutions**: the Chief Rabbinate and others protested
- **Individual rabbis and community leaders**: across the Jewish world

### Diplomatic Contacts

Several governments applied pressure on the Soviet Union:

- The **Latvian government** (the Rayatz held Latvian citizenship through his time in Riga) made formal diplomatic representations
- **Mordechai Dubin**, a Latvian Jewish parliamentarian and Chabad supporter, traveled to Moscow to personally negotiate with Soviet officials
- Various diplomatic back-channels were activated through Jewish community connections

### The German Jewish Intervention

Berlin Jewry played a particularly effective role, documented in the Chabad-Germany volume:

- **Rabbi Dr. Meir Hildesheimer** (head of the Orthodox Berlin Rabbinical Seminary) and **Dr. Leo Baeck** (leader of Liberal/Reform Berlin Jewry) — in an extraordinary act of cross-denominational solidarity — jointly petitioned **Dr. Oskar Cohn** (a Jewish socialist Reichstag member)
- Cohn met with Foreign Minister **Stresemann** and Vice-Reichskanzler **Weissmann**, who pressured Soviet Ambassador **Krestinsky** in Berlin
- Cohn also personally contacted Soviet Foreign Commissar **Chicherin** — his personal friend — to intervene directly
- The **Frankfurt Orthodox community** sent the Rayatz a formal rabbinical appointment letter — providing a face-saving rationale for Soviet release

See [[events/german-jewish-intervention-1927|German Jewish Intervention to Free the Rayatz (1927)]] for full detail.

### The Role of Mordechai Dubin

[[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Mordechai Dubin]], a member of the Latvian Saeima (parliament) and a devoted Chabad chassid, played a pivotal role. He traveled to Moscow, leveraged his diplomatic status, and negotiated directly with Soviet officials. His intervention is credited as one of the decisive factors in securing the commutation and eventual release.

---

## Liberation: 12–13 Tammuz

On **12 Tammuz 5687 (July 12, 1927)**, the Rayatz was informed that his sentence had been commuted and he was free to leave the Soviet Union. On **13 Tammuz**, he departed Kostroma.

The liberation was perceived as miraculous — a modern echo of divine redemption. The Rayatz himself described it in providential terms, connecting it to earlier moments of Jewish liberation.

---

## Expulsion from the Soviet Union

The liberation came with a condition: the Rayatz was expelled from the Soviet Union. He traveled first to **Riga, Latvia**, where he established a temporary Chabad headquarters. From Riga, he continued to direct the underground operations inside the Soviet Union, though at a greater remove.

His subsequent movements: [[events/riga-years-1927-1933|Riga (1927–1933)]] → [[events/warsaw-years-1933-1939|Warsaw (1933–1939)]] → [[events/escape-from-warsaw-1939|Escape from Warsaw (1939)]] → [[events/chabad-america-arrival|America (1940)]].

## The Pattern of Arrest and Liberation

The Rayatz's arrest and liberation fits a striking pattern in Chabad history:

| Rebbe                            | Arrest           | Liberation | Holiday           |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------- | ---------- | ----------------- | ------------------- |
| [[entities/people/alter-rebbe    | Alter Rebbe]]    | 1798       | 19 Kislev 5559    | **Yud-Tes Kislev**  |
| [[entities/people/alter-rebbe    | Alter Rebbe]]    | 1800       | 3 Tammuz 5561     | (commemorated)      |
| [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe | Mitteler Rebbe]] | 1826       | 10 Kislev 5587    | **Yud Kislev**      |
| [[entities/people/rayatz         | Rayatz]]         | 1927       | 12–13 Tammuz 5687 | **Yud-Beis Tammuz** |

Each arrest followed a period of intensified spiritual and communal activity; each liberation was interpreted as divine vindication of the Rebbe's mission; each liberation date became a permanent Chabad holiday marked by farbrengens, Torah study, and celebration.

---

## Legacy

Yud-Beis Tammuz is celebrated annually in Chabad communities worldwide as a day of spiritual renewal and recommitment. The Rayatz's defiance of Soviet tyranny — his willingness to face death rather than cease his religious work — became a paradigm of [[concepts/mesirus-nefesh|mesirus nefesh]] (self-sacrifice) for subsequent generations.

The Rayatz wrote extensively about his imprisonment, and these accounts — collected in various publications — provide both historical documentation and spiritual teaching about the nature of faith under persecution.
