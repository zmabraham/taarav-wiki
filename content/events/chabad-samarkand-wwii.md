# Chabad in Samarkand During World War II (1941–1946)

**The congregation of Chabad families in Samarkand, Central Asia, as refugees fled the Nazi advance; creation of a vibrant underground Jewish community in Uzbekistan**

## Background

When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941 (Operation Barbarossa), Jewish families from Leningrad, White Russia, and Ukraine fled eastward ahead of the advancing German army. Many Chabad families from Leningrad and other cities made their way to Central Asia — particularly to Samarkand, Tashkent, and surrounding areas in Uzbekistan.

## The Samarkand Community

Samarkand already had an indigenous Jewish community — the Bukharan Jews — who had lived in Central Asia for centuries. The arrival of thousands of Eastern European Jewish refugees transformed the community.

The Shusterman memoir (Lmaan Yadau) provides a firsthand account of the Chabad refugee community in Samarkand:

- **Arrival** — The refugees arrived beginning in fall 1941; many had to find housing in the old city ("Alt Shtat")
- **Religious life** — Despite the Soviet ban on religion, the Chabad community organized prayer minyanim. The first Yom Kippur services were held in private apartments, with extreme secrecy to avoid NKVD detection
- **Secret Torah scrolls** — At least one Chabad family in Samarkand had a Torah scroll; private homes rotated as locations for Torah reading

**Key figures in wartime Samarkand included:**
- **R' Pinchas Shreiber (Raksbishker)** — a celebrated Chabad Chassid who participated in the underground minyanim
- **R' Yeshaya Hofin** — helped new arrivals find housing and organize religious life
- **R' Aizik Karasik** — had a Torah scroll and organized Torah readings

The community also received an influx of Polish Jews who had been released from Siberian labor camps in 1941–42 under the Sikorski-Stalin agreement. These Polish Jewish refugees, streaming into Samarkand for the winter, created spontaneous large-scale gatherings for Kol Nidrei.

## Significance

The wartime Samarkand community was one of the most significant concentrations of Chabad Chassidim outside of New York during the 1940s. The underground religious life maintained there — Torah study, prayer, observance of Jewish law under Soviet anti-religious pressure — represented a continuation of the underground Chabad network that had operated in the Soviet Union since the 1920s. After the war, many of these families eventually made their way to the West (via Eastern Europe, displaced persons camps, and Paris), arriving in America and Israel.
