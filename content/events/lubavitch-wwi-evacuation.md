# WWI Evacuation from Lubavitch (1914–1915)

**Date**: 1914–1915 (5684–5685)  
**Location**: [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]], Russia → Rostov-on-Don  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]], [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]

---

## Overview

In **1914–1915**, World War I reached the Pale of Settlement, and the front lines approached [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]]. The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]] ordered the evacuation of the entire Chabad community — including the [[events/tomchei-temimim-founding|Tomchei Temimim yeshiva]] — westward and southward, ultimately to Rostov-on-Don. This traumatic displacement ended Lubavitch's 102-year role as Chabad's spiritual capital and scattered a community that had been the center of Chabad life for generations.

---

## Background: World War I and the Eastern Front

**August 1914**: World War I began. The Russian Empire faced Germany and Austro-Hungary on its western borders.

**1914–1915**: The Eastern Front moved through the Pale of Settlement — the region of western Russia where most Jews lived. German forces advanced deep into Russian territory, capturing major cities including Vilna (Vilnius), Warsaw, and much of present-day Belarus and Ukraine.

**Jewish Refugees**: The Russian military forcibly evacuated Jewish populations from border regions, fearing collaboration with the enemy. Hundreds of thousands of Jews became refugees, moving eastward in desperate conditions.

Lubavitch, in the Mogilev region of present-day Belarus, was not immediately on the front but was close enough to be endangered. The German advance was unpredictable, and the town lay within the potential zone of combat.

---

## The Decision to Evacuate

The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]] faced a difficult choice:

**Option 1: Remain in Lubavitch**

- Risk: The front could shift suddenly, placing the community in combat zone
- Danger: German occupation, though initially less hostile to Jews than the Russians, would bring its own dangers
- precedent: During the [[events/napoleons-invasion-1812|Napoleonic invasion of 1812]], the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] had chosen to flee rather than remain under potential French occupation

**Option 2: Evacuate**

- Risk: Displacement, loss of property, disruption of communal life
- Danger: Refugee conditions, disease, hunger
- Precedent: The Alter Rebbe's flight in 1812 had resulted in his death in exile

The Rashab chose evacuation. His reasoning was practical: the safety of the community took precedence over sentimental attachment to Lubavitch. He chose **Rostov-on-Don** as the destination — a major city in southern Russia (present-day Russia, near the Ukrainian border), far from the front lines and relatively stable.

---

## The Evacuation Process

The evacuation was not instantaneous but unfolded over months in **1914–1915**:

### Tomchei Temimim Students

The **Tomchei Temimim yeshiva** was the first priority. Hundreds of students needed to be moved:

- **Travel**: Students traveled in groups by train and wagon, navigating a transportation system overwhelmed by military needs
- **Documentation**: Travel permits were required; the yeshiva secured these through connections with sympathetic officials
- **Destination**: Students were initially sent to various temporary locations before regrouping in Rostov
- **Loss**: Many students lost their personal belongings, books, and possessions

The evacuation of the yeshiva was particularly chaotic. The Rashab's son and successor, the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], was heavily involved in coordinating this effort.

### Families and Community Members

The broader Chabad community in Lubavitch also evacuated:

- **Homes**: Families sold or abandoned homes and property, often at a fraction of value
- **Businesses**: Shops and livelihoods were dismantled
- **Synagogues**: Sacred objects and Torah scrolls were removed; buildings were locked or repurposed
- **Cemeteries**: Ancestors' graves remained behind — a source of ongoing pain

The emotional toll was immense. Lubavitch had been home for generations. Many families had lived in the town since the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]] established Chabad there in [[events/chabad-in-lubavitch-1813|1813]].

---

## The Journey: Chaos and Hardship

The journey from Lubavitch to Rostov-on-Don covered approximately **800 miles** (1,300 kilometers) — a vast distance in wartime conditions.

**Transportation**: Trains were overcrowded with soldiers and refugees. Wagon travel was slow and dangerous, particularly in winter.

**Conditions**: Refugee camps sprang up along the route. Disease (particularly typhus) spread. Food was scarce.

**Security**: The war zone made travel hazardous. Bands of deserters and looters roamed. Military checkpoints demanded documentation.

**Emotional Impact**: Families were separated en route. Communications broke down. Many did not know whether loved ones had survived until reaching Rostov.

---

## Arrival in Rostov-on-Don

**1915**: The first waves of evacuees began arriving in Rostov-on-Don. The city, though overwhelmed with refugees, had infrastructure that Lubavitch lacked:

- **Housing**: Temporary accommodations were found — often overcrowded apartments
- **Synagogue**: Existing Jewish communities shared space
- **Yeshiva**: Tomchei Temimim attempted to reestablish operations, though in cramped conditions

The Rashab established a new court (_chatzer_) in Rostov, but it could not fully replicate what had been lost. The physical, social, and spiritual community of Lubavitch had been shattered.

---

## The Rashab's Leadership During Crisis

Throughout the evacuation and resettlement, the Rashab provided leadership that balanced spiritual authority with practical compassion:

**Spiritual Guidance**: He delivered discourses and provided Torah teachings despite the upheaval. He framed the situation as a test of faith, not merely a political catastrophe.

**Material Support**: The Rashab distributed charity from his own limited resources to support families who had lost everything.

**Decision-Making**: Hard choices had to be made continuously — who received housing, how funds were allocated, whether individuals should return to Lubavitch. The Rashab made these decisively.

**Presence**: Simply being present — the Rebbe was accessible to his chassidim even in displacement — provided enormous comfort.

---

## The Fate of Lubavitch

After the evacuation, Lubavitch's fate was sealed:

**1915–1917**: The town was effectively abandoned by its Chabad community. Buildings fell into disrepair.

**1917–1921**: Following the Russian Revolution, Bolshevik authorities took control. Religious institutions were closed. Tomchei Temimim went underground.

**1921**: The physical compound of the Rebbe's court in Lubavitch was burned — likely by Bolshevik or revolutionary forces, though the exact circumstances are unclear. The building that had housed generations of Rebbes was destroyed.

**1941**: In November 1941, during the Nazi occupation, all **483 remaining Jews** of Lubavitch were murdered. The town that had been Chabad's world center became a site of Holocaust annihilation.

---

## Legacy: Displacement as Permanent Condition

The 1915 evacuation marked a before-and-after in Chabad history:

**Before 1915**: Chabad was rooted in a specific place — Lubavitch. The Rebbe's court was fixed. Pilgrims came to the Rebbe.

**After 1915**: Chabad became perpetually mobile. The Rebbe's court moved repeatedly:

- Rostov-on-Don (1915–1920)
- Rostov under the Rayatz (1920–1924)
- Leningrad (1924–1927)
- Riga, Latvia (1927–1933)
- Warsaw, Poland (1933–1939)
- Otwock, Poland (1939–1940)
- Brooklyn, New York (1940–1994)
- Crown Heights as permanent center (post-1950)

This displacement trained Chabad in adaptability. The movement learned to function without a fixed geographic center, to operate across borders, and to maintain communal cohesion despite physical dispersion. These lessons proved essential during the Soviet persecution, the Holocaust, and the postwar global diaspora.
