# Chabad in Poland Between the Wars (1918–1939)

**The growth and development of Chabad institutions in independent Poland, culminating in the Rayatz's residence in Warsaw (1933–1939)**

## Background

Following World War I and the establishment of independent Poland (1918), the Jewish community of Poland — approximately 3.5 million people, the largest Jewish population in Europe — entered a period of intense cultural and religious activity. Chabad, while a minority within Polish Jewry, built an institutional presence during this period.

## Key Institutions

### Tomchei Temimim Branches

Multiple Tomchei Temimim branches operated in interwar Poland:
- **Otwock** — major branch near Warsaw
- **Warsaw** — central branch, especially after 1933
- **Lodz** — branch in Poland's second city
- **Chelm** — southeastern Poland branch
- **Vilna** — branch in the culturally significant city (then in Poland)
- **Zambin/Zembin** — White Russian branch under Polish rule

### Key Figures

**Educators and Rabbis:**
- R' Yitzchak Eber — rosh yeshiva in the Warsaw region
- R' Moshe Chefetz — mashpia in Riga and Polish branches
- R' Eliyahu Chaim Althaus — mashpia in Riga
- R' Shmaryahu Gurary — son-in-law of the Rayatz, active in Poland
- R' Alter Shimchovitch, R' Yosef Blumenfeld, R' Avraham Michel Lis — Warsaw Chassidim

## The Rayatz in Warsaw (1933–1939)

After leaving Riga (1933), the Rayatz settled in Warsaw. His presence transformed Warsaw into the de facto Chabad center for Eastern Europe outside the Soviet Union. Warsaw's Chabad institutions — Tomchei Temimim, Kehot publishing (nascent), and the Rebbe's court — attracted Chassidim from throughout Poland, Lithuania, and the diaspora.

## Destruction

The German invasion of September 1939 ended the Polish Chabad community. The Rayatz escaped in November 1939 (see [[events/escape-from-warsaw-1939]]). Most of the Polish Chabad Chassidim perished in the Holocaust; some escaped eastward into the Soviet Union.

## Significance

The interwar Polish period represents Chabad's most significant institutional presence in Poland. The combination of a major Jewish population, relative political freedom (compared to Soviet Russia), and the Rayatz's direct leadership created conditions for substantial growth. The destruction of this community in the Holocaust was one of the most devastating losses in Chabad history.
