# Rayatz's First Visit to America (1929–1930)

**Date**: 13 Elul 5689 – various dates through 5690 (September 1929 – 1930)
**Location**: United States, primarily New York; also visited Eretz Yisrael en route

## Overview

The Rayatz's extended visit to America in 5689–5690 (1929–1930) was his first encounter with American Jewry — a community that would become, a decade later, his permanent home and the new center of world Jewry. The primary stated purpose was to advocate for Soviet Jewry and raise funds for Chabad institutions, but the visit also served as a crucial first-hand assessment of American Jewish life and its potential.

## Background and Purpose

In Shevat 5689 (January 1929), R' Yisrael Jacobson received a letter from the Rayatz's secretary R' Yechezkel Feigin informing him that the Rayatz was planning to visit America in the summer. The visit was motivated primarily by the crisis of Soviet Jewry — the Rayatz had devoted himself completely to maintaining Jewish life under Bolshevik persecution, and needed American financial support and political advocacy to continue this work.

R' Yisrael Jacobson organized preparations on the American side, including sending out invitation letters from Chabad communities and synagogues, raising funds for the visit's expenses, and arranging accommodations. Attorney Yekutiel Kremer secured $1,000 for the preparations — described as an exceptional sum at the time. A new office was rented for Agudas Chassidei Chabad on Henry Street on the Lower East Side to serve as headquarters for organizing the visit.

## Arrival: September 18, 1929

The Rayatz arrived at the South Ferry dock in New York on **Wednesday, 13 Elul 5689 (September 18, 1929)**. A delegation of communal leaders traveled out on a government cutter to meet the ship before it docked — made possible through the influence of attorney Yekutiel Kremer, who had connections in government circles.

Those on the reception delegation included R' Mordechai Menachem Lokshin, attorney Yekutiel Kremer and his brother R' Chaim Zalman Kremer (chairman of Agudas Chassidei Chabad), Judge Philip Kleinfeld, and R' Shmuel Elyezer Nelson.

A large crowd had waited at the dock, but the day was rainy and many left before the ship arrived in the late afternoon. Police motorcycles escorted the entire motorcade through the city.

The Rayatz's first public words to the assembled crowd at the dock were: **"Sadna de-ar'a chad hu"** ("The earth is one entity") — a Talmudic phrase suggesting that the same divine service applies everywhere, even in America.

## Initial Accommodations

The Rayatz initially stayed at **R' Eliya Simpson's home in Boro Park, Brooklyn**, where there was a synagogue on the lower floor. R' Eliya Simpson had wanted the Rayatz to inaugurate his new home, but it quickly became clear this was not suitable for the Rayatz's needs and standards. The Rayatz expressed dissatisfaction, and alternative accommodations were arranged.

The Rayatz then moved to a rented house at **184 Brooklyn Avenue** (in Crown Heights), paying $1,800 in rent — paid by R' Gavriel Dvorkin. Staff was hired, including a young man from Atlantic City experienced in hotel service, named Levi Yitzchak Eizer.

## The Shabbat After Arrival

On Shabbat Parshat Tavo (the Shabbat after arrival), the Rayatz said the Chassidic discourse "Hashkifa" — subsequently published.

## Personal Reception and Key Relationships

In the second week of the visit, the Rayatz told Jacobson: "Yisrael, I think with you one can talk as in the old home (_alte heim_)." This established a close personal relationship between the two that would define their work together for the next two decades.

The visit involved almost daily visits from Chabad followers, prominent Orthodox figures, and leaders of the broader Jewish community, who came for private audiences (_yechidus_). Before Rosh Hashana, approximately **30 Temimim** came for yechidus — the assembled leadership of the American Chabad community at that point. These included R' Eliya Simpson, R' Gershon Simpson, R' Avraham Eliya Axelrod of Baltimore, and others.

## The Rayatz's Rebuke on Dress

When the Rayatz's secretary R' Yechezkel Feigin mentioned to him that the American Temimim were wearing shirts and neckties, the Rayatz replied that in America wearing such clothes is normal and not a betrayal — they should not stand out unnecessarily. This was a characteristic adjustment: the Rayatz distinguished between the demands of the Russian environment and American realities.

## Assessment of American Jewry

The Rayatz observed American Jewish life with what Jacobson described as a combination of concern and hope. The Chabad community was tiny and disorganized; most Jews had little connection to traditional observance. The Rayatz used the phrase "chassidei hanosach vehagazah" — "Chassidim of the style and lineage" (rather than authentic practitioners) — to describe many who identified as Chabad in America. He noted that the passage to America had weakened most immigrants' religiosity.

Yet he also saw potential: American freedom permitted religious life; there were resources; and even the "chassidei hanosach" retained some feeling for Lubavitch that could be reawakened.

## Travels and Activities

During the visit, the Rayatz traveled to various American cities. Between trips he would return to New York; sometimes he stayed at the Brooklyn Avenue house, sometimes at a hotel in Manhattan.

## The Maamad

One of the urgent goals of the visit was reestablishing the maamad (personal support fund) on a stable basis. R' Moshe DovBer Rivkin chaired a committee for this purpose. Despite the small and struggling community, a fund-raising gathering was organized at which approximately 12 Temimim pledged at least $100 each (R' Gershon Simpson pledged $150).

## Significance

The visit marked the beginning of a direct relationship between the Rayatz and American Jewry that would eventually result in his permanent immigration in 1940. The logistical, financial, and organizational challenges Jacobson and others faced in hosting the visit — described in great detail in Jacobson's memoir — reveal how thin the American Chabad infrastructure was in 1929 and how much needed to be built before the Rayatz's permanent arrival a decade later.
