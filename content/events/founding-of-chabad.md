# The Founding of Chabad (1772–1796)

**Period**: 1772–1796  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi (Alter Rebbe)]], [[entities/people/maggid-of-mezeritch|Maggid of Mezeritch]], [[entities/people/baal-shem-tov|Baal Shem Tov]]  
**Key Date**: 1796 — publication of the _Tanya_

---

## Overview

Chabad-Lubavitch emerged as a distinct school within the broader Chassidic movement during the quarter-century between the death of the [[entities/people/maggid-of-mezeritch|Maggid of Mezeritch]] (1772) and the publication of the _Tanya_ (1796). Founded by [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi]] — known as the Alter Rebbe — Chabad represented a unique synthesis: the mystical fervor of Chassidism fused with rigorous intellectual discipline. The name **Chabad** is an acronym for _Chochmah, Binah, Da'at_ (Wisdom, Understanding, Knowledge), reflecting the movement's emphasis on contemplative worship through the mind.

---

## Background: The Chassidic Movement

The [[entities/people/baal-shem-tov|Baal Shem Tov]] (c. 1698–1760) had launched the Chassidic revolution in the mid-eighteenth century, emphasizing joy in worship, divine immanence in all creation, and the spiritual capacity of every Jew — not only scholars. After his death, leadership passed to his primary disciple, [[entities/people/maggid-of-mezeritch|Rabbi Dov Ber of Mezeritch]] (the Maggid), who systematized Chassidic thought and trained the next generation of leaders.

Among the Maggid's inner circle, the young Schneur Zalman of Liadi stood out. Born in 1745 in Liozna (modern Belarus), he arrived at Mezeritch at approximately age twenty. The Maggid recognized his extraordinary combination of Talmudic mastery and mystical aptitude, assigning him to compose an updated _Shulchan Aruch_ (code of Jewish law) — a task that would eventually produce the _Shulchan Aruch HaRav_.

---

## The Maggid's Death and the Dispersal (1772)

When the Maggid died on 19 Kislev 5533 (December 1772), the Chassidic movement faced a crisis of succession. Unlike the Baal Shem Tov, the Maggid had trained dozens of disciples, each of whom established independent Chassidic courts across Eastern Europe. The movement fragmented geographically:

- **Rebbe Elimelech of Lizhensk** → Galicia
- **Rebbe Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev** → Ukraine
- **Rebbe Menachem Mendel of Vitebsk** → initially Lithuania, then Eretz Yisrael (1777)
- **Rebbe Schneur Zalman of Liadi** → Lithuania, Belarus, and Russia

Rabbi Schneur Zalman initially deferred to [[entities/people/menachem-mendel-of-vitebsk|Rebbe Menachem Mendel of Vitebsk]], who was the senior disciple. When Menachem Mendel emigrated to the Land of Israel in 1777, he appointed Schneur Zalman as the Chassidic leader for the communities of Lithuania and White Russia. This was the effective beginning of what would become the Chabad movement.

---

## The Alter Rebbe's Emergence (1772–1788)

Throughout the 1770s and 1780s, the Alter Rebbe developed his distinctive approach:

### Intellectual Chassidism

Where other Chassidic masters emphasized emotion, story, miracle, and the tzaddik's intercession, the Alter Rebbe insisted that **contemplation** (_hisbonenus_) was the primary path to divine service. A Jew should not merely feel love and fear of God; he should _understand_ the intellectual basis for those emotions through deep meditation on the divine structure of reality.

### The Liozna Period

Based in Liozna, the Alter Rebbe attracted a growing circle of followers. He delivered oral discourses (_maamarim_) that were recorded by his students. He held _yechidus_ (private audiences) where he guided individuals in their spiritual development — a practice that became central to the Rebbe-Chassid relationship in Chabad.

### Recognition as Leader

By approximately 1788, the Alter Rebbe was widely recognized as the undisputed Chassidic leader for the northern territories. His following numbered in the thousands, concentrated in Belarus, Lithuania, and parts of Russia.

---

## The Mitnagdic Opposition

The Alter Rebbe's rise occurred against the backdrop of fierce opposition from the _Mitnagdim_ — traditional Talmudic scholars who viewed Chassidism as a dangerous innovation. The primary opponent was the [[entities/people/vilna-gaon|Vilna Gaon]] (Rabbi Eliyahu of Vilna, 1720–1797), who issued bans (_cherem_) against the Chassidim in 1772 and again in 1781.

The disputes centered on several issues:

- **Changes to prayer liturgy**: the Chassidim adopted the Sephardic _nusach_ (Nusach Ari)
- **Altered prayer times**: Chassidim delayed morning prayers for contemplative preparation
- **Authority of the tzaddik**: Mitnagdim saw this as undermining traditional rabbinic authority
- **Slaughter practices**: disputes over the Chassidic method of sharpening knives for ritual slaughter

The Alter Rebbe made repeated attempts to reconcile with the Mitnagdim, including a famous (unsuccessful) journey to meet the Vilna Gaon. Despite the Gaon's refusal to meet, the Alter Rebbe maintained a policy of respect toward Mitnagdic scholars — a stance that became characteristic of Chabad.

## The Tanya: The Founding Document (1796)

The culmination of the founding period was the publication of the _Tanya_ — formally titled _Likkutei Amarim_ ("Collected Sayings") — in 1796. This was not merely a book; it was the charter document of a new school of Jewish thought.

### Genesis of the Tanya

The Alter Rebbe had been delivering individual spiritual guidance (_yechidus_) to thousands of followers. He found that many of the same questions recurred — questions about the nature of the soul, the struggle between good and evil, the meaning of divine service. The _Tanya_ was composed to serve as a "written yechidus" — a systematic guide that could address these common spiritual challenges.

The work was refined over twenty years before publication. Unauthorized copies circulated widely, which finally compelled the Alter Rebbe to publish an authoritative edition.

### Structure and Content

The _Tanya_ is divided into five parts:

1. **Likkutei Amarim** (Book of the Average Person) — 53 chapters on the inner spiritual life, the two souls (divine and animal), and the path of the _beinoni_ (intermediate person)
2. **Shaar HaYichud VeHaEmunah** (Gate of Unity and Faith) — 12 chapters on the nature of divine unity and continuous creation
3. **Iggeret HaTeshuvah** (Epistle of Repentance) — 12 chapters on the process of return and spiritual repair
4. **Iggeret HaKodesh** (Holy Epistle) — 32 letters on various aspects of divine service and communal responsibility
5. **Kuntres Acharon** (Last Treatise) — supplementary essays resolving apparent contradictions in Kabbalistic sources

### Revolutionary Ideas

The _Tanya_ introduced several ideas that defined Chabad:

- **The Beinoni**: The spiritual ideal is not the tzaddik (who has conquered evil) but the _beinoni_ — the ordinary Jew who constantly struggles and never sins in action, despite inner temptation. This democratized spiritual aspiration.
- **Two Souls**: Every Jew possesses a _nefesh ha'elokit_ (divine soul) and a _nefesh habehamit_ (animal soul). Life is the arena of their interaction.
- **Contemplative Worship**: Through _hisbonenus_ — sustained meditation on the divine — a person generates authentic love and fear of God.
- **Continuous Creation**: God continuously recreates the world from absolute nothingness at every moment. Existence is not a one-time event but an ongoing divine act.

### Impact

The _Tanya_ was reprinted dozens of times during the Alter Rebbe's lifetime and has been published in over 7,000 editions since. It became the foundational text studied in all Chabad communities — referred to as "the Written Torah of Chassidus" alongside the Chassidic discourses that serve as its "Oral Torah."

## The Spread of Chabad

Between 1772 and 1796, Chabad grew from a small circle in Liozna to a major movement spanning Belarus, Lithuania, and parts of Russia. Key factors:

- **Systematic teaching**: Unlike other Chassidic courts, Chabad produced written texts that could be studied independently
- **Intellectual appeal**: The Alter Rebbe attracted Talmudic scholars who found the intellectual rigor of Chabad compatible with their training
- **Organizational structure**: The Alter Rebbe appointed local representatives (_mashpi'im_) who could teach Chassidus in communities far from his court
- **Correspondence**: The Alter Rebbe maintained extensive written correspondence with followers — later collected in _Iggeret HaKodesh_

## Legacy

The founding period established the defining characteristics of Chabad that persist to the present day:

- **Intellectual depth** as the path to emotional and spiritual vitality
- **Written texts** as the vehicle of transmission (unlike the oral tradition of other Chassidic courts)
- **Universalism** — every Jew, not only the spiritual elite, is capable of genuine divine service
- **Engagement with opponents** — respect and dialogue rather than isolation

The _Tanya_ remains the daily study text in Chabad communities worldwide, and the Alter Rebbe's liberation on 19 Kislev 1798 — the anniversary of the Maggid's passing — became the movement's founding holiday.
