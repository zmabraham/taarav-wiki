# פליטי חב"ד בערי בוכרה בתקופת המלחמה

## Chabad Chassidim as Refugees in Bukharan Jewish Communities (1941–1945)

**Period**: 1941–1945  
**Location**: [[entities/places/samarkand|Samarkand]] and other cities in Soviet Central Asia ([[entities/places/bukhara|Bukhara / Uzbekistan]])  
**Significance**: Encounter between Ashkenazic Chabad culture and ancient Bukharan Jewish civilization; creation of lasting bonds

---

## Overview

When Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, hundreds of Chabad chassidim fled the advancing front eastward into Soviet Central Asia. The cities of [[entities/places/samarkand|Samarkand]], Bukhara, Tashkent, and other Uzbek centers became crucial refuges — and there, the Chabad refugees encountered one of the most ancient and distinctive Jewish communities in the world: the **Bukharan Jews**.

This encounter between Ashkenazic Chabad chassidim and Bukharan Jews — who spoke Bukhori (Judeo-Tajik), maintained their own distinctive customs, and had been largely separate from mainstream Ashkenazic developments for over a millennium — is one of the most unusual episodes of inter-Jewish cultural encounter in modern history.

---

## The Bukharan Jewish Community

Bukharan Jews traced their presence in Central Asia to ancient times, some claiming roots from the Babylonian exile. They maintained:

- **Bukhori language** (Judeo-Tajik/Judeo-Persian)
- Distinctive **Sephardic-influenced liturgy and customs**
- Traditional Jewish practice largely separate from Ashkenazic developments
- Strong community structures in cities like Samarkand, Bukhara, and Tashkent

---

## The Encounter

The arrival of thousands of Ashkenazic Jewish refugees — many of them Chabad chassidim with their distinctive Chassidic practices, customs, and culture — in Bukharan Jewish communities created an unprecedented intercultural moment:

**Religious exchange**: Chabad chassidim and Bukharan Jews prayed in the same cities, sometimes the same synagogues. Each exposed the other to their traditions.

**Musical influence**: Bukharan Jewish music — with its distinctive modal style and Persian influences — made a lasting impression on Chabad refugees. Some Bukharan melodies entered the Chabad repertoire.

**Communal support**: Bukharan Jewish families hosted and helped Chabad refugees, sharing scarce food and resources during the wartime period.

**Lasting bonds**: Relationships formed during these years created connections between the two communities that persisted after the war.

---

## Documentary Sources

This period is documented in:

- The memoir _Zichronosai_ (Yisrael Yehuda Levi) — who spent the war in [[entities/places/samarkand|Samarkand]]
- The memoir _Lemaan Yede'u Banim Yivaledu_ (M. Shusterman) — who was in Samarkand during the war
- The _Chabad Bukhara_ volume (_Toldot Chabad BeYahadut Bukhara_) — which devotes a section to this period: "Erei Miklat beTekufat haMilchama" (Cities of Refuge During the War)

---

## Aftermath

After the war, many Chabad refugees used forged Polish documents to escape the Soviet Union through the [[events/great-escape-1946|Great Escape (1946–47)]]. The Bukharan Jewish community eventually emigrated en masse — primarily to Israel and New York — where they encountered Chabad institutions and the bonds of the wartime period could be renewed in freedom.
