# The Shpalerka Prison Account — The Rayatz's Imprisonment (1927)

**Date**: 15 Sivan – 12 Tammuz 5687 (June – July 1927)  
**Location**: Shpalerka Prison, Leningrad, Soviet Russia  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], Nachmanson (interrogator), GPU officials

---

## Overview

Following his [[events/rayatz-arrest-shavuot-1927|arrest on 15 Sivan 5687]], the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] was imprisoned in **Shpalerka prison** in Leningrad for approximately three weeks. The prison — a notorious facility used for political prisoners — became the stage for one of the most extraordinary confrontations in modern Jewish history: a Rebbe of Chabad facing interrogation, sentencing, and the threat of execution with unwavering faith. This page details the prison conditions, the interrogations, and the Rayatz's responses that became part of Chabad tradition.

---

## Shpalerka Prison: The Facility

**Location**: Central Leningrad (St. Petersburg)
**Function**: A prison used primarily for political prisoners and those accused of "counter-revolutionary" crimes
**Reputation**: Known for harsh conditions, cold cells, and brutal interrogations

The prison was part of the GPU (State Political Directorate) system — the Soviet secret police apparatus. Prisoners in Shpalerka were those the state considered particularly dangerous or ideologically threatening.

---

## Cell Conditions

The Rayatz was placed in a **solitary confinement cell** — standard for political prisoners. The conditions were deliberately harsh:

**Physical Conditions**:

- **Cold**: The cell was inadequately heated, even in the summer nights of Leningrad
- **Damp**: Leningrad's climate meant pervasive dampness that penetrated clothing and bedding
- **Small**: The cell was cramped, with minimal space to move
- **Light**: Limited natural light; artificial lighting was harsh and always on
- **Furnishings**: A wooden platform or bed (often just boards), possibly a table and stool, nothing more

**Sanitation**:

- Access to washing facilities was limited
- Prison meals were inadequate — often inedible or intentionally unpleasant
- The cell likely had a bucket for waste, emptied irregularly

**Isolation**:

- Solitary confinement meant no contact with other prisoners
- Limited contact with guards — mostly silent delivery of food or transport to interrogations
- Complete isolation from the outside world — no news, no correspondence, no way to know what was happening

These conditions were designed to break prisoners physically and psychologically. The combination of cold, hunger, isolation, and uncertainty was often sufficient to force confessions.

---

## The Interrogations

### The Interrogator: Nachmanson

The **chief interrogator** was a man named **Nachmanson** — a Jewish Communist working for the GPU. The irony was profound: a Jew, born into a tradition that the Rayatz represented, now tasked with interrogating a Rebbe for the "crime" of keeping Judaism alive.

Nachmanson's background is not fully documented, but his role is clear: he was the point person in breaking the Rayatz, extracting a confession, and preparing the case for trial and sentencing.

### Interrogation Techniques

The GPU used standard interrogation techniques:

**Psychological Pressure**:

- Emphasizing the hopelessness of the situation
- Threatening execution
- Promising leniency in exchange for cooperation
- Attempting to create doubt about divine protection

**Physical Deprivation**:

- Using hunger, cold, and sleep deprivation to weaken resistance
- Keeping the prisoner in uncomfortable positions for extended periods

**Mental Manipulation**:

- Arguing that the Rebbe's religious activities harmed the Jewish people
- Claiming that religious leaders exploited their followers
- Suggesting that other activists had already confessed and implicated him

### The Rayatz's Responses

The Rayatz's responses to interrogation became legendary:

**"I Will Not Change My Ways"**

When pressed to confess, to promise to cease religious activity, or to betray his community, the Rayatz's response was consistent:

**"I will not change my ways."**

This simple statement — repeated through hours and days of interrogation — conveyed a profound truth: the Soviet state could imprison his body, but could not touch his soul or his mission.

**Theological Framing**

The Rayatz framed his situation in explicitly religious terms:

- He was a prisoner of God, not merely of the GPU
- His suffering had meaning within the context of Jewish history
- His refusal to compromise was an obligation to Torah, not personal stubbornness

**Defending His Community**

Rather than save himself by betraying others, the Rayatz defended his community:

- He explained that his work was educational, not political
- He argued that supporting Jewish education was a religious obligation, not counter-revolution
- He refused to provide names of other activists, despite pressure

---

## The Sentencing

### Initial Sentence: Death

After days of interrogation, the Rayatz was sentenced to **death by firing squad**. This was the standard GPU sentence for "counter-revolutionary" activity.

The sentence reflected:

- The Soviet state's determination to eliminate religious leadership
- The seriousness with which they viewed the underground Chabad network
- The expectation that a death sentence would terrify other religious activists

### First Commutation: Solovki

Following initial interventions — the details of which involved behind-the-scenes diplomatic pressure — the death sentence was commuted to **ten years in the Solovki labor camp**.

**Solovki**: The **Solovki prison camp** on the Solovetsky Islands in the White Sea was one of the most brutal locations in the Soviet gulag system. Prisoners faced:

- Arctic conditions
- Forced labor in quarries and forests
- Inadequate food and clothing
- High mortality rates

For a man of the Rayatz's age (46 in 1927) and health, a ten-year Solovki sentence was effectively a death sentence.

### Second Commutation: Exile

Additional pressure — including the [[events/rescue-campaign-1927|international campaign]] then mobilizing — resulted in a further commutation: **three years of internal exile in Kostroma**.

**Kostroma**: A city northeast of Moscow, exile there meant:

- Separation from the community
- Restriction of movement
- Constant surveillance
- But no forced labor and possibility of survival

This commutation — from death, to Solovki, to Kostroma — reflected the Soviet government's calculations. International attention was making the Rayatz's case diplomatically inconvenient. It was easier to reduce the sentence than to risk creating a martyr.

## Spiritual Practices in Prison

Despite the conditions, the Rayatz maintained religious observance:

**Prayer**:

- He prayed three times daily, even without prayer books
- He may have recited Psalms (_Tehillim_) from memory
- He maintained faith despite circumstances that would break most people

**Study**:

- Without books, he reviewed Torah teachings from memory
- He may have composed or reviewed Chassidic discourses mentally
- He treated the prison as a place for spiritual work, not merely physical confinement

**Trust in Divine Providence**:

- The Rayatz understood his situation as part of a larger divine plan
- He did not despair, even when sentenced to death
- His faith was not naive but grounded in generations of Chassidic teaching about facing persecution

---

## International Attention

Even while in Shpalerka, international attention was building:

**News Spread**: Word of the arrest reached Jewish communities worldwide through telegram, newspaper, and personal networks.

**Protests**: Jewish organizations began protesting to Soviet embassies and consulates.

**Diplomatic Pressure**: Several governments, including Latvia (where the Rayatz had citizenship through his residence in Riga), made representations to Moscow.

The Soviet government — though authoritarian and ruthless — was sensitive to international opinion during this period. The Rayatz's case was becoming a public relations liability.

---

## Liberation from Prison

**12 Tammuz 5687 (July 12, 1927)**: The Rayatz was informed that his sentence had been commuted and he was being released from prison.

**From Death to Freedom**:
In less than one month, the Rayatz had moved from:

- Arrest → Death sentence → Solovki → Kostroma → Liberation

This extraordinary reversal was understood within Chabad as miraculous — a modern example of divine intervention, comparable to the liberation of the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] on 19 Kislev 1798.

**Transfer to Exile**:
Initially, the Rayatz was told he was being sent to Kostroma for three years of exile. However, continued international pressure eventually resulted in full liberation and permission to leave the Soviet Union.

See [[events/rayatz-liberation-1927|Liberation and Expulsion (1927)]], [[events/rayatz-in-malakhov|The Rayatz in Malakhov]].

## Legacy: The Shpalerka Account

The Rayatz's account of his imprisonment — later published in various forms — became part of Chabad's collective memory:

**Model of Mesirus Nefesh**:
The Shpalerka experience became the paradigm of _mesirus nefesh_ (self-sacrifice) for subsequent generations. When Chabad shluchim (emissaries) face difficulties, they reference the Rayatz in Shpalerka.

**Source of Strength**:
Knowing that the Rebbe faced interrogation and death threats without breaking gives chassidim strength in their own challenges.

**Historical Documentation**:
The Rayatz's writings about his imprisonment provide historical documentation of Soviet persecution of religious leaders.

**Spiritual Teaching**:
Beyond historical narrative, the Shpalerka account contains spiritual teachings about faith under persecution, the nature of divine providence, and the meaning of suffering.
