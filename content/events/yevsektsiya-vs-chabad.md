# Yevsektsiya vs. Chabad — The War on Underground Judaism (1918–1927)

**Date**: 1918–1927 (primary period of active conflict)  
**Location**: Soviet Union (Russia, Ukraine, Belarus)  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], Yevsektsiya operatives (named below), GPU agents

---

## Overview

The **Yevsektsiya** (יעווסעקציע — _Yevreiskaya Sektsiya_, Jewish Section of the Communist Party) was the Soviet state's specialized weapon against Jewish religious life. Composed primarily of Jewish Communists who had abandoned tradition, the Yevsektsiya targeted Chabad with particular ferocity — understanding that as long as the Tomchei Temimim underground network functioned, Judaism would survive in the Soviet Union. This page details the specific tactics, named operatives, and documented incidents of the Yevsektsiya's war on Chabad.

---

## What Was the Yevsektsiya?

Established in **1918** as a section of the Communist Party's agitprop (agitation and propaganda) apparatus, the Yevsektsiya had a dual mission:

1. **Radicalize Jewish workers** by reaching them in Yiddish with Communist ideology
2. **Destroy traditional Jewish institutions** — synagogues, schools, communal organizations

The Yevsektsiya was staffed largely by **Jewish Communists** who often harbored particular hostility toward the traditional world they had left. They understood Jewish community structure from the inside and knew exactly how to dismantle it.

**Funding and Support**: The Yevsektsiya operated with full state backing — funding from the Communist Party, enforcement power through the GPU (secret police), and ideological legitimacy as part of the revolutionary project.

**Disbandment**: In **1930**, Stalin ordered the Yevsektsiya dissolved. By that time, its work was largely done — Jewish religious life had been driven underground. The Yevsektsiya's own members would later become victims of Stalinist purges.

## Key Yevsektsiya Operatives

Historical records and Chabad memoirs preserve the names of several Yevsektsiya operatives who targeted Chabad communities:

### Dimansky

**Role**: Yevsektsiya operative active in **Leningrad** during the early 1920s.

**Activities**: Dimansky was involved in the campaign to close Chabad synagogues and institutions in Leningrad. He participated in raids on underground yeshivos and was present during the surveillance of the Rayatz that preceded his 1927 arrest.

**Methods**: Known for aggressive interrogation tactics and pressure tactics on community members to inform on each other.

### A. Litvak

**Role**: Yevsektsiya official in **Ukraine**.

**Activities**: Litvak coordinated the closure of Chabad institutions in Ukrainian cities including Kiev, Kharkov, and Odessa. He was particularly focused on dismantling the underground Tomchei Temimim network that operated through shifting locations in these cities.

### Nachmanson

**Role**: Yevsektsiya interrogator involved in the **Rayatz's 1927 arrest**.

**Activities**: Nachmanson, a Jewish Communist, personally interrogated the Rayatz during his imprisonment in **Shpalerka prison** in Leningrad. The irony of a Jew interrogating a Rebbe for the "crime" of promoting Judaism was not lost on either party.

See [[events/shpalerka-prison-account|The Shpalerka Prison Account]].

### Unnamed Operatives

Memoirs of the Soviet underground preserve references to numerous other Yevsektsiya operatives, many known only by their function or position. These included:

- Local Yevsektsiya chairmen in cities with major Chabad populations
- "Cultural officials" tasked with closing religious schools
- GPU liaison officers who worked with Yevsektsiya to identify targets

## Tactics Against Chabad

The Yevsektsiya employed a range of tactics designed to destroy Chabad's capacity to function:

### 1. Show Trials

Public trials designed to humiliate religious leaders and discourage religious practice:

**The 1920s Trials**: Chabad activists, including teachers and mashpi'im (spiritual mentors), were put on trial for "counter-revolutionary activities." The charges were always the same:

- Operating illegal religious schools
- Collecting funds for religious institutions
- Maintaining contact with foreign Jewish leaders
- "Obscurantism" — promoting religious "superstition"

These trials were scripted spectacles with predetermined verdicts. The purpose was propaganda — demonstrating that even Jews condemned their religious leaders.

### 2. Forced Closures of Synagogues

The Yevsektsiya systematically closed Chabad synagogues:

**Leningrad (1924–1927)**: Multiple Chabad synagogues were shut down using pretexts:

- "Sanitary violations"
- "Building code violations"
- "Noise complaints"
- "Public order concerns"

Once closed, buildings were often converted to secular uses — workers' clubs, warehouses, even stables. Sacred objects, Torah scrolls, and books were confiscated or destroyed.

**Kiev, Kharkov, Odessa**: Similar campaigns in major Ukrainian cities with significant Chabad populations.

### 3. School Raids

The **Tomchei Temimim underground network** was a primary target:

**Raids**: GPU agents, acting on Yevsektsiya intelligence, raided apartments where underground yeshivos were operating. Students and teachers were arrested, books were confiscated, and the locations were permanently compromised.

**Informants**: The Yevsektsiya recruited informants within the Jewish community — sometimes through blackmail, sometimes through genuine ideological conversion to Communism. These informants identified underground activities.

### 4. Pressure on Families

The Yevsektsiya understood that Chabad activists would risk their own lives but might break under pressure to their families:

**Hostage-Taking by Another Name**: Family members of known activists were threatened with loss of housing, employment, or education opportunities unless the activist ceased religious activities.

**Employment Blacklists**: Known chassidim were placed on employment blacklists, making it nearly impossible to find legal work. This created pressure to compromise or leave the country.

## The Cat-and-Mouse Game (1920–1927)

The period between the Rashab's death (1920) and the Rayatz's arrest (1927) was characterized by a continuous struggle between the Yevsektsiya and Chabad's underground network.

### Chabad's Defensive Strategies

**Constant Movement**: Underground yeshivos relocated frequently — never staying in one location for more than a few months. This made it difficult for the Yevsektsiya to maintain surveillance.

**Coded Communication**: The Rayatz and his network communicated in code. Letters used innocuous business language to convey information about educational activities, funds, and personnel.

**Women as Activists**: The Yevsektsiya was less likely to suspect women of clandestine activity. Chabad women served as teachers, messengers, and organizers — moving between cities with less scrutiny.

**Legal and Semi-Legal Activities**: Some Chabad institutions maintained nominal compliance with Soviet law while continuing religious function underground.

### The Yevsektsiya's Countermeasures

**Surveillance**: GPU agents followed the Rayatz and other known activists, documenting their movements and contacts.

**Infiltration**: The Yevsektsiya attempted to place agents within Chabad communities to gather intelligence.

**Arrests of Key Figures**: By arresting teachers, mashpi'im, and community leaders, the Yevsektsiya aimed to decapitate the movement.

## Documented Incidents

### The Leningrad Synagogue Closures (1924–1926)

Under Yevsektsiya pressure, Leningrad authorities closed multiple synagogues:

**The Bolshoy Prospect Synagogue**: A major Chabad synagogue, closed using the pretext of "structural unsoundness." The building was converted to a workers' club.

**The Second Synagogue**: Closed for "violating noise ordinances" after complaints orchestrated by the Yevsektsiya.

These closures forced religious life into private apartments — smaller, harder to maintain, constantly at risk of discovery.

### The Kiev Underground Raids (1925–1926)

In Kiev, the Yevsektsiya conducted a series of raids on underground Chabad activities:

**Raid on a Private Home**: GPU agents burst into a private apartment where approximately 15 students were studying. All were arrested. The teacher — a Tomchei Temimim graduate — was sentenced to three years in the Solovki labor camp, where he died.

**Confiscation of Sacred Texts**: Torah scrolls, Talmuds, and Chassidic books were seized during raids. Many were burned publicly as "counter-revolutionary literature."

### The Kharkov Show Trial (1926)

Several Chabad activists were put on public trial in Kharkov:

**Charges**: "Operating illegal religious schools" and "collecting funds for religious purposes."

**Verdict**: All defendants were found guilty and sentenced to exile in Siberia. The trial was widely covered in the Soviet Yiddish press as a warning to other religious communities.

## The Climax: 1927

The Yevsektsiya's campaign against Chabad culminated in the **1927 arrest of the Rayatz** — an attempt to decapitate the movement by imprisoning its leader.

**Preparation**: The Yevsektsiya spent years gathering evidence, building a case, and preparing for the arrest. They documented the Rayatz's underground activities, his correspondence with foreign Jewish leaders, and his role in funding underground education.

**Arrest**: On **15 Sivan 5687 (June 15, 1927)**, GPU agents arrested the Rayatz at his home in Leningrad.

**Sentencing**: The Rayatz was initially sentenced to death, then commuted to Solovki, then commuted again to exile in Kostroma.

**International Pressure**: The arrest provoked international outrage. Jewish communities worldwide protested. The Latvian government, through [[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Mordechai Dubin]], applied diplomatic pressure.

**Liberation**: On **12-13 Tammuz 5687 (July 12-13, 1927)**, the Rayatz was released and expelled from the Soviet Union.

See [[events/rayatz-arrest-1927|Arrest and Liberation of the Rayatz (1927)]], [[events/shpalerka-prison-account|The Shpalerka Prison Account]], [[events/rescue-campaign-1927|The International Rescue Campaign (1927)]].

## Aftermath: The Underground Continues

Despite the Yevsektsiya's efforts — and despite the Rayatz's expulsion — Chabad's underground network survived:

**1927–1941**: Under leaderless conditions, Chabad activists continued operating underground schools, mikvaos (ritual baths), and religious courts throughout the Soviet Union.

**The Great Escape (1946–1947)**: After World War II, approximately 1,000 Chabad chassidim escaped from the Soviet Union through a daring operation. Many of these were graduates of the underground system the Yevsektsiya had tried to destroy.

See [[events/great-escape|The Great Escape (1946–1947)]].

## Historical Judgment

The Yevsektsiya is remembered as one of the most tragic phenomena in Jewish history — Jews turning against Jews, using state power to destroy their own religious heritage. Its operatives, many motivated by sincere if misguided revolutionary idealism, ultimately accomplished little except to drive Judaism underground, where it survived in diminished but persistent form.

The Yevsektsiya was disbanded in 1930 — its work considered complete, its members themselves vulnerable to Stalinist terror. But the underground networks it had fought — particularly Chabad's — continued, providing the foundation for Jewish religious survival in the Soviet Union through the darkest decades.
