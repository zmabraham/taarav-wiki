# The Mendel Beilis Blood Libel Trial (1911–1913)

**Date**: 1911 (arrest) – 1913 (trial and acquittal)  
**Location**: Kiev, Russian Empire  
**Key Figures**: Mendel Beilis, [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]], [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]

---

## Overview

The **Mendel Beilis trial** (1911–1913) was the last major **blood libel** trial in European history — a judicial proceeding based on the medieval accusation that Jews murdered Christian children to use their blood for ritual purposes. Beilis, a Jewish manager of a brick factory in Kiev, was falsely accused of murdering a Christian boy, Andrei Yushchinsky. The case became an international cause célèbre. Chabad's response to the trial — particularly the involvement of the young [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] in relief efforts — reflected the movement's commitment to defending Jewish honor and supporting those persecuted for their faith.

---

## Background: The Blood Libel

The **blood libel** (_alilat dam_) was one of the most persistent and destructive antisemitic accusations in history:

- Originating in England in the 12th century (William of Norwich, 1144)
- Spreading across medieval and early modern Europe
- Leading to massacres, expulsions, and judicial murders
- Revived periodically despite repeated official refutations

By the early 20th century, blood libels had become rare in Western Europe but persisted in the Russian Empire, where both popular antisemitism and official government indifference allowed them to flourish.

---

## The Crime and Accusation (1911)

**March 20, 1911**: The body of 13-year-old Andrei Yushchinsky was discovered in a cave on the outskirts of Kiev. He had been stabbed multiple times.

**Initial Investigation**: Kiev police initially investigated various leads. However, reactionary forces within the Russian government — particularly the **Union of the Russian People** (an ultranationalist, antisemitic organization) — pressured authorities to frame a Jew.

**July 1911**: Mendel Beilis, a 39-year-old Jewish father of five who managed a brick factory near where the body was found, was arrested despite a complete lack of evidence against him. His arrest was purely based on his Jewishness and convenience.

---

## The Trial: September–October 1913

Beilis was held in prison for **two years** before his trial finally began in **September 1913**. The proceedings were a farce:

**The Prosecution's Case:**

- No physical evidence linking Beilis to the crime
- No witnesses to the murder
- "Expert testimony" from a Catholic priest who claimed that Jewish religious law required the use of Christian blood
- Reliance on medieval superstitions and antisemitic stereotypes

**The Defense:**

- Led by prominent Russian lawyers including Oscar Gruzenberg, a Jewish attorney
- Presentation of forensic evidence showing the crime scene was inconsistent with the prosecution's theory
- Expert testimony from actual rabbis and scholars demonstrating that Judaism explicitly forbids consumption of blood in any form
- Exposure of the political motivations behind the case

**The Verdict**: On **October 28, 1913**, after deliberating for several hours, the jury acquitted Beilis. The verdict was split — some jurors voted for acquittal on the murder charge but also found that the crime had "ritual characteristics" — a moral victory for antisemites despite the legal acquittal.

---

## International Jewish Response

The Beilis affair provoked outrage and mobilization throughout the Jewish world:

**Western Europe and America**: Major Jewish organizations protested. The press covered the trial extensively, framing it as evidence of Russian backwardness and antisemitism.

**Petitions and Diplomacy**: Jewish leaders appealed to Western governments to intervene. The case became a factor in deteriorating perceptions of Russia in the years leading to World War I.

**Financial Support**: Funds were raised to support Beilis's family and pay for his defense.

---

## Chabad's Response

### The Rashab's Position

The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]] was deeply distressed by the blood libel. As a leader who understood the dangers that antisemitism posed to Russian Jewry, he saw the Beilis case as both a specific injustice and a sign of worsening conditions. He encouraged his chassidim to support the defense effort and to pray for Beilis's acquittal.

### The Young Rayatz's Involvement

The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] — then in his early thirties, assisting his father in communal work — played a significant role in Chabad's response:

**Relief Efforts**: The Rayatz helped coordinate support for Beilis's family and for the legal defense. This included raising funds, organizing petitions, and mobilizing Chabad communities.

**Spiritual Mobilization**: The Rayatz called for special prayers, the recitation of Psalms, and increased Torah study on Beilis's behalf. He framed the situation as not merely a legal matter but a spiritual crisis requiring a spiritual response.

**Public Defense**: The Rayatz used whatever channels were available to publicly condemn the blood libel and defend Jewish honor. In an era when public Jewish engagement was limited, this was significant.

**Support for Beilis After Acquittal**: When Beilis was finally acquitted, the Rayatz — like many Jewish leaders — helped him and his family. Beilis ultimately emigrated to Palestine in 1920, then to the United States in 1925; Jewish leaders worldwide helped him rebuild his life after the ordeal.

---

## Historical Context: Rising Persecution

The Beilis trial occurred during a period of intensifying persecution for Russian Jewry:

- **1903 Kishinev pogrom** and subsequent waves of violence
- **1905 Revolution** and its aftermath, which included pogroms
- Government support for antisemitic organizations like the Union of the Russian People
- Restrictions on Jewish settlement, education, and economic activity

Within a few years of the trial, World War I (1914) and the Russian Revolution (1917) would bring even greater catastrophes. The Beilis affair, in retrospect, was one of the last gasps of the old regime's antisemitism before the Revolution brought new forms of persecution.

---

## The Trial's Aftermath

**For Beilis**: Though acquitted, his life was ruined. He had spent two years in prison, his health was broken, and he could not remain in Kiev. He moved first to Palestine, then to the United States, where he died in 1934.

**For Russian Jewry**: The trial was a humiliation for the Russian government and a warning sign. It demonstrated the depth of official antisemitism and the willingness of the state to sacrifice innocent Jews for political purposes.

**For International Jewish Opinion**: The case accelerated support for Zionism and emigration. If Russia could not protect its Jews, then Jews needed their own homeland or at least the ability to leave.

**For Chabad**: The Rayatz's involvement in the Beilis affair was part of his preparation for the leadership role he would assume in 1920. The trial taught him lessons about defending Jewish honor, mobilizing communities, and maintaining faith under persecution — lessons that would prove essential during the Soviet era.

---

## Literary and Cultural Legacy

The Beilis trial left a significant cultural imprint:

**Bernard Malamud's _The Fixer_ (1966)**: A novel based loosely on the case, winning the Pulitzer Prize and National Book Award.

**Film Adaptations**: Several films have depicted the story, introducing it to new generations.

**Jewish Historical Memory**: The trial remained, throughout the 20th century, a symbol of the dangers of antisemitism and the importance of legal defense of Jewish rights.
