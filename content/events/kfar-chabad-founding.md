# Founding of Kfar Chabad (1949)

**Date**: 1949  
**Location**: Central Israel, near Lod (Lydda)  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]

---

## Overview

**Kfar Chabad** (literally "Chabad Village") was established in 1949 as a Chabad settlement in the newly founded State of Israel. Directed by the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] from Brooklyn, the village became the institutional center of Chabad in [[entities/places/eretz-yisrael|Eretz Yisrael]] — home to yeshivos, schools, publishing houses, and a thriving community of Chassidim, many of whom were survivors of the Soviet underground who had escaped via the [[events/great-escape|Great Escape]] of 1946–1947.

---

## Background

After the [[events/great-escape|Great Escape]], hundreds of Chabad chassidim who had fled the Soviet Union found themselves in displaced persons camps across Europe and in the nascent State of Israel. They needed a permanent home — a place where they could rebuild their communal life, establish institutions, and raise families in the Chassidic tradition.

The Rayatz, directing affairs from [[events/770-eastern-parkway|770 Eastern Parkway]] in Brooklyn, gave the directive to establish a Chabad settlement in Eretz Yisrael. The vision was not merely a residential neighborhood but a self-contained Chassidic village — a center from which Chabad could operate throughout the Land of Israel.

---

## Establishment

The Israeli government allocated land in the **Sharon region**, near the city of **Lod** (Lydda), approximately 15 kilometers southeast of Tel Aviv. The location was strategically accessible — close to the country's population centers and transportation networks — while offering enough space for institutional development.

### First Settlers

The founding population consisted primarily of:

- **Soviet escapees**: Chassidim who had survived decades of underground religious life in the Soviet Union and escaped during 1946–1947
- **Holocaust survivors**: Chabad families who had survived the war in Europe
- **Established Chabad families**: Some who had been in Eretz Yisrael before the state's founding

These settlers brought with them not only their personal belongings but the living traditions of Chabad Chassidism — the nigunim (melodies), the farbrengen culture, the educational methods, and the texts they had preserved through decades of persecution.

---

## Institutional Growth

Kfar Chabad rapidly developed into a major institutional center:

### Educational Institutions

- **Tomchei Temimim Yeshiva**: Originally established in **Lod** after the war, the flagship Chabad yeshiva relocated to Kfar Chabad, becoming its anchor institution. This was a direct continuation of the [[events/tomchei-temimim-founding-1897|original yeshiva]] founded by the Rebbe Rashab in 1897.
- **Girls' schools**: Beth Rivkah and other educational institutions for girls and young women
- **Elementary schools**: Chabad day schools serving the village and surrounding communities
- **Vocational training**: Programs to help immigrants integrate economically

### Community Facilities

- **Synagogues**: Multiple synagogues serving different communities and prayer traditions
- **Mikvah**: Ritual bath — a central institution for any Chassidic community
- **Publishing**: Kfar Chabad became a center for Hebrew-language Chabad publishing

---

## The Rayatz's Death (1950)

The Rayatz passed away on **10 Shevat 5710 (January 28, 1950)** — barely a year after Kfar Chabad's founding. He did not live to see the village's full development, but the institutional foundations he had directed were already in place.

The [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]], who assumed leadership in 1951, took a deep personal interest in Kfar Chabad's development. He sent directives, dispatched emissaries, and provided guidance on institutional decisions. Under his leadership, the village grew from a small settlement into a thriving town.

---

## Challenges and Growth

### Security

In the early years, Kfar Chabad faced security challenges common to Israeli settlements of the period. The village organized its own defense and participated in the broader national security framework.

### The 1956 Massacre

On **11 Adar I, 5716 (January 24, 1956)**, Palestinian fedayeen infiltrators attacked a vocational school in Kfar Chabad, murdering five students and a teacher. The attack devastated the community. The seventh Rebbe's response — a telegram reading "B'hemshech habinyan tenachamu" ("Through the continuation of building, you will be comforted") — became a defining motto. The community responded by intensifying construction and institutional development rather than retreating.

### Expansion

Over the decades, Kfar Chabad grew to include:

- Thousands of residents
- The **Chabad Heritage Center** (Beit Agudas Chassidei Chabad)
- A replica of **770 Eastern Parkway**
- Industrial and commercial enterprises
- A major annual **Lag BaOmer** celebration and parade

---

## Significance

Kfar Chabad represents the physical realization of a vision that stretches back to the earliest days of Chabad:

- The [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] encouraged settlement in the Holy Land
- The [[entities/people/tzemach-tzedek|Tzemach Tzedek]] sent his granddaughter [[entities/people/menucha-rochel|Menucha Rochel]] to Hebron (1845)
- The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]] established [[events/toras-emes-hebron-1911|Toras Emes Yeshiva]] in Hebron (1911)
- The Rayatz directed the founding of Kfar Chabad (1949)

The village became the operational base for Chabad's activities throughout Israel — education, outreach, military chaplaincy, social services — and remains one of the most important Chabad communities in the world.
