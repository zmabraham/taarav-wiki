# Hebron Riots (1929) — End of the Chabad Hebron Community

**Date**: 23-24 Av 5689 (August 23-24, 1929)  
**Location**: Hebron, Mandatory Palestine

---

## Overview

The **1929 Hebron Riots** (part of the broader 1929 Palestine riots) were a devastating pogrom that effectively ended the Chabad Jewish community in Hebron — a community that had existed for over a century, surviving the [[events/hebron-pogrom-1834|1834 riots]] and producing one of the most distinguished Chabad outposts outside Russia. 67 Jews were killed in Hebron alone; the survivors evacuated, and the city was without a permanent Jewish community for decades.

---

## Background: 1929 Palestine Unrest

The 1929 riots across Mandatory Palestine grew out of Arab-Jewish tensions, particularly over access to the Western Wall in Jerusalem. Incitement spread rapidly through Arab communities. Hebron — a city with a small, vulnerable Jewish population — became one of the most catastrophic sites of violence.

---

## The Chabad Community on the Eve of the Riots

By 1929, the Chabad community in Hebron had a proud century-long history:

- Founded in 1823
- Survived the 1834 riots
- Anchored by [[entities/people/menucha-rochel|Rebbetzin Menucha Rochel Slonim]] from 1845 to 1888
- Formalized as [[events/toras-emes-hebron-1911|Toras Emes yeshiva]] in 1911
- Running a functioning Jewish communal and educational life

The community consisted primarily of old families with deep roots in Hebron, plus students from the Toras Emes yeshiva.

---

## The Violence

On August 23-24, 1929, Arab mobs attacked the Jewish quarter of Hebron with extreme violence. 67 Jews were murdered — men, women, and children. Many were sheltered by Arab neighbors who protected them at personal risk, but the overall scale of violence was catastrophic.

The British Mandate authorities were slow to respond and insufficient in providing protection.

The Toras Emes yeshiva students who survived were those who managed to hide or escape.

---

## Evacuation

The surviving Jewish community evacuated Hebron under British escort. For the first time in decades, the city was without a Jewish permanent community.

The Chabad community's institutional infrastructure — including the Toras Emes yeshiva and synagogues — was left behind.

---

## Legacy

The 1929 Hebron massacre is one of the most painful events in the history of the Jewish Yishuv in Mandatory Palestine. For Chabad specifically, it ended the oldest continuously operating Chabad community in Eretz Yisrael — a community that had maintained Jewish presence in one of Judaism's holiest cities for over a century.

The Toras Emes yeshiva relocated and continued in Jerusalem, but the specific Hebron chapter of Chabad history ended in 1929.
