# The Printing of the Tanya

## A History of Chabad's Foundational Text (1796–Present)

**Also known as:** Likkutei Amarim, Sefer Shel Beinonim
**First printing:** 1796 (5556), Slavita
**Subsequent early printings:** 1799 Zolkiev, 1800 Kapust, 1814 Shklov
**Author:** [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] (R' Schneur Zalman of Liadi)
**Significance:** The foundational text of Chabad Chassidus

---

## Overview

The **Tanya** (Likkutei Amarim, "Collection of Discourses") is the foundational text of Chabad Chassidus, authored by the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]]. First printed in **1796** in **Slavita**, the Tanya has been reprinted thousands of times in hundreds of locations around the world. Its printing history reflects both the centrality of this work to Chabad and the extraordinary **mesirus nefesh** (self-sacrifice) of Chabad chassidim to preserve and disseminate it.

---

## The Text and Its Structure

### Likkutei Amarim (The Tanya)

The Tanya, officially titled **Likkutei Amarim**, is also known as **Sefer Shel Beinonim** ("Book of the Intermediates") because of its focus on the "beinoni" — the person who is neither perfectly righteous nor wicked but struggles with imperfect impulses.

### Structure

The work consists of five parts:

1. **Sefer Shel Beinonim** (Book of Intermediates) — The core text
2. **Sha'ar HaYichud V'HaEmunah** (Gate of Unity and Faith)
3. **Igrot Kodesh** (Holy Letters) — Letters on various topics
4. **Kuntres Acharon** (Last Pamphlet) — Additional teachings
5. **Appendices** — Various later additions

---

## First Printing: Slavita (1796)

### The Slavita Press

The first edition of the Tanya was printed in **1796 (5556)** in **Slavita** (present-day Ukraine), a major center of Hebrew printing. The Slavita press was owned by the **Shapira family**, renowned printers of Jewish works.

### The Alter Rebbe's Supervision

The Alter Rebbe personally supervised this first printing:

- He reviewed the text carefully
- He ensured accuracy of the typesetting
- He made corrections and refinements
- He approved the final version

This personal supervision reflected the importance the Alter Rebbe attached to this work as the definitive presentation of his teachings.

### Significance of First Edition

The first printing was significant because:

- It made the Alter Rebbe's teachings available in fixed form
- It allowed systematic study beyond oral transmission
- It established the text that all subsequent editions would follow
- It marked Chabad's transition from oral to written tradition

---

## Second Printing: Zolkiev (1799)

### The Zolkiev Edition

The second edition was printed in **1799 (5559)** in **Zolkiev** (present-day Ukraine), another important Hebrew printing center.

### Improvements and Corrections

The Zolkiev edition included:

- Corrections from the first edition
- Possible additions or refinements
- Improved typesetting and layout
- Wider distribution

### Post-Arrest Context

This printing came shortly after the Alter Rebbe's first arrest (1798), making it an act of defiance and commitment — continuing to publish despite persecution.

---

## Third Printing: Kapust (1800)

### The Kapust Edition

The third edition was printed in **1800 (5560)** in **Kapust** (present-day Belarus), closer to the center of Chabad communities.

### Accessibility

Printing in Kapust made the Tanya more accessible to Chabad chassidim in:

- Belarus
- Lithuania
- The Pale of Settlement

This geographic expansion reflected the growing demand for the Tanya as Chabad spread.

---

## The Tzava'ah Controversy

### What Was the Tzava'ah?

The **Tzava'ah** ("Will" or "Testament") was a controversial document that appeared around this time, purporting to be the Alter Rebbe's will prohibiting certain types of Kabbalistic study.

### Connection to Tanya Printing

Some opponents of Chabad used the Tzava'ah to argue that:

- The Tanya contained dangerous teachings
- Its printing should be restricted
- The Alter Rebbe had restricted certain teachings

### Chabad's Response

Chabad sources maintain that:

- The Tzava'ah was forged or misrepresented
- The Alter Rebbe never prohibited the Tanya
- The work's continued printing was with his full approval
- The Tzava'ah controversy was part of broader opposition to Chassidism

Despite the controversy, printing continued unabated.

---

## Fourth Printing: Shklov (1814)

### Post-Alter Rebbe Context

The **1814** edition was printed in **Shklov**, Belarus — two years after the Alter Rebbe's death and during the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe's]] leadership.

### Continuity

This printing demonstrated:

- Continuity of commitment after the founder's passing
- The Mitteler Rebbe's support for disseminating his father's work
- The enduring importance of the Tanya to Chabad communities

---

## 19th Century Printings

### Expansion and Distribution

Throughout the 19th century, the Tanya was printed repeatedly in:

- **Lubavitch** (Chabad's headquarters)
- **Kapust** (nearby Chabad center)
- **Shklov** (major Jewish community)
- **Other locations** throughout the Pale of Settlement

### Role of Shluchim

**Meshulachim** (emissaries) who traveled to collect funds also:

- Distributed copies of the Tanya
- Taught from it in remote communities
- Created demand for new printings
- Reported back on wear and tear of existing copies

---

## Soviet Period: Underground Printing

### The Danger of Printing

Under Soviet rule, printing the Tanya was:

- **Illegal**: Religious publications were banned
- **Dangerous**: Those caught could be imprisoned
- **Difficult**: Printing presses were monitored

### Underground Operations

Despite the dangers, Chabad chassidim:

- Printed the Tanya **underground** in secret locations
- Used **clandestine presses** or hand copying
- Distributed copies **covertly** to trusted individuals
- Risked **imprisonment** to preserve the text

### Mesirus Nefesh

This underground printing exemplified **mesirus nefesh**:

- Ordinary chassidim taking extraordinary risks
- Prioritizing spiritual needs over physical safety
- Preserving tradition despite official opposition
- Passing copies from hand to hand secretly

The Tanya printed during this period became particularly treasured as evidence of this sacrifice.

---

## Post-War Printing: Lubavitch and Beyond

### Lubavitch Revival

After World War II and the Holocaust:

- The Tanya was printed in **displaced persons camps**
- New editions appeared in **France**, **America**, and **Israel**
- Surviving chassidim prioritized reprinting foundational texts
- The **770** press in Brooklyn began regular printings

### Global Distribution

Under the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|Seventh Rebbe]]:

- The Tanya was translated into **multiple languages**
- Printings occurred in **dozens of countries**
- **Study groups** formed worldwide
- The Tanya became **universally accessible** to Chabad communities

---

## The Modern Era

### Thousands of Printings

Today, the Tanya has been:

- **Printed thousands of times**
- In **hundreds of locations** worldwide
- In **dozens of languages**
- With **commentaries** and explanations

### The Printer in 770

As noted in historical sources, the printer **M. Shusterman** worked on Tanya printing at **770 Eastern Parkway** in Brooklyn — the world Chabad headquarters. This symbolizes the continuity from the Alter Rebbe's personal supervision in Slavita to the global center of Chabad today.

### Study and Dissemination

The Tanya is studied:

- **Daily** by thousands of chassidim
- In **shiurim** (classes) worldwide
- Through **online resources** and apps
- As part of **Chabad's core curriculum**

---

## Historical Significance

### Textual Preservation

The printing history of the Tanya demonstrates:

- The **centrality** of this text to Chabad identity
- The **commitment** to preserving the Alter Rebbe's teachings
- The **sacrifice** chassidim made to keep the text available
- The **evolution** from hand-copied manuscripts to global distribution

### Resistance to Oppression

Each printing — whether under Czarist censorship, Soviet bans, or wartime conditions — represented:

- **Defiance** of oppressive authorities
- **Commitment** to spiritual values over physical safety
- **Continuity** of tradition across generations
- **Victory** of light over darkness
