# פתיחת מוזיאון ליובאוויטש

## Lubavitch Museum Opening (2001)

**Date**: 2001  
**Location**: [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]], Russia  
**Significance**: Memorial to the destroyed Chabad center and its Jewish community

---

## Overview

In **2001**, the **Lubavitch Museum** was opened in the town of [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]] — commemorating the history of the town that served as Chabad's world center for 102 years (1813–1915) and whose Jewish community was entirely murdered in the Holocaust. The _Lubavitch_ book (Vol. 9 of the Mesibbv L'Chassidus series) was published to coincide with this opening.

---

## Background: The Destruction of Lubavitch's Jews

Lubavitch had been Chabad's world center for over a century. After the Rebbe's court burned down in 1921, the town's remaining Jewish community continued to live there under Soviet rule. In **November 1941**, Nazi forces murdered all **483 remaining Jews of Lubavitch** — every man, woman, and child in what had been the spiritual capital of a worldwide Jewish movement.

---

## The 1989 Ohel Reconstruction

Before the museum, an earlier act of restoration had occurred: in **1989**, the ohel (grave marker) of the [[entities/people/tzemach-tzedek|Tzemach Tzedek]] and other Rebbes was rebuilt after decades of Soviet-era destruction or neglect, enabling renewed pilgrimage to the Rebbes' graves.

---

## The Museum

The Lubavitch Museum opened in 2001 to:

- Commemorate the 102-year history of Chabad in Lubavitch
- Honor the memory of the 483 murdered Jews
- Document the town's transformation from anonymous Russian provincial town to world religious center
- Provide an educational resource for visitors and pilgrims

The publication of _Lubavitch_ (Book 9 of the series) by [[entities/people/shalom-dov-levin|R' Shalom DovBer Levin]] as a town history was directly connected to the museum's opening.

---

## Historical Significance

The Lubavitch Museum represents an attempt to maintain memory of a destroyed community in the very place where it once flourished — as opposed to the relocation of memory to diaspora institutions. It is one of many efforts in the former Soviet Union to document and commemorate Jewish communities annihilated in the Holocaust.
