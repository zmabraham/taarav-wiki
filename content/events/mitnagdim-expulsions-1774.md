# Mitnagdic Expulsions of Chassidic Leaders (1774–1775)

## Coordinated Expulsions of Chassidic Figures from Their Communities

**Dates:** תקל"ד–תקל"ה (approximately 1774–1775)
**Locations:** Pinsk, Karlin, and surrounding communities in White Russia/Lithuania
**Key figures expelled:**
- Rabbi Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev — expelled from Pinsk
- Rabbi Shlomo of Karlin — expelled from Karlin
- Other Chassidic figures in surrounding communities

---

## Overview

The Mitnagdic Expulsions of 1774–1775 represent a second major wave of organized anti-Chassidic action following the initial herem of 1772. If the 1772 herem established the religious-legal framework for opposition to Chassidism, the expulsions of 1774–75 demonstrated that the Mitnagdic establishment was prepared to use communal coercion — up to and including physical removal — to enforce its exclusion of Chassidic leaders from their communities.

## The Pinsk Expulsion (Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev)

Rabbi Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev — later famous as one of the most beloved chassidic masters in Jewish memory, known for his passionate advocacy on behalf of the Jewish people before God — was at this time serving in Pinsk. The Mitnagdic community of Pinsk organized against him and succeeded in forcing him out of the city.

Levi Yitzchak's expulsion from Pinsk was a significant blow: Pinsk was a major Jewish community in the Lithuanian-Belarusian borderlands, and his removal demonstrated that even a charismatic and personally admired Chassidic leader could be expelled by organized communal opposition. He subsequently moved to Berdichev in Ukraine, where he spent the rest of his career and where he is buried.

## The Karlin Expulsion (Shlomo of Karlin)

Simultaneously, Rabbi Shlomo of Karlin — leader of the Karlin Chassidic community that had been founded by Rabbi Aaron the Great — was expelled from Karlin. The Karlin Chassidim were particularly targeted because of their distinctive, emotionally intense prayer style, which Mitnagdic critics viewed as the most egregious example of Chassidic excess. The expulsion removed Rabbi Shlomo from his community's base and forced him to relocate to Ludmir (Vladimir-Volynsky) in Ukraine.

## Methods and Significance

The expulsions were carried out through a combination of communal herem, social pressure, appeals to local authorities, and in some cases physical intimidation. The Mitnagdic communities organized formally, with rabbinic leadership providing the halachic justification and lay leaders providing the communal and political muscle.

These expulsions:
1. Dispersed the Chassidic leadership geographically, weakening concentrated centers of Chassidic influence in Lithuania-Belarus
2. Demonstrated the limits of what Chassidic diplomacy (like the Shklov debate of 1772) could achieve against determined opposition
3. Reinforced the separation between Chassidic and Mitnagdic communities, making peaceful coexistence increasingly the practical reality rather than active persecution

## Context within Chabad History

The Alter Rebbe operated in this environment of active persecution throughout the 1770s. While Chabad in White Russia was somewhat less exposed than the Karlin community in Pinsk due to geographic distance from the major Mitnagdic centers, the Alter Rebbe was aware of and affected by these events. The expulsions of 1774–75 helped shape his approach to managing Chabad's relationship with non-Chassidic communities: emphasizing solid halachic practice, avoiding unnecessary provocation, and building Chabad's internal institutions rather than seeking direct confrontation.
