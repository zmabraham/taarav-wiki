# Rashab's Final Days in Rostov (1915–1920)

**Date**: 1915–1920 (5675–5680)  
**Location**: Rostov-on-Don, Russia  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]], [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]

---

## Overview

The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab's]] final years (1915–1920) were spent in **Rostov-on-Don**, where he had relocated after the [[events/lubavitch-wwi-evacuation|WWI evacuation from Lubavitch]]. These were years of physical decline but spiritual productivity. Despite increasing illness, the Rashab continued teaching, writing, and guiding his community. His death on **2 Nissan 5680 (March 21, 1920)** marked the end of an era and the beginning of the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz's]] leadership. The Rashab's grave in Rostov became an _ohel_ (shrine) that remains a pilgrimage site to this day.

---

## Life in Rostov (1915–1920)

### The New Court

After the trauma of evacuation, the Rashab established a new Chabad center in Rostov:

- **Housing**: A building on **Bolshoy Street** served as the Rashab's residence and the new court
- **Synagogue**: A synagogue was established in or near the residence
- **Study Hall**: Tomchei Temimim operated in cramped conditions, attempting to maintain the intense learning schedule of Lubavitch
- **Community Life**: Chassidim who had evacuated from Lubavitch slowly reconstituted communal life

Rostov was a very different environment from Lubavitch. It was a large commercial city, not a small shtetl. The Jewish community was more assimilated, less uniformly religious. The Rashab and his followers were strangers in a strange place.

### The Rashab's Health

The Rashab's health began to decline significantly during this period:

- **1915–1917**: Periodic illnesses, increasingly frequent
- **1917–1919**: More serious deterioration, including what appears to have been heart or respiratory problems
- **1919–1920**: Final decline, with periods of confinement to bed

Despite this, the Rashab maintained his schedule as much as possible. He continued to receive visitors, answer questions, and provide guidance to individuals and communities.

---

## Teaching Until the End

One of the most remarkable aspects of the Rashab's final years was his continued productivity as a teacher and author:

### Chassidic Discourses (Maamarim)

The Rashab continued delivering formal Chassidic discourses even when ill:

- **Public Discourses**: Delivered on Shabbat, holidays, and special occasions
- **Private Teachings**: Smaller, more intimate sessions with close chassidim
- **Written Works**: He continued writing and editing his discourses, many of which were published posthumously

The content of these late discourses often dealt with themes of exile, redemption, and faith in times of darkness — reflecting the historical moment of war, revolution, and persecution.

### Correspondence

The Rashab maintained extensive correspondence:

- **With Chabad communities** throughout Russia
- **With supporters and patrons** who provided financial assistance
- **With his children** and other family members
- **With rabbis and leaders** of other Jewish communities

These letters provide a window into his concerns during these final years: funding for the yeshiva, the welfare of refugees, the preservation of Jewish life under increasingly hostile conditions.

---

## The Russian Revolution (1917)

The **Bolshevik Revolution of October 1917** occurred during the Rashab's final period. The impact on Rostov:

- **Civil War**: Rostov changed hands multiple times between Red (Bolshevik) and White (anti-Bolshevik) forces during 1918–1919
- **Chaos**: Food shortages, violence, and displacement
- **Anti-Religious Measures**: Even before the Bolsheviks consolidated power, religious institutions faced pressure

The Rashab, physically weak and unable to flee, remained in Rostov through this turmoil. His presence provided stability to the Chabad community, even as the world collapsed around them.

---

## Final Illness and Death

### The Last Weeks

In the final weeks of his life, the Rashab's condition worsened:

- **Bedridden**: He became increasingly confined to bed
- **Pain**: Sources indicate he suffered significant physical pain
- **Clarity**: Despite physical weakness, his mental clarity remained intact until the end

### Deathbed Instructions

The Rashab used his final moments to prepare his son and successor, the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], for the challenges ahead. Key instructions included:

**1. Maintain Tomchei Temimim**
The Rashab emphasized that the yeshiva was the foundation of Chabad's future. No matter how difficult conditions became, the educational network must survive.

**2. Strengthen Faith Under Persecution**
Anticipating the Soviet persecution that would begin in earnest after his death, the Rashab told the Rayatz: _"There will be difficult times; you will need to have mesirat nefesh bepoel (actual self-sacrifice). But eventually, the Communist government will disintegrate."_

This prophecy — recorded in the Rayatz's diary — would be cited repeatedly in subsequent decades as the Rayatz and his followers faced Soviet persecution. Its fulfillment in the Soviet collapse of 1990–1991 was understood as validation of the Rashab's vision.

**3. Care for the Community**
The Rashab instructed the Rayatz to continue caring for the material and spiritual needs of Chassidim, even in conditions of scarcity and danger.

**4. Publish His Writings**
The Rashab had written extensively over his lifetime. He asked the Rayatz to ensure his works were published and disseminated — a task the Rayatz would pursue throughout his leadership.

### The Passing

**Date**: **2 Nissan 5680** (March 21, 1920)
**Time**: Morning hours
**Place**: His home in Rostov-on-Don

The Rashab was surrounded by his family and close chassidim. His final words, according to sources, were expressions of faith and acceptance of the divine will. He was approximately 59 years old (born 1860/1861).

---

## Funeral and Burial

### The Funeral

The Rashab's funeral took place in Rostov under difficult conditions:

- **Civil War Context**: Ongoing civil conflict made public gatherings dangerous
- **Soviet Suspicion**: Bolshevik authorities viewed public religious ceremonies with hostility
- **Chassidic Participation**: Despite obstacles, chassidim gathered from throughout Russia to pay their respects

The funeral procession wound through Rostov's streets — a public demonstration of Jewish faith in a time when such demonstrations were increasingly dangerous.

### The Burial

The Rashab was buried in the **Jewish cemetery of Rostov-on-Don**. His grave was marked, and his wife, Rebbetzin Shterna Sarah (who would pass away in 1942), would later be buried beside him.

Over time, an **ohel** (structure built over a grave) was constructed. The ohel became a pilgrimage site for Chabad chassidim who would travel to Rostov to pray at the Rebbe's grave. This practice continued even during the Soviet period, when making the pilgrimage required genuine _mesirus nefesh_ (self-sacrifice).

---

## Immediate Aftermath

### The Rayatz Assumes Leadership

The Rayatz, then 39 years old, assumed leadership of Chabad immediately upon his father's death. The transition was smooth in terms of communal acceptance — the Rayatz had been his father's designated successor for years, and his qualifications were universally recognized.

However, the circumstances were dire:

- **Soviet Power**: The Bolsheviks were consolidating control over former Russian territory
- **Anti-Religious Campaign**: The systematic persecution of religion was beginning
- **Economic Collapse**: War and revolution had devastated the economy

The Rayatz would spend the next seven years (1920–1927) waging a desperate struggle to keep Judaism alive under Soviet rule, culminating in his [[events/rayatz-arrest-1927|arrest and liberation in 1927]].

### Community Grief

The Rashab's passing was mourned throughout the Chabad world:

- **Lubavitch**: Chassidim who remembered the town's glory days grieved for the passing of the last Rebbe who had reigned there
- **Russia**: Communities throughout Russia mourned the loss of their spiritual leader
- **Palestine and Beyond**: Chabad communities in Hebron, Jerusalem, and elsewhere held memorial gatherings

---

## The Ohel in Rostov: A Continuing Presence

The Rashab's grave in Rostov became a site of ongoing spiritual significance:

**Soviet Period**: Despite the dangers, chassidim continued to visit the ohel clandestinely. The pilgrimage required travel permits, navigation of Soviet bureaucracy, and risk of surveillance.

**Post-Soviet Period**: After the fall of the Soviet Union, the ohel became fully accessible. It was renovated and expanded. Today, thousands of visitors come annually to pray at the graves of the Rashab and Rebbetzin Shterna Sarah.

**Spiritual Practice**: Visitors write notes (_kvitlach_) with prayers and requests, following the practice common at the graves of tzaddikim (righteous individuals). The ohel functions as a spiritual focal point — a place where the living connect with the righteous dead.

---

## Historical Significance

The Rashab's final years and death represent a transition point in Chabad history:

**End of an Era**: The Rashab was the last Rebbe who had lived in Lubavitch before the World War I evacuation. His death marked the definitive end of the "Lubavitch period" (1813–1915).

**Beginning of the Soviet Era**: His death in 1920 occurred as the Bolsheviks were consolidating power. The Soviet persecution of Judaism — which would define Chabad life for the next seven decades — began in earnest immediately after his passing.

**Passing of the Torch**: The seamless transition to the Rayatz's leadership ensured continuity through the catastrophes to come: Soviet persecution, the Holocaust, and the postwar rebuilding.
