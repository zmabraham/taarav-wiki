# The International Rescue Campaign for the Rayatz (1927)

**Date**: June–July 1927 (Sivan–Tammuz 5687)  
**Location**: International — coordinated from New York, London, Riga, Warsaw, Jerusalem  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], [[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Mordechai Dubin]], American Jewish Congress, international Jewish leaders

---

## Overview

The arrest of the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] by the Soviet GPU in June 1927 triggered one of the most remarkable international campaigns in modern Jewish history. Within weeks, Jewish communities across the globe mobilized — organizing protests, petitions, diplomatic interventions, and media pressure. This campaign, coordinated across continents and despite significant logistical obstacles, achieved what seemed impossible: the liberation of a sentenced prisoner from the Soviet Union. The story of this campaign is a testament to Jewish solidarity and the power of organized advocacy.

---

## The Campaign's Structure

The rescue effort was not centralized but emerged organically from multiple centers:

**Primary Centers**:

- **United States**: Major Jewish organizations in New York
- **Latvia**: The Latvian government and [[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Mordechai Dubin]]
- **Western Europe**: Jewish communities in Britain, France, Germany
- **Palestine**: The Jewish community under British Mandate
- **Eastern Europe**: Jewish communities in Poland, Romania

Each center operated somewhat independently but coordinated through telegrams, personal networks, and shared diplomatic channels.

---

## American Jewish Mobilization

### The American Jewish Congress

The **American Jewish Congress** (founded 1918) took a leading role in the American campaign:

**Leadership**: Figures like **Rabbi Stephen Wise** and other prominent American Jewish leaders became involved.

**Strategy**:

- Public protests and demonstrations
- Petitions with thousands of signatures
- Pressure on the U.S. State Department to intervene
- Media coverage in American newspapers

**Challenges**:

- The United States did not have diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union in 1927
- This meant direct diplomatic pressure through official channels was limited
- The campaign relied instead on public opinion and indirect diplomatic pressure

### Other American Organizations

Additional organizations joined the effort:

- **B'nai B'rith**
- Various Orthodox organizations
- Landsmanshaftn (immigrant mutual aid societies) representing Jews from specific regions
- Individual synagogues and community institutions

These groups sent telegrams to Soviet embassies abroad, organized prayer vigils, and raised awareness through the Yiddish and English press.

## The Latvian Intervention: Mordechai Dubin

### Mordechai Dubin's Role

**[[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Rabbi Mordechai Dubin]]** (1889–1956) was a Latvian Jewish parliamentarian, businessman, and devoted Chabad chassid who played the pivotal role in the Rayatz's liberation:

**Background**:

- Member of the Latvian Saeima (parliament)
- Leader of the Agudat Yisrael faction in Latvia
- Successful businessman with connections to Latvian government officials
- Personal chassid of the Rayatz

**Diplomatic Status**:

- Latvia had diplomatic relations with the Soviet Union
- Dubin could travel to Moscow with official status
- He could meet with Soviet officials as a representative of a foreign government

### The Mission to Moscow

Dubin traveled to **Moscow** in late June or early July 1927 on a diplomatic mission:

**Objective**: Secure the Rayatz's release from prison and exile.

**Meetings**:

- Dubin met with high-level Soviet officials, including possibly **Georgy Chicherin** (People's Commissar for Foreign Affairs) or other foreign ministry officials
- He presented the case as both a humanitarian issue and a diplomatic matter

**Arguments Used**:

- The Rayatz held Latvian citizenship through his residence in Riga
- His imprisonment was damaging Soviet-Latvian relations
- International opinion was turning against the Soviet Union
- His execution would create a martyr and lasting damage to Soviet reputation

**Leverage**:

- Latvia was a small country but a sovereign nation with diplomatic standing
- The Soviet Union, though authoritarian, cared about international opinion during this period
- Dubin's personal status as a parliamentarian gave him access

### The Outcome

Dubin's intervention was decisive:

- Soviet authorities agreed to commute the death sentence
- Further pressure resulted in commutation from Solovki to exile
- Finally, full liberation and expulsion from the Soviet Union was granted

Dubin's role was recognized within Chabad as the key human instrument of the Rayatz's liberation — a _shaliach_ (emissary) whose diplomatic work achieved what seemed impossible.

## European Jewish Responses

### British Jewry

Jewish communities in Britain organized:

- **Protests** to the Soviet embassy in London
- **Petitions** signed by community leaders
- **Media coverage** in British newspapers
- **Parliamentary questions** raised by sympathetic members of Parliament

### Continental European Communities

**France, Germany, Poland**:

- Jewish communities in these countries sent protests and petitions
- In Poland, with its large Jewish population and history with the Rayatz, the response was particularly strong
- Community leaders used whatever local political influence they had

### Central and Eastern European Jewry

Jewish communities in **Romania, Lithuania, and other countries** also protested, though with less diplomatic impact than the larger Western communities.

---

## Palestinian Jewish Response

The Jewish community in **Palestine** (under British Mandate) responded strongly:

**Chief Rabbinate**: The Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi and other religious leaders protested.

**Communities**: Both religious and secular Jewish communities in Palestine joined the campaign — unusual solidarity given the ideological divides of the time.

**Significance**: The Palestinian Jewish community had a special relationship with the Rayatz, who had supported institutions there including the [[events/toras-emes-hebron-1911|Toras Emes yeshiva in Hebron]].

## Soviet Calculations

Why did the Soviet Union agree to release the Rayatz?

### International Opinion

Despite its authoritarian nature, the Soviet government in 1927 was sensitive to international opinion:

- The USSR was seeking recognition and trade relations with Western countries
- Negative press about religious persecution was diplomatically damaging
- Jewish communities had influence in countries the Soviets hoped to engage with

### Cost-Benefit Analysis

Soviet leaders likely calculated:

- **Cost of executing the Rayatz**: International outrage, martyr status, ongoing damage to reputation
- **Cost of imprisoning him**: Continued protests, diplomatic complications
- **Cost of releasing him**: Temporary embarrassment, but the problem would leave the country
- **Benefit of release**: Improved international opinion, removal of a diplomatic liability

### The Decision

The decision evolved in stages:

1. **Death sentence commuted to Solovki**: Concession to international pressure
2. **Solovki commuted to exile**: Further concession as pressure continued
3. **Exile commuted to liberation and expulsion**: Final concession, removing the problem entirely

The Soviet solution — liberation combined with expulsion — achieved their goal: the Rayatz was gone, the international pressure ended, but they avoided creating an immediate martyr through execution.

## The Liberation

**12-13 Tammuz 5687 (July 12-13, 1927)**: The Rayatz was informed of his liberation and permitted to leave the Soviet Union.

**Departure from Leningrad**:

- Thousands of chassidim gathered at the train station to see him off
- The crowd was so large that Soviet authorities were alarmed
- The Rayatz described the 18-hour journey to Riga as "eighteen hours with anguish in the heart, pain in the soul"

**Arrival in Riga**:

- Large crowds welcomed him
- The Latvian government received him officially
- Dubin's role was publicly recognized

**Yud-Beis Tammuz**:
The liberation date became the Chabad holiday of **Yud-Beis Tammuz** — celebrated annually as the "Festival of Liberation" alongside 19 Kislev (Alter Rebbe) and 9-10 Kislev (Mitteler Rebbe).

## Legacy of the Campaign

The 1927 rescue campaign established several important precedents:

### Jewish Advocacy Works

The campaign demonstrated that **international Jewish advocacy could achieve results**, even against an authoritarian regime like the Soviet Union. This lesson would inform subsequent campaigns for Soviet Jewry.

### Global Solidarity

The campaign showed **Jewish global solidarity** — communities on every continent mobilizing for a single cause. This solidarity, despite ideological differences, would recur in later crises.

### Diplomatic Channels Work

**[[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Mordechai Dubin's]]** role demonstrated that diplomatic engagement, through the right channels and with credible status, could achieve what public pressure alone could not.

### The Rebbe's International Status

The campaign revealed that the **Rayatz was recognized as a major Jewish leader** internationally — not merely the head of a Hasidic sect but a figure of global Jewish significance.
