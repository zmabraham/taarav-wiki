# Lubavitch Fire (1921)

**Major fire that destroyed the main buildings of the Lubavitch courtyard, including the Rebbe's residence; symbolic end of the classic Lubavitch era**

## Background

After the Rashab's death in Rostov in 1920 and the Rayatz's assumption of leadership, the Chabad center had effectively moved from Lubavitch to Rostov and then to Leningrad. The town of Lubavitch itself — which had been the Chabad capital for approximately a century — was now under Soviet rule and the Jewish community faced increasing hardships.

The Lubavitch courtyard had been the physical and spiritual heart of Chabad since the era of the Mitteler Rebbe. Its buildings had been developed across multiple generations, housing the Rebbe's court, Tomchei Temimim yeshiva, guest quarters for thousands of visiting Chassidim, and the central synagogue.

## What Happened

In 1921, a major fire broke out in the Lubavitch courtyard, causing widespread destruction. The main buildings of the compound were burned, including the Rebbe's residence. The fire was devastating not only physically but symbolically — it consumed the architectural legacy of Chabad's classical era.

The fire occurred under Soviet rule, when the community was already weakened and lacked the resources or organizational ability to rebuild. Unlike earlier fires in the 19th century that had been repaired and the buildings restored, this fire was permanent — the courtyard was not rebuilt.

## Significance

The 1921 fire marks the definitive physical end of Lubavitch as the Chabad capital. It complemented the organizational transition that had already occurred with the Rashab's death and the Rayatz's establishment of the Chabad center in other cities. The fire became a poignant symbol in Chabad memory — the physical destruction paralleling the broader destruction of the old Jewish world under Soviet and later Nazi rule.

The site remained in disrepair for decades under Soviet rule. Only much later was a museum opened at the site (2001) as part of the restoration of Jewish heritage sites in the former Soviet Union.
