# The Great Escape (1946–1947)

**Date**: Late 1946 – early 1947  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], [[entities/people/binyamin-gorodetsky|Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky]]  
**Location**: Soviet Central Asia → Lvov → Poland → Western Europe

---

## Overview

The **Great Escape** (_HaBricha HaGedolah_) was the organized exodus of approximately 1,000 Chabad chassidim from the Soviet Union in 1946–1947, exploiting a brief window created by the Soviet-Polish repatriation agreement. It was one of the most daring operations in modern Jewish history — a clandestine mass flight involving forged documents, bribed officials, and extraordinary coordination between chassidim trapped inside the Soviet Union and the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]'s headquarters in New York.

---

## Background: Trapped Behind the Iron Curtain

After World War II, the Soviet Chabad community was in desperate straits. Decades of [[events/soviet-persecution|Soviet persecution]] — the Yevsektsiya campaigns, the closure of synagogues and yeshivos, arrests, executions, and exile — had decimated religious life. The war itself had scattered chassidim across Central Asia, where many had fled or been evacuated from the western Soviet Union.

By 1945–1946, several hundred Chabad families were concentrated in cities like **Samarkand**, **Tashkent**, and **Bukhara** in Uzbekistan. They had maintained underground religious life — secret yeshivos, ritual slaughter, mikvaos — but lived under constant surveillance and the threat of arrest. The Iron Curtain was descending, and with it, the last hopes of ever practicing Judaism openly.

---

## The Opening: The Soviet-Polish Repatriation Agreement

In 1945–1946, the Soviet Union and the new Polish government signed an agreement allowing **ethnic Poles** who had been stranded in the Soviet Union during the war to repatriate to Poland. This created a narrow legal window: anyone who could demonstrate Polish citizenship or origin could leave the Soviet Union.

For the Chabad chassidim — almost all of whom were Russian, Belarusian, or Ukrainian by origin — this meant one thing: they needed to become "Poles."

---

## The Operation

### Forged Documents

The operation required the mass production of forged Polish identity documents. Skilled counterfeiters within the community modified existing papers or created new ones from scratch. Each family needed:

- Polish birth certificates or identity papers
- Documentation showing Polish origin or residence
- Travel permits for internal Soviet movement (to reach the border)

The quality of the forgeries was critical. Soviet border officials were trained to detect fakes, and a single error could lead to arrest — not only of the individual but of the entire network.

### The Rabbinical Court in Lvov

A critical organizational center was established in **Lvov** (Lemberg), the Ukrainian city near the Polish border. Here, a **rabbinical court of 23** (_beis din_) was convened to:

- Set halachic guidelines for the operation (including questions about the permissibility of using forged documents, traveling on Shabbat in emergencies, and other legal issues)
- Pool financial resources from families who had saved money, gold, or valuables
- Coordinate the logistics of moving hundreds of people from Central Asia across thousands of miles to the border
- Establish contacts with Polish officials and border guards

### Women as Messengers

A distinctive feature of the operation was the use of **women as primary messengers and coordinators**. Soviet authorities were less likely to suspect women of clandestine activity, and women could move between cities with less scrutiny. Chabad women carried forged documents, money, and instructions across vast distances — often traveling alone by train through Soviet territory.

### The Border Crossing

The actual crossing took place primarily through **Lvov**, with chassidim traveling in groups disguised as Polish repatriates. Transport was often in **cattle cars** — the same type of railroad cars associated with wartime deportations — packed with families and their meager possessions.

The crossings were timed carefully. Guards were bribed where possible. Several groups were stopped, interrogated, and some members arrested. The operation was not without casualties — some chassidim were caught with forged papers and imprisoned.

---

## The Az Yashir Moment

One of the most dramatic episodes occurred on **December 2, 1946**. A large group of chassidim was attempting to cross the border. In New York, the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] — who was directing the operation from afar through coded correspondence and emissaries — suddenly called for the recitation of **Az Yashir** (_Shirat HaYam_, the Song of the Sea) at precisely the time the crossing was taking place.

The chassidim later learned that their border crossing succeeded at that exact moment. The synchronicity was understood within the community as a demonstration of the Rebbe's supernatural awareness — a manifestation of the Chassidic belief in the tzaddik's spiritual connection to his followers regardless of physical distance.

---

## The Rayatz's Role

The Rayatz coordinated the entire operation from **770 Eastern Parkway** in Brooklyn:

- He dispatched **Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky** as his primary field coordinator in Europe
- He raised and transmitted funds — including **$7,000 in bribes** to secure the release of chassidim who were arrested during border crossings
- He maintained coded correspondence with contacts inside the Soviet Union
- He used diplomatic channels — including contacts in the American Jewish community and possibly the U.S. State Department — to apply pressure where possible

[[entities/people/binyamin-gorodetsky|Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky]] served as the Rayatz's representative in Western Europe, receiving the refugees as they crossed into Poland and arranging their onward movement.

---

## Aftermath and Dispersion

Once across the border into Poland, the chassidim were not yet safe. Poland itself was unstable, and the communist government was tightening its grip. The refugees moved through **displaced persons camps** and transit points in:

- **Poland** → **Germany** (American occupation zone)
- **Austria**
- **France** (particularly Paris, where a Chabad community was established)

From Western Europe, the chassidim dispersed globally:

- Many went to **Eretz Yisrael**, where they became the founding population of [[events/kfar-chabad-founding|Kfar Chabad]] (1949)
- Others joined the Rayatz in **Brooklyn**, strengthening the nascent American Chabad community
- Some remained in **Western Europe**, establishing Chabad communities in France, England, and elsewhere
- A smaller number went to **Australia**, **Canada**, and **South America**

---

## Significance

The Great Escape was significant on multiple levels:

1. **Demographic**: It saved approximately 1,000 chassidim — a critical mass — from Soviet oblivion. These families became the seed population for Chabad's postwar growth.
2. **Institutional**: The evacuees brought with them the living traditions, texts, and practices of the underground Soviet Chabad community — an irreplaceable cultural transmission.
3. **Spiritual**: The operation reinforced the Chabad ethos of [[concepts/mesirus-nefesh|mesirus nefesh]] (self-sacrifice) and the centrality of the Rebbe's guidance even in the most extreme circumstances.
4. **Historical**: It was one of the last organized Jewish escapes from the Soviet Union before the Iron Curtain sealed completely. Those who did not escape in 1946–1947 would remain trapped for decades — many until the 1970s–1990s emigration waves.

---

## Those Left Behind

Not all chassidim were able to escape. Hundreds remained in the Soviet Union, continuing underground religious life for another generation. Their story — the clandestine schools, the secret mikvaos, the hidden Torah scrolls — is one of the most remarkable chapters in Jewish history. Many of their descendants eventually emigrated during the Soviet Jewry movement of the 1970s–1990s.
