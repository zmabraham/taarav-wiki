# Chabad in Displaced Persons Camps (1945–1950)

**Date**: 1945–1950  
**Location**: Germany, Austria, Italy — Displaced Persons (DP) camps  
**Key Figures**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], [[entities/people/binyamin-gorodetsky|Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky]], DP camp activists

---

## Overview

In the aftermath of World War II, approximately **250,000 Jewish survivors** (known as **_She'erit Hapletah_** — "the surviving remnant") found themselves in **Displaced Persons (DP) camps** in Germany, Austria, and Italy. These camps — often former concentration camps or military barracks — became temporary homes for Jews who had nowhere else to go. Chabad was among the first Jewish organizations to establish a presence in these camps, providing religious, educational, and humanitarian support to survivors rebuilding their lives from nothing.

---

## The DP Camps: Context

### What Were DP Camps?

**Displaced Persons Camps** were temporary facilities established by the Allied military authorities (American, British, and French zones of occupation) to house:

**Concentration Camp Survivors**:

- Jews liberated from Nazi death camps and concentration camps
- Many were near death from starvation, disease, and abuse

**Forced Laborers**:

- Jews who had been taken to Germany for forced labor
- Displaced from their homes across Europe

**Refugees**:

- Jews who fled or were expelled from their home countries
- Many from Eastern Europe (Poland, USSR, Romania, Hungary)

**Population**: By 1946–1947, approximately 250,000 Jews were in DP camps — a significant percentage of European Jewry's survivors.

### Major DP Camps with Chabad Presence

**Germany**:

- **Feldafing** — Bavaria, American zone
- **Landsberg** — Bavaria, American zone
- **Föhrenwald** — Bavaria, American zone
- **Bergen-Belsen** — former concentration camp, British zone

**Austria**:

- **Riedenburg** — near Salzburg, American zone

**Italy**:

- **Transit camps** for refugees en route to Palestine or America

## Chabad's Entry into the DP Camps

### Early Response (1945–1946)

Chabad activists arrived in the DP camps soon after liberation:

**Immediate Relief**:

- Food packages and basic supplies
- Medical assistance
- Emotional and spiritual support

**Religious Revival**:

- Many survivors had lost all religious infrastructure
- Chabad helped reestablish synagogues, mikvaos (ritual baths), and Torah study
- Provided religious texts, tefillin, prayer books, and other religious articles

**Organizational Support**:

- **[[events/founding-machne-israel|Machne Israel]]** — Chabad's humanitarian arm — provided funding
- The **Rayatz** coordinated the effort from 770 Eastern Parkway in Brooklyn
- **[[entities/people/binyamin-gorodetsky|Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky]]** served as the Rayatz's primary representative in Europe

---

## Activities and Programs

### Reestablishing Religious Life

**Synagogues**:

- Chabad helped establish prayer spaces in DP camps
- Often, these were simple rooms or barracks transformed into synagogues
- Daily and Shabbat services were organized

**Mikvaos (Ritual Baths)**:

- Building or renovating mikvaos was a priority
- Many survivors had not been able to use a mikvah during the war years
- The mikvah was seen as essential for religious and communal renewal

**Religious Articles**:

- Tefillin were distributed and used
- Torah scrolls were obtained or repaired
- Prayer books (siddurim) and other religious texts were provided

### Educational Programs

**Schools for Children**:

- **Cheders** (elementary religious schools) were established
- Many children had been deprived of any education during the war
- Chabad provided teachers and curricula

**Yeshivos**:

- **Tomchei Temimim yeshivos** were reestablished in DP camps
- Young men who had survived or escaped persecution studied Torah
- These yeshivos became centers of religious revival

**Adult Education**:

- Classes for adults who had lost their Jewish education
- Study sessions for survivors reconnecting with Judaism
- Preparation for those who had forgotten how to observe

### Humanitarian Assistance

**Material Support**:

- Food packages from American Jewish organizations
- Clothing and basic supplies
- Financial assistance for families in need

**Family Reunification**:

- Helped survivors locate missing family members
- Assisted with correspondence and search efforts
- Connected families separated by the war

**Immigration Preparation**:

- Many survivors wanted to leave Europe as soon as possible
- Chabad helped with paperwork, visas, and connections
- Directed survivors toward Palestine, America, or other destinations

## Key Figures in the DP Camps

### Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky

**[[entities/people/binyamin-gorodetsky|Rabbi Binyamin Gorodetsky]]** was the Rayatz's primary representative in postwar Europe:

**Role**:

- Traveled between DP camps, assessing needs and delivering support
- Coordinated with Allied military authorities
- Organized religious, educational, and humanitarian programs
- Served as the Rayatz's eyes and ears on the ground

**Activities**:

- Established synagogues and schools
- Distributed funds and materials
- Provided spiritual guidance and encouragement
- Helped coordinate immigration

### Local Activists

**Chabad Survivors**:

- Many Chabad chassidim who survived the Holocaust naturally became activists
- They understood the community's needs
- They provided leadership in rebuilding religious life

**Survivors Who Became Religious**:

- Some survivors, secular before the war, became religious in the DP camps
- The experience of surviving the Holocaust led many to return to Judaism
- Chabad provided the framework for this return

## Life in the DP Camps

### Conditions

Despite being "liberated," conditions in DP camps were difficult:

**Physical Conditions**:

- Overcrowded barracks
- Inadequate food and supplies
- Disease and malnutrition persisted
- Psychological trauma was widespread

**Uncertainty**:

- Survivors didn't know what the future held
- Many had no homes to return to
- Immigration options were limited (particularly before 1948)

**Spiritual Crisis**:

- The Holocaust raised profound questions about faith
- Many struggled to believe in God after such suffering
- Religious revival was not automatic — it required active work

### The Role of Chabad

In this context, Chabad provided:

**Spiritual Answers**:

- Chassidic teachings addressed the problem of suffering
- The Rebbe's guidance provided direction
- Faith was presented as a choice, not merely a feeling

**Community**:

- Chabad created communal structures
- Survivors found community and connection
- Isolation was countered with belonging

**Purpose**:

- Survivors found meaning in rebuilding Jewish life
- Every religious observance was an act of defiance against the Nazis
- The survival of Judaism became a collective mission

## Immigration and Dispersion

### Departure from the DP Camps

Between 1945 and 1950, most DP camp survivors left:

**Palestine/Israel (1945–1948)**:

- **Aliyah** (immigration) to Palestine was restricted by the British
- Many went illegally (_Ha'apalah_ or _Aliyah Bet_)
- After Israel's establishment in 1948, immigration increased dramatically

**United States**:

- The U.S. relaxed immigration quotas for DPs
- Many survivors moved to America
- Chabad helped connect them with Jewish communities

**Other Destinations**:

- Canada, South America, Australia, and elsewhere
- Survivors dispersed globally

### The Great Escape Connection

Many of the Chabad chassidim who escaped from the Soviet Union in the **Great Escape (1946–1947)** passed through DP camps:

- **From Soviet Union** → **Poland** → **Germany/Austria DP camps** → **Palestine/America**
- In DP camps, they received support from Chabad activists
- They then continued to their final destinations

See [[events/great-escape|The Great Escape (1946–1947)]].

## Long-Term Impact

### Kfar Chabad

Many DP camp survivors who were influenced by Chabad eventually settled in **[[events/kfar-chabad-founding|Kfar Chabad]]** — the Chabad village in Israel founded in 1949:

- DP camp experience created a community of survivors bound by shared suffering
- Kfar Chabad became a home for many of these survivors
- The village's character was shaped by the DP camp experience

### Chabad's Postwar Expansion

The DP camp work established patterns for Chabad's postwar expansion:

**Global Reach**:

- Chabad activists learned to work in diverse environments
- They developed expertise in rebuilding Jewish communities
- This expertise was applied worldwide

**Outreach Model**:

- The DP camps proved that Chabad could reach even the most disconnected Jews
- Outreach to secular or assimilated Jews became a Chabad strength
- The shlichus (emissary) system built on this foundation

---

## Historical Significance

Chabad's work in the DP camps was significant for several reasons:

**Immediate Relief**:

- Provided essential support to survivors when few others would or could
- Helped survivors maintain their humanity and dignity
- Enabled religious and cultural survival

**Symbolic Importance**:

- Demonstrated that Jewish life would continue despite the Holocaust
- Every religious observance was an act of defiance
- The rebuilding of religious life was a victory over Nazism

**Institutional Growth**:

- Chabad emerged from the war with enhanced reputation and capacity
- The DP work established Chabad as a major force in postwar Jewish life
- This enabled the dramatic global expansion of subsequent decades

**Testament to Faith**:

- The DP camp work is a testament to the power of faith and community
- Despite experiencing the worst of humanity, survivors rebuilt
- Chabad provided the framework for this rebuilding
