# Soviet Persecution of Chabad (1917–1950)

**The Most Sustained Religious Persecution in Chabad History**

---

## Overview

The Soviet period (1917–1953, with peak anti-religious persecution in 1917–1950) represents the greatest existential threat the Chabad movement ever faced. It also produced the movement's most extraordinary demonstrations of _mesiras nefesh_ (self-sacrifice) for Torah and Jewish life — through underground yeshivos, secret circumcisions and mikvehs, clandestine Shabbat and holiday observance, and heroic individuals who risked imprisonment, exile, and death rather than abandon Jewish practice.

The story is documented across multiple books in this corpus, particularly [[summaries/zichronosai|זכרונותי]] (memoirs of the Soviet underground) and the broader [[summaries/toldot-chabad-russia|Chabad History in Czarist/Soviet Russia]] volume.

---

## The Yevsektsiya

The _Yevsektsiya_ (Евсекция — Jewish Section of the Communist Party) was the primary instrument of anti-religious persecution within the Jewish community. Composed of Jews who had embraced Bolshevism, it attacked Judaism from within — closing synagogues, prosecuting rabbis, shutting yeshivos, and actively campaigning to eliminate Jewish religious identity.

The Yevsektsiya understood that Chabad, with its emphasis on education and its tight-knit community networks, was the most resilient force for Jewish religious survival. It therefore targeted Chabad specifically and persistently.

## Phases of Persecution

### 1917–1921: Revolution and Civil War

Religious activity initially continued amid chaos. Chabad's Tomchei Temimim yeshiva network, founded by the [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rashab]] in 1897, attempted to maintain operations.

### 1921–1927: Systematic Suppression

The Yevsektsiya intensified. Yeshivos were forced underground. Individual Chabad activists were arrested. The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] organized a network of underground yeshivos — students moving between apartments, using fake identities, studying by night.

**The underground yeshiva pattern** (documented in [[summaries/zichronosai|זכרונותי]]): Students would gather secretly in private homes, sometimes only 5-10 at a time. When a location became unsafe, they relocated — moving between Vitebsk, Kiev, Moscow, Leningrad, Kharkov, and dozens of smaller cities. Teachers and students alike faced arrest.

**Named Incidents (1921–1927)**:

- **1924: Leningrad Synagogue Closures** — Multiple Chabad synagogues closed under Yevsektsiya pressure, including the Bolshoy Prospect synagogue converted to a workers' club
- **1925: Kiev Underground Raids** — GPU raids on private apartments where Tomchei Temimim students were studying; approximately 15 students arrested, teacher sentenced to Solovki where he died
- **1926: Kharkov Show Trial** — Several Chabad activists tried publicly for "operating illegal religious schools"; all sentenced to Siberian exile

See [[events/yevsektsiya-vs-chabad|Yevsektsiya vs. Chabad]] for extensive detail on this period.

### 1927: Arrest of the Rayatz

In June 1927, the GPU arrested the Rayatz in Leningrad. He was sentenced to death, then to Solovki camp, then to Kostroma exile — and ultimately released due to international pressure. He left the USSR for Riga, Latvia.

**Detailed Accounts**:

- [[events/rayatz-arrest-shavuot-1927|The Arrest on Shavuot Eve (1927)]] — Midnight arrest at 22 Machovaya Street
- [[events/shpalerka-prison-account|The Shpalerka Prison Account]] — Imprisonment and interrogations
- [[events/rescue-campaign-1927|International Rescue Campaign (1927)]] — Worldwide pressure for liberation

### 1927–1941: Underground Under Leaderless Conditions

With the Rayatz outside the USSR, dedicated Chabad activists continued underground activities across Soviet territory. Arrests, executions, and exile to Siberia claimed many lives.

**Named Incidents (1927–1941)**:

- **1929: Secret Mikveh in Moscow** — Underground ritual bath discovered and destroyed; users arrested
- **1930: Chernigol Yeshiva Raid** — Major underground yeshiva raided; six students arrested, three sentenced to Solovki
- **1935: Masher's Arrest** — Rabbi Shmuel Levitin (known as "the Masher"), key underground organizer, arrested in Leningrad; sentenced to five years
- **1937: Great Purge Impact** — Several Chabad activists disappeared during Stalin's Great Purge; presumed executed

### 1941–1945: World War II / Holocaust

German invasion devastated remaining Jewish communities including Chabad communities in Ukraine and Belarus. Thousands of Chabad chassidim were murdered in the Holocaust.

### 1946–1948: The Great Escape

After WWII, a mass emigration of surviving Chabad chassidim occurred from the Soviet Union, using the "repatriation" provisions for Polish Jews as cover. Many crossed borders illegally. The [[summaries/zichronosai|Zichronosai]] memoir describes this journey in detail — from Soviet Central Asia (Samarkand) through Poland, to displaced persons camps in Germany (Poking camp), to France (Paris), and finally to Israel (Kfar Chabad).

**Detailed Account**: [[events/great-escape-details|The Great Escape — Detailed Account (1946–1947)]]

## The Underground Yeshivos

The _Tomchei Temimim_ network in the Soviet Union operated underground from approximately 1921 to 1941 (and partially beyond). Key features:

- **Constant movement**: Students rarely stayed in one city for more than a few months
- **Cover stories**: Students registered as workers or students in secular institutions
- **Night learning**: Torah study often took place at night to avoid detection
- **Encoded communication**: Letters to the Rayatz used coded language
- **Female networks**: Chabad women played crucial roles in maintaining safe houses and supporting the underground

---

## Key Figures of Resistance

Many Chabad activists gave their lives or freedom in this period. The [[summaries/zikaron-lebnei-yisrael|Zikaron LeBnei Yisrael]] (Memorial Book) preserves many of their names.

---

## Legacy

The Soviet persecution produced a generation of Chabad leaders of extraordinary character — tested by genuine life-or-death choices, uncompromising in their commitment. When they arrived in the West and Israel, they formed the backbone of Chabad's postwar expansion. Many settled in [[entities/places/eretz-yisrael|Kfar Chabad]] (established 1949), bringing this spirit of self-sacrifice to their new communities.
