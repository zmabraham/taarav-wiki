# Malshin — Jewish Informers in Czarist Russia

**Hebrew Term**: מלשין (malshin), plural מלשינים (malshinim)  
**Also Known As**: Informers; denouncers; moser (plural: moserim)

---

## Overview

A malshin (informer) was a Jew who denounced fellow Jews to secular authorities, often for personal or ideological reasons. In the context of Czarist Russia, where the Jewish community had limited autonomy and lived under strict state regulation, informing was considered a grave sin and communal violation. The [[entities/people/simcha-kissin|Simcha Kissin]] case of 1825–1828 is a documented example of this phenomenon.

---

## Legal Framework

Russian Jewish law provided channels for denunciations:
- The 1804 Jewish Statute established state supervision over rabbinic appointments
- Jews could petition governors directly about alleged violations
- Informants could submit documents (in Russian translation) as evidence
- State authorities were required to investigate serious allegations

Simcha Kissin exploited this system by submitting Hebrew documents with Russian translations to [[entities/people/governor-khavanski|Governor Khavanski]], alleging illegal financial practices by the Mitteler Rebbe.

---

## Motivations

The sources for the 1825 case do not fully explain [[entities/people/simcha-kissin|Simcha Kissin's]] motivation, but several factors are identified as common in such cases:

### Personal Grievance
- Family disputes over inheritance or leadership
- Resentment against rabbinic authority
- Financial jealousy

### Ideological Opposition
- The **Mitnagdim–Chassidim conflict** that divided Jewish communities for decades
- Anti-Chassidic sentiment among "true holders of the Old Religion"
- Perception that Chassidic practices like polished knives or separate prayer services pressured traditional Jews

Kissin's denunciation explicitly framed his accusations as defending "true Jews who hold to the Old Religion" against the "Karlin sect or Chassidim" who "oppress the Jews."

### Financial Incentive
- In some periods, informers could receive a portion of confiscated property
- The Czarist state offered rewards for information about illegal activities
- Professional informers made a living from denunciations

---

## Historical Context

Informing was not unique to Chabad history or the 19th century. The Talmud and rabbinic literature discuss informers as a recurring communal danger. In the Russian Empire, where Jewish communal autonomy was limited and state power was absolute, a single denunciation could trigger a full imperial investigation — as happened in 1825.

The danger was amplified when informers obtained private documents from estates, as Kissin did with the late [[entities/people/pinchas-shik|Pinchas Shik's]] papers. This vulnerability reflected how family succession and estate matters could expose private records to hostile parties.

---

## The Kissin Case

[[entities/people/simcha-kissin|Simcha Kissin]] of Vitebsk filed three denunciations between June and July 1825, accusing [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|the Mitteler Rebbe]] of:
1. Collecting large sums illegally (the maavad system)
2. Exercising arbitration authority beyond rabbinic limits
3. Oppressing traditional Jews

He submitted Hebrew documents from Pinchas Shik's estate, including a distribution letter and personal letters from the Mitteler Rebbe. These materials, once translated, became the centerpiece of Governor Khavanski's investigation.

---

## Communal Response

The Chabad community's defense strategy in the 1825 case involved:
- Documenting that all donations were voluntary (not coerced)
- Showing the emperor had granted permission for Chassidic practice (the "freedom" of the Alter Rebbe's era)
- Presenting witness testimony from multiple communities
- Arguing that Kisin's allegations lacked legal basis

The final Imperial Senate decision (1827) noted that "no legal basis for the denunciation made by the Jew Kissin was found" and fully exonerated the Rebbe.

---

## Historical Significance

The Kissin case illustrates:
1. **The vulnerability** of Jewish communities to internal betrayal in an authoritarian state
2. **The power** of archival documentation (Kissin used estate papers as weapons)
3. **The resilience** of Chabad in defending its leadership through legal means
4. **The tragic role** that inter-Jewish conflict (Mitnagdim vs. Chassidim) could play when weaponized through state channels

The 10th of Kislev holiday commemorating the Mitteler Rebbe's liberation implicitly celebrates the defeat of this informer's plot.
