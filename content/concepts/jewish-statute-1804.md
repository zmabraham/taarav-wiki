# Jewish Statute of 1804

**Also Known As**: The Jewish Statute; Regulations for the Jews (Положение о евреях 1804 года)  
**Promulgated**: 9 December 1804 by Czar Alexander I

---

## Overview

The Jewish Statute of 1804 was the first comprehensive Russian imperial law governing Jewish life. It established the framework for Jewish communal administration, rabbinic appointments, economic activity, and settlement patterns. Sections 51 and 52 became central to the 1825 legal case against [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|the Mitteler Rebbe]], concerning rabbinic authority over financial collections and civil arbitration.

---

## Key Provisions

### Section 49–50: Communal Structure
- Established the **Kahal** (Jewish communal council) as the basic administrative unit
- Required state approval for rabbinic appointments
- Defined the kahal's authority to collect taxes

### Section 51: Rabbinic Authority Limits
Prohibited rabbis from:
- Exercising civil judicial authority
- Imposing fines or corporal punishment
- Interfering in secular civil disputes
This section was invoked by [[entities/people/governor-khavanski|Governor Khavanski]] to argue that [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|the Mitteler Rebbe's]] arbitration courts (beit din) exceeded legal authority.

### Section 52: Rabbinic Compensation
- Limited rabbis to a **fixed salary** from the kahal
- Required governor approval for the salary amount
- Prohibited rabbis from collecting additional funds or fees
This was the core allegation against the Rebbe: that the chassidic [[entities/concepts/maavad|financial support system]] (voluntary donations, not a fixed salary) violated this section.

### Section 10: Language Requirements
Required rabbis to know one of: Russian, Polish, or German
The Mitteler Rebbe's inability to read or write Russian (he signed documents in Hebrew) became a point of controversy — Khavanski argued he should be disqualified from serving as rabbi until he learned a state language.

---

## Application to the 1825 Case

The investigation repeatedly asked whether the Mitteler Rebbe:
1. Exceeded Section 51 by conducting arbitration (borrut) courts
2. Violated Section 52 by collecting funds beyond a fixed salary

Witness testimony established that:
- The Rebbe conducted voluntary arbitration by mutual consent of parties
- He did not receive a fixed salary but relied on voluntary donations
- Donations were given spontaneously without compulsion
- The chassidic community was not an officially appointed "rabbi" under the statute

---

## Khavanski's Proposed Reforms

Governor Khavanski used the 1825 case to argue that the 1804 statute needed strengthening:
1. **Strengthen Section 52**: Explicitly forbid any rabbinic collections beyond approved salaries
2. **Expand Section 51**: Prohibit rabbis from any civil arbitration whatsoever
3. **Governor approval**: Require governors to approve rabbinic salary contracts
4. **Enforcement**: Oblige governors to strictly supervise rabbinic activities

The Imperial Senate (1827–1828) partially adopted Khavanski's views — it instructed governors to enforce Sections 49, 51, and 52 strictly — but rejected his proposal to change the law itself.

---

## Broader Historical Context

The 1804 Statute reflected Enlightenment-era reform ideas:
- **Integrationist goals**: Bring Jews into state administrative structures
- **Regulation over autonomy**: Replace traditional communal self-governance with state control
- **Protection of subjects**: Shield Jews from communal "abuses" (like heavy taxation)

For Chabad, which operated outside the state-appointed rabbinate, these provisions created permanent legal vulnerability. The 1825 case tested whether a voluntary spiritual leadership model could survive within the statutory framework.
