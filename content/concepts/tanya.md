# Tanya — תניא

**The Written Torah of Chabad Chassidism**

---

## Overview

The _Tanya_ (officially _Likkutei Amarim_ — "Collected Sayings") is the foundational philosophical text of Chabad-Lubavitch, written by the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] (Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi). First published in Slavuta in **1796 (5557)**, it remains the most studied and reprinted Chabad text, with over 7,000 editions published in nearly every language and in locations on every continent (including Antarctica).

The Alter Rebbe called it _Sefer shel Beinonim_ — "The Book of Intermediate People" — addressing not the perfect tzaddik or the wicked person, but the _beinoni_: the normal, struggling person who wants to serve G-d but faces inner conflict.

---

## The Title

The word _Tanya_ is the first word of the book, from a Talmudic citation: "Tanya — it was taught in a Baraita..." The name stuck, though the formal title is _Likkutei Amarim_.

---

## Structure

The Tanya consists of five parts, each addressing a different dimension of Jewish spiritual life:

### Part One: Likkutei Amarim (The Main Text)

The first part, consisting of 53 chapters, is the heart of the Tanya. It addresses the fundamental question: How can an ordinary person serve G-d when they are torn between competing desires?

**Chapters 1–10: The Spiritual Anatomy**

- **Chapter 1** introduces the _beinoni_ as the book's central concern — not the perfect tzaddik or the wicked rasha, but the intermediate person who struggles
- **Chapters 2–5** explain the two souls: the _Nefesh HaElohit_ (divine soul) that desires G-d, and the _Nefesh HaBehamit_ (animal soul) that desires physical pleasure
- **Chapters 6–10** explore the ten faculties of each soul and how they manifest in thought, speech, and action

**Chapters 11–26: The Inner Battle**

- **Chapters 11–17** explain why the animal soul is not evil but merely animalistic — it can be harnessed for good
- **Chapters 18–25** provide the practical tools for winning the inner battle: Torah study, prayer with concentration (_kavanah_), and the cultivation of love and fear of G-d
- **Chapter 26** explains _bitul_ — self-nullification before G-d's will

**Chapters 27–34: The Divine Service of the Beinoni**

- How the beinoni serves G-d through constant Torah study
- The power of Torah to transform the animal soul
- The importance of reviewing one's learning

**Chapters 35–43: Controlling the Animal Soul**

- Practical strategies for dominating the animal soul
- The role of joy in divine service
- How even unintentional thoughts can be elevated

**Chapters 44–53: Tzaddikim and the Ultimate Purpose**

- The difference between the beinoni and the tzaddik
- The purpose of creation: G-d desired a dwelling in the lower worlds
- The messianic redemption as the culmination of divine service

### Part Two: Shaar HaYichud VeHaEmuna (Gate of Unity and Faith)

This section, consisting of 11 chapters, addresses the philosophical foundations of Jewish belief. It explains:

- **G-d's Unity (_Yichud_)**: G-d is absolutely one — not one in a numerical sense (which implies parts) but one in an absolute sense that transcends human comprehension
- **Creation Ex Nihilo**: The universe was created from nothingness by G-d's speech
- **Constant Creation**: G-d's creative force continuously sustains existence at every moment — without this constant influx, all would revert to nothingness
- **The Divine Immanence**: G-d's presence permeates all reality ("there is nothing besides Him") — yet G-d also transcends creation
- **The Panentheistic Doctrine**: Chabad's unique contribution to Jewish theology, articulating how G-d is both within the world and beyond it

This section provides the intellectual foundation for the spiritual practices described in Part One. When a Jew serves G-d, they are connecting to the G-d who is both transcendent and immanent.

### Part Three: Iggeret HaTeshuvah (Epistle of Repentance)

This section offers a comprehensive guide to _teshuvah_ (repentance/return) in 13 chapters plus 5 supplementary chapters. It addresses:

- **The Definition of Teshuvah**: Return to G-d through regret, confession, and resolution
- **The Universality of Sin**: Everyone sins; the important thing is to return
- **The Power of Teshuvah**: Sincere teshuvah can repair any spiritual damage, even intentional sins
- **The Role of Torah Study**: Torah study protects against sin and inspires teshhuvah
- **The Teshuvah of the Beinoni**: How the intermediate person does teshuvah
- **Teshuvah from Love vs. Fear**: Teshuvah from love transforms intentional sins into merits

The Alter Rebbe emphasizes that teshhuvah is not an extraordinary feat for extraordinary people, but a constant process for everyone. Every Jew can return to G-d, no matter how far they have strayed.

### Part Four: Iggeret HaKodesh (Holy Epistle)

This section consists of 32 letters that the Alter Rebbe wrote to his chassidim, addressing specific questions and concerns. The letters cover:

- **Marital Harmony**: The spiritual significance of marriage and the proper conduct between husband and wife
- **Charity**: The importance of giving tzedakah, even when one has little
- **Education**: How to raise children with Torah values
- **Community**: The responsibilities of communal leaders and members
- **Faith and Trust (_Bitachon_)**: Trusting in G-d's providence in all matters
- **Joy**: Serving G-d with joy, even in difficult circumstances

These letters demonstrate how the abstract principles of Chabad philosophy apply to concrete situations. They show the Alter Rebbe's pastoral wisdom and his deep understanding of human nature.

### Part Five: Kuntres Acharon (Final Treatise)

This section contains 14 chapters of advanced philosophical essays, addressing:

- **The Purpose of Creation**: Why G-d created the universe and what role humanity plays
- **The Divine Service of Tzaddikim**: How perfect tzaddikim serve G-d
- **The Difference Between the Beinoni and the Tzaddik**: A deeper exploration of this fundamental distinction
- **The Soul Garments**: How thought, speech, and action serve as "garments" for the soul
- **The Messianic Era**: The nature of the redemption and what it will look like

The Kuntres Acharon is the most philosophically sophisticated section of the Tanya, building on all that came before and addressing questions that arise from deep study of the earlier parts.

## Composition

The Tanya was not written as a single composition but compiled from the Alter Rebbe's responses to individual questions — hence "Likkutei Amarim" (Collected Sayings). Chassidim would bring their personal questions to the Rebbe; the Rebbe realized that similar questions recurred and that a book addressing the universal human spiritual struggle would serve everyone.

The Alter Rebbe reportedly said: "I cannot accommodate everyone with a private audience; let this book be their answer."

---

## The _Beinoni_ — The Book's Central Innovation

The Tanya's most radical contribution is its redefinition of the _beinoni_ (intermediate person):

- **Traditional definition**: someone whose merits and sins are equal
- **Tanya's definition**: someone who _never sins in action, speech, or thought_ — even momentarily — but whose animal soul still desires forbidden things

This _beinoni_ is not a tzaddik (whose animal soul has been transformed) but someone who controls the animal soul despite its continued existence. Crucially, the Alter Rebbe says: **every person can become a beinoni if they choose to**. This democratizes spiritual aspiration — you don't need to be a tzaddik; you just need to not give in.

The Tanya explains that the beinoni achieves this level through:

- **Constant Torah study**: Torah study illuminates the mind and strengthens the divine soul
- **Prayer with concentration**: Prayer connects the person to G-d and awakens love and fear
- **Mindfulness (_zehirus_)**: Being aware of one's thoughts and not allowing them to stray
- **Review of one's actions**: Examining one's behavior daily and correcting mistakes

The revolutionary aspect of this teaching is that it makes spiritual excellence accessible to everyone. You don't need to be born a tzaddik; you can become a beinoni through effort and dedication.

---

## The Two Souls

The Tanya's analysis of the human inner world is one of its most influential contributions:

**The Divine Soul** (_Nefesh HaElohit_): A "piece of G-d from above" — literally a part of the divine. It has ten faculties (three intellectual: chochma/bina/da'at; seven emotional: chesed/gevurah/tiferet/netzach/hod/yesod/malchut) and desires only holiness, Torah, and connection with G-d.

**The Animal Soul** (_Nefesh HaBehamit_): Not evil but animalistic — it derives from the _kelipat nogah_ (the "shell that has a spark of holiness"). It desires physical pleasure, status, comfort, and survival. When channeled properly, it powers love and enthusiasm for mitzvos. When misused, it drives sin.

The Tanya explains that these two souls are in constant tension. Every thought, word, and action is won by one side or the other. The goal is not to destroy the animal soul but to harness it for divine service — to transform its energy from selfish to selfless purposes.

This analysis provides a nuanced understanding of human psychology that avoids both demonization of the body and naive celebration of physical desires. The body is not evil; it is a vessel that can be elevated.

---

## Bitul (Self-Nullification) — The Spiritual Methodology

The Tanya introduces the concept of _bitul_ — self-nullification before G-d's will. This is not self-abnegation but the transcendence of ego in service of something greater. Through bitul, a person aligns their will with G-d's will.

Bitul is achieved through:

- **Intellectual apprehension**: Understanding that G-d is the true reality
- **Emotional surrender**: Feeling one's smallness before G-d's greatness
- **Practical application**: Acting in accordance with G-d's will, not one's own desires

The Tanya teaches that bitul is the foundation of all divine service. Without it, even Torah study and prayer can become ego-driven. With it, every action becomes a connection to G-d.

---

## The Purpose of Creation: A Dwelling in the Lower Worlds

One of the Tanya's most famous teachings concerns the purpose of creation: G-d desired a "dwelling place in the lower worlds." What does this mean?

G-d is inherently perfect and needs nothing. Therefore, creation cannot add to G-d's perfection. Instead, creation is an expression of G-d's desire to be known by beings who are not G-d — to have a relationship with creatures who have free will and can choose to recognize G-d or not.

The "lower worlds" refer to our physical world, which is the lowest of all spiritual planes. By transforming this material world into a place where G-d's presence is revealed, we fulfill the purpose of creation. This is why the Tanya emphasizes practical action — mitzvos performed in the physical world — over purely spiritual achievements.

---

## Mitzvot as the Bridge Between Soul and Body

The Tanya provides a sophisticated understanding of mitzvot (commandments):

- **Mitzvot with the body**: Physical actions performed according to Torah law connect the physical body to G-d
- **Mitzvot with the heart**: Emotional mitzvot (love, fear, joy) connect the emotions to G-d
- **Mitzvot with the mind**: Intellectual mitzvot (Torah study, belief) connect the mind to G-d

The Tanya teaches that every mitzvah has a physical aspect and a spiritual aspect. The physical action draws down divine light into the world; the spiritual intention (kavanah) connects the soul to that light.

This understanding explains why Chabad places such emphasis on the detailed performance of mitzvot — it is not legalism but a recognition that the physical action itself has cosmic significance.

---

## Prayer as the Ladder of the Soul

The Tanya provides a detailed analysis of prayer (_tefillah_) as the primary means of spiritual elevation:

- **The structure of prayer**: The daily prayers correspond to the ascent of the soul through spiritual worlds
- **Concentration (_kavanah_)**: The intention behind the words is more important than the words themselves
- **The struggle for focus**: The Tanya acknowledges how difficult it is to maintain concentration during prayer and offers practical strategies

Prayer, in the Tanya's system, is not just a obligation but an opportunity — the moment when the soul can ascend and draw closer to G-d. This is why Chabad emphasizes long, meditative prayer, even when it is difficult.

---

## Love and Fear of G-d

The Tanya explores the emotions of love (_ahavah_) and fear (_yirah_) of G-d in depth:

- **Natural love**: Every Jew has a natural love for G-d, inherited from the patriarchs and matriarchs
- **Intellectual love**: Love that comes from understanding G-d's greatness
- **Fear of awe**: Reverence for G-d's majesty
- **Fear of sin**: Fear of the consequences of transgressing G-d's will

The Tanya teaches that these emotions can be cultivated through study and meditation. They are not mystical gifts but the natural result of understanding who G-d is and what our relationship to G-d is.

---

## The Tzaddik: The Rare Spiritual Elite

While the Tanya focuses on the beinoni, it also describes the tzaddik — the perfectly righteous person:

- The tzaddik's animal soul has been completely transformed; it no longer desires anything contrary to G-d's will
- The tzaddik serves G-d with _ratzo v'shov_ — running and returning, oscillating between transcendence and immanence
- The tzaddik's Torah study and prayer have cosmic effects, drawing divine blessing into the world

The Tanya makes it clear that tzaddikim are rare. Most people will be beinonim, not tzaddikim. But the existence of tzaddikim provides spiritual sustenance for the entire Jewish people.

---

## Joy in Divine Service

One of the Tanya's most distinctive teachings is the importance of joy (_simchah_) in serving G-d:

- Sadness and depression are tools of the animal soul; they prevent divine service
- Joy breaks all barriers and enables a person to serve G-d with enthusiasm
- Joy comes from recognizing the privilege of serving G-d

The Tanya quotes the verse, "Serve G-d with joy" (Psalms 100:2), and explains that this is not merely aspirational but essential. Joyless service is incomplete service.

This teaching has had a profound influence on Chabad culture, which is known for its joyful approach to Judaism — even in difficult circumstances.

## Publication History

- **1796 (5557)**: First edition, Slavuta — printed without the Alter Rebbe's explicit permission
- **1797 (5557)**: Second edition, with the Alter Rebbe's corrections and additions
- **Copied and circulated in manuscript** for years before publication — chassidim would hand-copy the text
- **1814**: Torah Or (Alter Rebbe's discourses on Chumash) published posthumously — complements the Tanya
- **20th century**: Kehot Publication Society prints millions of copies worldwide in dozens of languages
- **Current**: Over 7,000 editions; printed at the North Pole, on ships, in hospitals, in remote villages — a Chabad custom to print Tanya wherever Jews are found

The publication history of the Tanya reflects its importance to Chabad. The fact that it was printed so widely and so early — in a time when Jewish books were expensive and rare — testifies to how central it was from the beginning.

## Study and Practice

The Tanya is not just studied; it is lived. Chabad has developed several practices that integrate the Tanya into daily life:

**Chitas**: The daily study cycle of Chumash, Tehillim, and Tanya. The Tanya is divided into daily portions corresponding to the days of the year. Many Chabad chassidim study the day's Tanya portion each morning.

**Tanya classes**: Almost every Chabad institution holds daily Tanya classes, often early in the morning before work. These classes provide communal reinforcement for individual study.

**Memorization**: Advanced students at Tomchei Temimim yeshivos are expected to memorize all 53 chapters of Part One. Many Chabad chassidim can recite significant portions by heart.

**Practical application**: The Tanya is studied not just for intellectual stimulation but for practical guidance. Chassidim will consult the Tanya when facing spiritual challenges, looking for the Alter Rebbe's guidance on their situation.

---

## Significance in Chabad History

The Tanya is not merely a book — it is the intellectual DNA of Chabad:

- **Every Chabad concept** (beinoni, bitul, hiskashrus, etc.) traces to the Tanya
- **The Alter Rebbe's arrest (1798)** was in part about Tanya-based ideas the government found threatening
- **The Tanya was the primary curriculum addition** that distinguished Tomchei Temimim from other yeshivos
- **The seventh Rebbe's sichos frequently cite** and expand on specific Tanya chapters — he would often say "see Tanya chapter X" when addressing a question
- **The global spread of Chabad** has been accompanied by the global spread of the Tanya — it is now studied in virtually every language

The Tanya represents the synthesis of the intellectual, emotional, and practical dimensions of Judaism. It is a book that speaks to the head, the heart, and the hands — offering a vision of Jewish life that is philosophically sophisticated, spiritually rich, and practically actionable.
