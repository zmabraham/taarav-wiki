# Basi L'Gani — באתי לגני

**The Mission Statement of the Seventh Rebbe's Leadership**

---

## Overview

_Basi L'Gani_ ("I have come to My garden") is the [[concepts/maamar|maamar]] (Chassidic discourse) delivered by the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]], Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, on **Yud Shevat 5711 (January 17, 1951)** — exactly one year after the passing of his father-in-law, the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] — at [[entities/places/770|770 Eastern Parkway]] in Brooklyn, New York. By delivering this maamar, the seventh Rebbe formally accepted the leadership of Chabad-Lubavitch.

The title is drawn from Song of Songs 5:1: "_Basi le'gani achoti kallah_" — "I have come to My garden, My sister, My bride." In the Rebbe's interpretation, the verse speaks of G-d's presence descending into the physical world — the "garden" — and the responsibility of every Jew to reveal that hidden divinity through Torah and mitzvos. This maamar is universally regarded as the mission statement of the seventh Rebbe's forty-three-year leadership and the theological foundation for the global [[concepts/shlichus|shlichus]] enterprise that would transform Chabad from a devastated post-Holocaust community into a worldwide movement.

---

## The Historical Context

The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] had passed away on 10 Shevat 5710 (January 28, 1950). A year of mourning and uncertainty followed, during which the Rebbe repeatedly and adamantly refused calls to assume leadership, maintaining that the proper successor was his brother-in-law, Rabbi Shmaryahu Gurary (the Rashag). Chabad chassidim, having experienced the Rayatz's leadership as their anchor through the Holocaust and the destruction of European Jewry, felt spiritually adrift.

On Yud Shevat 5711, a farbrengen was held at 770 to mark the first yahrzeit. Elder Chassid Rabbi Sender Nemtzov, one of the last surviving students of the Tzemach Tzedek, called out: "Reb Mendel, say a maamar!" After a moment of silence, the Rebbe ascended to the platform. He chose as his text the last maamar the Rayatz had published before his death — a discourse also titled _Basi L'Gani_. As the Rebbe spoke, building on the Rayatz's words and expanding them with his own commentary, the atmosphere transformed. When Rabbi Nemtzov leaped onto a table and proclaimed, "Chassidim, we have a Rebbe!" the assembled Chassidim broke into spontaneous song and dance. See [[events/yud-shevat-1951|Yud Shevat 5711 (1951)]] and [[events/seventh-rebbe-acceptance-1951|Seventh Rebbe Accepts Leadership (1951)]] for detailed accounts.

## Continuity with the Rayatz's Final Maamar

The Rebbe's choice of _Basi L'Gani_ was not coincidental — it was a deliberate act of spiritual continuity. The Rayatz had published a maamar on this same verse as his last discourse before his passing, and the seventh Rebbe's maamar was a direct continuation, picking up from the exact conceptual point where the Rayatz's discourse ended. This gesture communicated two messages simultaneously: unbroken transmission from master to disciple, and the beginning of a new era with its own distinct voice.

The Rebbe would later deliver an annual maamar on _Basi L'Gani_ each Yud Shevat for the remainder of his leadership, each year exploring a different dimension of the same verse. These annual discourses became one of the most important teaching cycles in the seventh Rebbe's Torah, mapping the spiritual priorities of each year of his leadership back to this foundational text.

## Core Teachings

The maamar develops several interconnected themes from the Song of Songs verse:

**The Garden as the Physical World**: The "garden" (_gan_) in the verse represents the physical world — the lowest plane of creation, where divinity is most concealed. The verse's declaration "I have come to My garden" expresses G-d's desire to dwell precisely in this realm of concealment, not in the higher spiritual worlds where His presence is obvious.

**The Seven Tzaddikim, the Seven Shepherds**: The maamar connects to a Midrash that lists seven righteous individuals — from Adam through Moshe — each of whom served G-d in a particular mode. The seventh Rebbe framed his own leadership as the seventh generation of Chabad (from the Alter Rebbe through the Rayatz) in parallel to these seven figures, suggesting that the seventh generation carries a unique responsibility to bring the divine plan to completion.

**The Mission of the Seventh Generation**: Drawing on the Rayatz's original maamar, the Rebbe articulated the task of the seventh generation: to transform the "garden" — the physical world — into a dwelling place for G-d through the performance of mitzvos with physical objects. This was not abstract theology but a call to action that would define the shlichus model: sending emissaries to every corner of the world to engage Jews in Torah and mitzvos.

**LeAlter LeGeulah — Immediately to Redemption**: The Rebbe concluded the maamar with the declaration "LeAlter leGeulah" — calling for the immediate coming of the Messiah. This urgency, expressed at the very outset of his leadership, would characterize his entire tenure. The Rebbe consistently taught that the redemption was not a distant hope but an imminent possibility, achievable through the cumulative impact of individual mitzvos performed by Jews everywhere.

---

## Significance in Chabad History

_Basi L'Gani_ is not merely the first maamar of the seventh Rebbe — it is the theological blueprint for everything that followed. The global expansion of [[concepts/shlichus|shlichus]], the Mitzvah Campaigns, the emphasis on reaching every Jew regardless of affiliation or observance, the belief in the transformative power of a single mitzvah — all trace their conceptual roots to this discourse.

The maamar established the seventh Rebbe's leadership on the principle of continuity with his predecessors while simultaneously introducing a dramatically expanded vision of what Chabad could become. Where the Rayatz had preserved Chabad through Soviet persecution and wartime devastation, the seventh Rebbe declared that the task of the seventh generation was not merely survival but transformation — to make the entire world a dwelling place for G-d.
