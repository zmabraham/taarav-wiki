# 770 Eastern Parkway (Chabad Headquarters)

**770 Eastern Parkway, Crown Heights, Brooklyn — Chabad-Lubavitch world headquarters since 1940; spiritual and organizational center of the global Chabad movement; known simply as "770."**

---

## Overview

770 Eastern Parkway in Crown Heights, Brooklyn, New York, is the world headquarters of the Chabad-Lubavitch movement. The three-story red-brick building was acquired by the Rayatz (Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn) in 1940 after his escape from Nazi-occupied Warsaw and his arrival in the United States. Since that year, 770 has served as the administrative, spiritual, and symbolic center of Chabad worldwide.

The building's address has become so synonymous with Chabad that it is known universally in the movement simply as "770" (pronounced "seven-seventy"). Such is its significance that dozens of Chabad centers around the world have been built as architectural replicas of 770 — a form of devotional homage to the original. The building houses the main Chabad synagogue, study halls, offices, and the apartment where both the Rayatz and later the seventh Rebbe lived and worked.

The Rayatz arrived in New York on March 9, 1940, having been rescued from Nazi-occupied Poland through American diplomatic intervention. Initially setting up in a smaller location, Chabad acquired 770 Eastern Parkway later that year. The choice of Crown Heights — then a middle-class neighborhood in Brooklyn — as the home of the movement's headquarters reflected both practical considerations and what became, in retrospect, a pivotal decision in the geography of American Jewish life.

## Significance in Chabad History

770 represents the American chapter of Chabad — the movement's transplantation from the destroyed world of Eastern European Jewry to the new world. The Rayatz's declaration upon his arrival in America — "America is not different" — was both a statement of principle and a challenge: the comforts and freedoms of American life should not be allowed to dilute the commitment to Torah observance and Chassidic life. 770 became the center from which this principle was put into practice.

Under the Rayatz (1940–1950), 770 housed the nascent institutions of American Chabad: Kehot Publication Society (Chabad's publishing arm), Merkos L'Inyonei Chinuch (the educational organization), and Machne Israel (the social services organization). Students came from around the world to be near the Rebbe; survivors of the Holocaust arrived and rebuilt their lives in the Crown Heights community; the movement was reconstituting itself from the ashes of European destruction.

Under the seventh Rebbe (1950–1994), 770 became the epicenter of a global expansion unprecedented in Jewish communal history. The weekly farbrengens (Chassidic gatherings) at 770 drew hundreds and were transmitted around the world; letters from the Rebbe to individuals, communities, and world leaders emanated from 770; and the shlichus (emissary) movement that sent thousands of young Chabad couples to every corner of the globe was directed from this building.
