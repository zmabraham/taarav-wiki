# Dnepropetrovsk / Yekaterinoslav

**Major city in central Ukraine; served as the rabbinate of Rabbi Levi Yitzchak Schneersohn (father of the 7th Rebbe); known as Yekaterinoslav during the imperial period.**

---

## Overview

Dnepropetrovsk (known as Yekaterinoslav / Ekaterinoslav during the Czarist period, and today called Dnipro) is one of Ukraine's major cities, situated on the Dnieper River in central Ukraine. It had a significant Jewish population — one of the largest in Ukraine — making it an important center of Jewish communal life.

The city's significance in Chabad history centers on its role as the rabbinate of Rabbi Levi Yitzchak Schneersohn (1878–1944), father of Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn (the seventh Rebbe). Rabbi Levi Yitzchak served as the city's chief rabbi, navigating the extraordinary challenges of serving a Jewish community under Soviet rule. He maintained this position even after the Bolshevik Revolution and through the Sovietization of Ukrainian Jewish life, until his arrest by the NKVD in 1939.

His wife Rebbetzin Chana and their son Menachem Mendel lived in Yekaterinoslav/Dnepropetrovsk for portions of this period, making it part of the seventh Rebbe's personal biography as well. The city's large Jewish community, its rabbinic culture, and its eventual fate under the Holocaust are all part of the broader context of Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's work.

## Significance in Chabad History

Yekaterinoslav/Dnepropetrovsk was the setting for Rabbi Levi Yitzchak Schneersohn's heroic rabbinate — serving a major Jewish community under increasingly dangerous Soviet conditions. His continued public functioning as a rabbi in the face of Soviet anti-religious policy was itself an act of defiance and devotion.

The city's Jewish community was decimated in the Holocaust. When Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, the Jews of Dnepropetrovsk faced mass murder. The community that Rabbi Levi Yitzchak had served was largely destroyed, and he himself had already been arrested and sent to exile in Kazakhstan in 1939, where he died in 1944 before the end of the war.

The seventh Rebbe's connection to this city — through his father's rabbinate and his own time there — made it part of his personal history. The manuscripts of Likkutei Levi Yitzchak, his father's scholarly work, were preserved by his mother Rebbetzin Chana and eventually brought to the West, becoming a published work of Kabbalistic scholarship.
