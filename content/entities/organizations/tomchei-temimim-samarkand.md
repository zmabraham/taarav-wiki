# Tomchei Temimim in Samarkand (Wartime Underground Yeshiva)

During World War II (1941–1946), Samarkand in Soviet Uzbekistan became the center of the underground Chabad yeshiva network. A large concentration of Chabad families and yeshiva students had fled there from the war zone, reconstituting the Tomchei Temimim yeshiva under extremely difficult conditions.

## Description
When the Chabad community fled eastward after the German invasion of June 1941, Samarkand absorbed a large portion of the underground network. The relative remoteness from Moscow, the wartime chaos, and the concentration of many Jews from elsewhere meant that local authorities were less familiar with individual community members — providing more cover for clandestine religious activity.

In Samarkand, the Tomchei Temimim yeshiva was re-established in underground form. Learning continued, children received religious education, and the community maintained Shabbos and kashrut observance as best they could. The memoir records that R. Moshe Katzman, a member of the community, was involved in educating orphaned children — specifically children brought from elsewhere who needed care.

Significant figures in Samarkand during this period included:
- **R. Mendel Futerfas** — later famous for his years in Soviet labor camps; was in Samarkand during the war
- **R. Abba Pliskin** — active in the underground community
- **R. Yisrael Yehuda Levin** — the memoirist; lived under the alias "Naftali Bat" to evade military draft
- **Mikhael Teitelboim** — member of the community
- **Manie Dobravsky** and family — active in the community
- **Asher Sasonkin** — married R. Yisrael Yehuda's sister Freyde there

The community also harbored those evading military conscription. R. Yisrael Yehuda had been sought by draft authorities and lived under his Polish identity "Naftali Bat" — documents obtained from a Polish refugee named Waldman.

After the war, most of the community escaped through the Soviet–Polish repatriation scheme (1946), with members obtaining Polish identity documents to leave as "repatriating Poles." This dispersed the Samarkand community to Poland and eventually to Western Europe and Eretz Yisrael.
