# NKVD / GPU (Soviet Secret Police)

The Soviet secret police — known at various times as the GPU, OGPU, NKVD, and other acronyms — was the primary instrument of Soviet political repression. For the underground Chabad community, the NKVD represented an existential threat, conducting arrests, interrogations, and deportations of religious Jews.

## Key Facts
| Field | Value |
|-------|-------|
| GPU | State Political Directorate (Gosudarsvennoye Politicheskoye Upravleniye), 1922–1934 |
| NKVD | People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs (Narodnyi Komissariat Vnutrennikh Del), 1934–1943 |

## Description
Throughout the memoir, the secret police are referred to as "NKVD" or by the Hebrew acronym "Nekvid" (נקביד). The organization changed names multiple times — from Cheka to GPU to OGPU to NKVD — but its functions remained consistent: political surveillance, arrest, interrogation, and deportation of those deemed enemies of the Soviet state.

For underground Chabad chassidim, the NKVD represented constant danger. Teaching Torah, maintaining a yeshiva, and practicing Judaism were all potentially criminal acts. The memoir records several direct encounters:

- **Kyiv synagogue raid**: Plainclothes NKVD agents entered the women's gallery of a Kyiv synagogue where yeshiva students were studying and arrested several students, including Moshe Binyamin Kaplan and Moshe Aharon Geisinsky.

- **Post-raid interrogation**: The NKVD interrogators — apparently Jewish and sympathetic — returned documents and effectively warned the arrested students to flee. A lawyer's assessment: each deserved "not less than five years."

- **Constant surveillance**: Throughout the 1930s, a young man matching R. Yisrael Yehuda's description was apparently sought by the NKVD, contributing to his need to constantly move between cities and eventually use a Polish identity.

- **Samarkand period**: In Samarkand, obtaining a Polish identity under the name "Naftali Bat" allowed R. Yisrael Yehuda to evade both military draft authorities and the NKVD.
