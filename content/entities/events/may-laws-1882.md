# May Laws of 1882

Czar Alexander III instituted the May Laws in 1882, heavily restricting Jewish residency, economic activity, and educational opportunities in the Russian Empire. The laws came in the wake of Alexander II's assassination in 1881, after which Alexander III rolled back liberal reforms and intensified oppression of Russian Jews.

## Description

Following the assassination of Czar Alexander II in 1881 — in which Jewish radicals were falsely implicated in the popular imagination — Alexander III used the pretext to dramatically increase state persecution of Russian Jews. The May Laws of 1882 prohibited Jews from:

- Settling in rural areas even within the Pale of Settlement
- Conducting business on Sundays and Christian holidays
- Holding leases or mortgages on land

The laws accelerated Jewish emigration from Russia and intensified the poverty and restriction under which communities like the Chabad center of Lubavitch operated. They formed part of the broader oppressive context in which the Rashab established Tomchei Temimim (1897) and in which the young Rayatz came of age.

## Broader Context

The May Laws were compounded by Alexander III's death in 1894 and succession by Nicholas II, who continued similarly oppressive policies, and by the institution of violent pogroms. The Pale of Settlement restrictions would only end with the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917.
