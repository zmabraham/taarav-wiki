# Rebbetzin Chaya Mushka Schneersohn

**Second daughter of the Rayatz; wife of Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn (7th Rebbe); a woman of remarkable refinement who supported her husband's world-historic leadership from a position of dignified privacy.**

---

## Overview

Rebbetzin Chaya Mushka Schneersohn was born in 1901 as the second daughter of Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (the Rayatz) and Rebbetzin Nechama Dina. She grew up in the Chabad court — first in Lubavitch and then in Rostov-on-Don — absorbing the world of Chassidic leadership from childhood. Her marriage to Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn on 14 Kislev 5689 (November 27, 1928) in Warsaw was celebrated as one of the great events in modern Chabad history, a "royal wedding" that united the son-in-law with the father-in-law's daughter in the tradition of Chabad dynastic marriages.

The wedding date, 14 Kislev, became a permanent date of celebration in the Chabad calendar, observed annually with farbrengens and joy. The young couple lived in Berlin and Paris during the 1930s, where the Ramash pursued studies in science and engineering. When Nazi Germany occupied Poland in 1939 and Warsaw came under German control, Chaya Mushka was part of the family that escaped in early 1940 — a rescue operation orchestrated through American diplomatic intervention — and arrived in New York. She shared her husband's life in Brooklyn for over five decades, maintaining an intensely private demeanor even as her husband became one of the most public Jewish figures in the world. She passed away on 22 Shvat 5748 (February 10, 1988), six years before her husband.

## Life and Role

Chaya Mushka navigated a unique dual position: born to one Rebbe and married to another. Her upbringing in the Chabad court gave her an intimate understanding of what it meant to be the Rebbetzin — the demands, the public expectations, the weight of leadership on a family. Yet she is remembered for having carved out a sphere of private life, maintaining boundaries between her public role and personal identity that were unusual and, in hindsight, clearly deliberate.

Her escape from Nazi-occupied Warsaw was one of the dramatic episodes in the broader story of how the Rayatz and his family were rescued in early 1940. She was among the family members who traveled through wartime Europe to reach America. Once in Brooklyn, she set up a quiet, dignified home that became, in the Chabad tradition, a place of welcome. The seventh Rebbe's public persona was matched by a private life of refinement and depth, and Chaya Mushka was the center of that private world.

After her passing on 22 Shvat 5748, the Rebbe's grief was palpable; he was visibly shaken in the public farbrengen that followed, and 22 Shvat has become a day of memory in the Chabad calendar. The Rebbetzin's legacy is one of quiet strength — a woman who inhabited a world-historic role with grace and without seeking the spotlight.
