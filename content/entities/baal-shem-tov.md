# Rabbi Yisrael Baal Shem Tov

**Founder of the Chassidic movement; teacher of the Maggid of Mezeritch; the spiritual foundation upon which Chabad-Lubavitch was built.**

---

## Overview

Rabbi Yisrael Baal Shem Tov (1698–1760), commonly known as the Baal Shem Tov (Master of the Good Name) or by the acronym Besht, was the founder of the Chassidic movement in Judaism. Born in Okopy (modern Ukraine) around 1698, he revealed his spiritual gifts and teachings publicly around 1736, having spent years as a hidden mystic and teacher. His emphasis on the divine immanence in all of creation, the spiritual value of simple Jews, the importance of joy in the service of G-d, and the centrality of prayer as intimate connection transformed the landscape of Eastern European Jewish religious life.

The Baal Shem Tov's primary successor was Rabbi DovBer of Mezeritch (the Maggid), who became the central transmitter of Chassidism to the next generation. Among the Maggid's leading disciples was Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi — the Alter Rebbe — who founded Chabad. The Baal Shem Tov thus stands as the spiritual grandfather of Chabad-Lubavitch: his teachings flowed through the Maggid to the Alter Rebbe and from there into the specific Chabad synthesis of Chassidic thought with intellectual rigor.

The Baal Shem Tov passed away on Shavuot 5520 (1760) in Medzhibozh (modern Ukraine). He left no written works of his own — his teachings were transmitted orally and later recorded by disciples. The most famous of these collections is Keter Shem Tov. His legacy is foundational not only to Chabad but to all branches of Chassidism, which spread from the Baal Shem Tov's initial circle to encompass hundreds of courts and millions of followers.

## Life and Role

The Baal Shem Tov's central theological insight — that the divine presence fills all of creation and that every Jew, regardless of learning level, can achieve genuine closeness to G-d through sincere prayer and joy — was revolutionary. In an era when Jewish religious authority was heavily concentrated in scholarly achievement and Talmudic learning, the Baal Shem Tov opened a path of connection accessible to simple Jews who lacked the time or ability for deep study.

His teachings emphasized: the divine immanence in all things (there is no place devoid of G-d); the importance of serving G-d with joy (sadness is a significant spiritual obstacle); the spiritual dignity of every Jew regardless of learning; the power of stories and tales to transmit spiritual truths; and the centrality of genuine, heartfelt prayer. These themes became the bedrock of Chassidism in all its branches.

The Baal Shem Tov's influence on Chabad, while mediated through the Maggid and then the Alter Rebbe, is fundamental. The Alter Rebbe's Tanya opens with a quotation from the Baal Shem Tov and builds its entire system of Chassidic psychology on the foundations the Baal Shem Tov laid. The Frierdiker Rebbe (Rayatz) frequently invoked the Baal Shem Tov's legacy in his own teachings, particularly in the context of the value of every individual Jew and the importance of outreach.
