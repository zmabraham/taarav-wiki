# Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn (Maharash)

**Fourth Lubavitcher Rebbe; youngest son of the Tzemach Tzedek; known for his "Lechatchila Ariber" teaching; father of the Rashab.**

---

## Overview

Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn (1834–1882), known as the Maharash (an acronym of Moreinu HaRav Shmuel — Our Teacher Rabbi Shmuel), was the fourth Lubavitcher Rebbe. He was the youngest of the Tzemach Tzedek's seven sons, and upon his father's passing in 1866, he assumed the leadership of Chabad in Lubavitch while his brothers led separate courts elsewhere.

The Maharash is perhaps best known for his teaching expressed in the phrase "Lechatchila Ariber" — "from the outset, go over [the obstacle]." The concept captures his approach to challenges: rather than working around or under an obstacle, a person should begin at the highest level, going "over" it from the start. This teaching has become one of the most cited Chabad aphorisms and reflects the Maharash's characteristically bold and forward-moving leadership style.

His leadership lasted only sixteen years before his death at age 48 in 1882. During this period, he led with energy and vision, strengthening Chabad networks and maintaining the court in Lubavitch. His son Rabbi Shalom DovBer (the Rashab) succeeded him as the fifth Rebbe. The Rashab's extraordinary scholarly work — including the Hemshech Taarav — was deeply influenced by his father's approach.

## Life and Role

The Maharash's assumption of leadership in 1866 came amid the transition following the Tzemach Tzedek's death. As the youngest son who remained in Lubavitch, he was the one who maintained the primary court while his brothers spread to other cities. This made Lubavitch, once again, the clear center of Chabad identity.

His "Lechatchila Ariber" teaching is best understood in its broader context: the Maharash taught that the Chassidic way is not to calculate how to minimize confrontation with an obstacle but to elevate oneself — through genuine connection to G-d — to a level where the obstacle simply does not exist as a barrier. This is not mere optimism but a spiritual prescription: through Chassidic work (avodah), one raises oneself above the plane on which problems exist.

His relatively brief leadership (16 years) contrasts with the long tenures of his father (40+ years) and his predecessors. Yet he left a significant mark through his teachings and through the character of his son, who became one of the most intellectually formidable Rebbes in Chabad history. The Rashab absorbed his father's boldness and combined it with extraordinary analytical depth — a combination that produced the Hemshech Taarav and the founding of Tomchei Temimim.
