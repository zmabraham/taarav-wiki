# Jan Zwartendijk

**Born**: October 31, 1896, Papendrecht, Netherlands  
**Died**: September 14, 1976, Rotterdam, Netherlands  
**Role**: Dutch consul in Kaunas (Kovno), Lithuania; stamped passports with "no visa required to Curacao," opening the escape route to Shanghai

---

## Overview

Jan Zwartendijk was the Dutch acting consul in Kaunas (Kovno), Lithuania, who in 1940 began stamping Jewish refugees' passports with a statement that "no visa is required" to enter the Dutch colony of Curacao. This seemingly small bureaucratic act opened an escape route — through Japan to Shanghai — that saved thousands of Jews including approximately 40 Chabad students.

---

## The Curacao Stamp

In the summer of 1940, as Nazi Germany overran Western Europe and Soviet forces occupied the Baltic states, Jewish refugees in Lithuania found themselves trapped. The window of escape was closing.

Jan Zwartendijk, who represented Dutch commercial interests in Lithuania (he worked for Philips), was appointed acting Dutch consul. A Jewish refugee approached him with a creative question: did Curacao (a Dutch Caribbean colony) require an entry visa? Technically, it did not — it required only the governor's permission upon arrival, which was entirely different from a formal visa.

Zwartendijk began stamping passports with: _"No visa required for entry into Surinam, Curaçao, and other Netherlands colonies."_ He eventually removed the Surinam reference but kept the Curacao notation. This stamp gave refugees a theoretical destination — not that anyone would actually go to Curacao, but it gave them something to show other consular officials.

---

## The Route: Lithuania → Japan → Shanghai

The Curacao stamp was only half the equation. The other half was provided by [[entities/people/chiune-sugihara|Chiune Sugihara]], the Japanese consul in Kaunas, who issued transit visas for Japan. Together, the two stamps created a route:

**Vilna → Moscow → Trans-Siberian Railway → Vladivostok → Kobe, Japan → Shanghai**

Chabad students among the first to use this route; eventually ~40 Chabad students and ~200 Mir Yeshiva students spent the war in Shanghai. The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] in Brooklyn coordinated logistics and funding from his end.

See [[events/shanghai-rescue-1940|Shanghai Rescue (1940)]].

---

## Recognition

For decades, Zwartendijk's role was largely unknown. He was eventually recognized as a Righteous Among the Nations by Yad Vashem, though this recognition came posthumously — he died in 1976 without public acknowledgment of what he had done.

He reportedly told his children about his actions only at the end of his life, having kept them private for decades.
