# Mitteler Rebbe

## Rabbi DovBer Schneersohn (1773–1827)

**Title:** כ"ק אדמו"ר האמצעי (His Holiness Our Master and Teacher, the Middle Rebbe)
**Also known as:** "Berkeh Shniur" (in Russian imperial documents), Der Mitteler Rebbe (Yiddish for "the Middle Rebbe")
**Born:** 9 Kislev 5534 (November 17, 1773), Liadi, Belarus
**Died:** 9 Kislev 5588 (November 17, 1827), Lubavitch, Russia
**Age at death:** 54 years old (exactly)
**Buried:** Lubavitch, Belarus (ohel rebuilt 1989)
**Father:** The [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] (R' Schneur Zalman of Liadi)
**Mother:** Sterna Segal
**Role:** Second Rebbe of Chabad Hasidism

---

## Overview

Rabbi DovBer Schneersohn, known as the **Mitteler Rebbe** (Yiddish for "the Middle Rebbe," referring to his position as the second of seven Chabad Rebbes), led Chabad from 1812 until his death in 1827. His relatively brief 15-year tenure was marked by extraordinary challenges — including arrest and persecution by Czarist authorities — and profound intellectual contributions to Chabad philosophy.

The Mitteler Rebbe is unique among Chabad Rebbes in that his birthday (9 Kislev) and yahrzeit (9 Kislev) fall on the same date — a date that also commemorates the beginning of his liberation (10 Kislev). This convergence of birth, liberation, and passing creates a unique spiritual resonance in the Chabad calendar.

---

## Early Life and Education

### Birth and Family Context

DovBer was born on **9 Kislev 5534 (1773)** in **Liadi**, Belarus, where his father the Alter Rebbe had established his court. He was the Alter Rebbe's second son and the one ultimately recognized as the spiritual heir to the leadership.

### Name and Significance

The name "DovBer" (בעריש in Yiddish, pronounced "Ber") combines "Dov" (bear) and "Ber" (also bear). In Jewish tradition, the name is associated with strength and tenacity. The Mitteler Rebbe's Yiddish name was "Ber," which appears in Russian documents as "Berkeh Shniur" (Ber, son of Shneur).

### Education Under the Alter Rebbe

The Mitteler Rebbe received his education directly from his father, who recognized his son's extraordinary capacities from an early age. The Alter Rebbe devoted significant attention to DovBer's spiritual development, personally supervising his:

- **Torah study**: Mastery of Talmud, halacha, and Kabbalah
- **Chassidic training**: Deep immersion in Chassidic thought and practice
- **Character development**: Formation of the refined character traits befitting a Rebbe

By his teenage years, DovBer was already regarded as a scholar of note, capable of explaining complex Chassidic concepts to others.

### Relationship with His Father

The relationship between the Alter Rebbe and the Mitteler Rebbe was one of profound closeness and mutual respect. The Alter Rebbe treated his son not merely as a child but as a spiritual partner, sharing with him teachings that he shared with no one else.

The Alter Rebbe once said of his son: "DovBer understands my teachings better than anyone else. He will be able to explain them to the generation that comes after me."

---

## Marriage and Family

### Marriage

The Mitteler Rebbe married **Sheina**, the daughter of a prominent Chassid, at approximately age 18 (c. 1791). The marriage was arranged by the Alter Rebbe, who sought a match that would support his son's spiritual destiny.

### Children

The Mitteler Rebbe and his wife Sheina had several children, including:

1. **Menucha Rochel** (1798–1888) — who would later settle in Hebron and become a major Chabad figure in Eretz Yisrael
2. **Daughter** — who would marry the [[entities/people/tzemach-tzedek|Tzemach Tzedek]] (her cousin)
3. Other children — some of whom died young

The Mitteler Rebbe's daughter's marriage to her cousin (the Tzemach Tzedek) created a double dynastic connection — the Tzemach Tzedek was both the Mitteler Rebbe's son-in-law and nephew (through his mother, the Mitteler Rebbe's sister).

---

## Life Before Leadership

Before inheriting the leadership of Chabad, the Mitteler Rebbe served as his father's trusted assistant and deputy. In this role, he:

- Taught Chassidic discourses to senior Chassidim
- Corresponded with Chabad communities throughout Russia
- Helped organize the collection and distribution of the **chalukah** (funds for Eretz Yisrael)
- Assisted in managing the court's affairs
- Began developing his own distinctive approach to Chassidic teaching

Even before formally becoming Rebbe, he was recognized as the **heir apparent** and the primary transmitter of his father's teachings.

---

## Succession to Leadership

### The Alter Rebbe's Death (1812)

The Alter Rebbe died on **24 Tevet 5573 (December 27, 1812)** in the village of **Pyena** while fleeing Napoleon's invading army. His death created a leadership crisis for the Chabad community:

- The community was in flight, scattered across the Russian countryside
- Communication was difficult due to war conditions
- No formal designation of successor had been made in writing

### The Succession Question

In principle, the Alter Rebbe's eldest son, **R' Chaim Avraham**, was the natural heir. However, for reasons that are not entirely clear in historical sources, Chaim Avraham did not assert his claim to the leadership. Various explanations have been offered:

- He recognized his younger brother DovBer's greater spiritual capacity
- He preferred to avoid the burdens of public leadership
- There was some understanding between the brothers

Ultimately, the Chassidim turned to DovBer, and he accepted the mantle of leadership.

### Challenges Facing the New Rebbe

When the Mitteler Rebbe inherited leadership in 1812–1813, he faced enormous challenges:

1. **Displacement**: The community was scattered due to the war with Napoleon
2. **Grief**: The Alter Rebbe's death was fresh and traumatic
3. **Persecution**: Russian authorities were suspicious of Chassidic groups
4. **Organizational chaos**: The structures of community life had been disrupted
5. **Financial pressure**: The war had disrupted economic life and charitable giving

Despite these challenges, the Mitteler Rebbe moved decisively to reorganize the community and establish a new center.

## Establishment in Lubavitch (1813)

### The Move to Lubavitch

In **1813 (5573)**, approximately six months after his father's death, the Mitteler Rebbe settled in **Lubavitch**, a town in the Smolensk region of Russia. This decision would shape Chabad history for the next century.

### Why Lubavitch?

Several factors likely influenced the choice of Lubavitch:

- **Geography**: Located in White Russia (Belarus), the heartland of Chabad support
- **Accessibility**: Positioned on major roads, accessible to pilgrims from across Russia
- **Isolation**: Sufficiently removed from major cities to avoid excessive government scrutiny
- **Community**: Existing Jewish community that could support the Rebbe's court
- **Safety**: Far enough from the war zone to provide refuge

### Lubavitch as Chabad Center

The Mitteler Rebbe established Lubavitch as Chabad's world headquarters. From 1813 until 1915 — a period of **102 years** — Lubavitch would serve as:

- The home of the Chabad Rebbe
- The seat of Chabad administration
- The location of the central yeshiva (Tomchei Temimim, founded later)
- A pilgrimage destination for Chassidim from across Russia
- The intellectual center of Chabad thought

The town became so closely identified with the movement that Chabad was often called "Lubavitch Chassidism" — a name that persists to this day.

## Intellectual Contributions

### Systematization of Chabad Thought

The Mitteler Rebbe's primary contribution was the development and systematization of his father's teachings. The Alter Rebbe had created the foundations of Chabad philosophy; the Mitteler Rebbe:

- Expanded these teachings into a comprehensive system
- Developed the meditative aspects of Chabad practice in particular depth
- Created frameworks for understanding mystical experiences
- Wrote extensively on the transformation of consciousness

### Major Works

The Mitteler Rebbe was a prolific author. His works include:

**Sha'ar HaEmunah** (Gate of Faith) — A systematic exposition of the principles of Jewish faith from a Chassidic perspective

**Sha'ar HaYichud** (Gate of Unity) — Explaining the concept of divine unity through Chassidic contemplation

**Sha'ar HaKavanot** (Gate of Intentions) — Detailed guidance on mystical intentions in prayer and mitzvah observance

**Biurei HaZohar** — Commentary on selected passages from the Zohar

**Maamarei Admor HaEmtzai** — Collected discourses (maamarim) organized by Torah portion and festival

** Derech Chaim** — Ethical teachings and guidance for character development

** Kuntras HaHispa'alus** — A detailed guide to Chassidic meditation and contemplation

### Teaching Style

The Mitteler Rebbe was known for:

- **Extremely long discourses** — Some of his maamarim lasted for several hours
- **Systematic exposition** — He would explain concepts from multiple angles
- **Practical guidance** — He didn't just teach theory but provided specific techniques
- **Personal attention** — He worked individually with students to guide their spiritual progress

## The Arrest and Liberation (1825–1827)

### Background

In **1825 (5586)**, the Mitteler Rebbe was arrested on charges brought by **Simcha Kissin of Vitebsk**, an informant who accused him of:

1. Collecting funds for Eretz Yisrael without government authorization
2. Running an unauthorized religious organization
3. Engaging in suspicious foreign correspondence

These charges were serious, as **collecting money for foreign destinations** could be construed as seditious activity by Czarist authorities.

### The Arrest

On **1 Tishrei 5586 (October 1, 1825)**, Rosh Hashanah, the Mitteler Rebbe was arrested by order of Governor **Nikolai Khavanski**. Arrested on the holiest day of the Jewish year was particularly traumatic for the community.

In Russian documents, the Rebbe was referred to as **"Berkeh Shniur"** — a bureaucratic rendering of his name (Ber, son of Shneur).

### Investigation and Interrogation

The Mitteler Rebbe was subjected to multiple interrogations:

- **First interrogation**: October 7, 1825
- **Written defense submitted**: October 9, 1825
- **Second interrogation**: October 15, 1825
- **Third interrogation**: October 19, 1825

Throughout, he maintained his composure and dignity, refusing to compromise his principles while providing respectful responses to the authorities.

### Three-Stage Liberation

The liberation process unfolded over three years:

1. **10 Kislev 5586 (1825)** — Local release from immediate custody. This became the primary celebration day.

2. **11 Kislev 5587 (1826)** — Regional court in Mogilev acquitted him of all charges.

3. **21 Kislev 5588 (1827)** — Imperial Senate in St. Petersburg confirmed acquittal — **12 days after the Rebbe's death**.

The **10th of Kislev** became a major Chabad holiday, celebrated as **Yud-Tet/Yud Kislev** — parallel to 19 Kislev (Alter Rebbe's liberation) and 12-13 Tammuz (Rayatz's liberation).

See [[summaries/masar-vegeulat-hamtza|מאסר וגאולת אדמו"ר האמצעי]] for the full documentary collection of this arrest.

## Death and Burial

### Final Illness

The Mitteler Rebbe's health deteriorated during his final year. Some sources suggest that the stress of the arrest and investigation contributed to his declining health.

### Death on Birthday

The Mitteler Rebbe died on **9 Kislev 5588 (November 17, 1827)** — exactly 54 years to the day after his birth. The convergence of birth and death on the same date is unusual and spiritually significant.

### Burial in Lubavitch

He was buried in **Lubavitch**, next to his father the Alter Rebbe (who had been reinterred there from Haditch). The ohel (burial site) became a pilgrimage destination.

During the Soviet period, the ohel fell into disrepair. In **1989 (5749)**, it was rebuilt, allowing Chabad chassidim to once again visit the graves of the early Rebbes.

## Relationship with the Tzemach Tzedek

### The Tzemach Tzedek as Successor

The Mitteler Rebbe was succeeded by his son-in-law (and nephew), **Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn** — the **Tzemach Tzedek**. The succession was smooth:

- The Tzemach Tzedek was the obvious heir — both as son-in-law and as a scholar of note
- The community accepted him without dispute
- The dynastic continuity was preserved

### Double Family Connection

The Tzemach Tzedek had a double connection to the Alter Rebbe:

- Through his mother, Devorah Leah — the Alter Rebbe's daughter
- Through his wife, the Mitteler Rebbe's daughter

This double connection strengthened his claim to leadership and symbolized the consolidation of the family dynasty.

## Character and Personality

### Humility

Despite his greatness, the Mitteler Rebbe was known for extraordinary humility. He consistently attributed his teachings to his father, saying: "I am only explaining what my father taught."

### Intensity

The Mitteler Rebbe approached divine service with tremendous intensity. He demanded complete dedication from himself and his followers, emphasizing that Chassidus required genuine transformation, not just intellectual understanding.

### Accessibility

Despite his intellectual attainments, the Mitteler Rebbe remained accessible to simple Jews. He would receive visitors from all walks of life and tailor his teachings to their level of understanding.

### Resilience

The Mitteler Rebbe demonstrated remarkable resilience in the face of persecution. Despite arrest and investigation, he continued leading the community and teaching without interruption.

---

## Key Disciples

The Mitteler Rebbe trained a generation of Chassidic leaders who would serve the Tzemach Tzedek and subsequent Rebbes:

1. **R' Hillel of Paritch** (1795–1864) — Major mashpia (spiritual mentor) who trained hundreds of chassidim

2. **R' Nissan Nemenov** — Scholar and teacher who would later serve in Soviet times

3. **R' Yeshiya Berlin** — Wealthy philanthropist who would later fund Tomchei Temimim

These disciples formed the bridge between the founding generation and the mass movement that Chabad would become under the Tzemach Tzedek.

---

## Children and Their Roles

### Menucha Rochel Slonim (1798–1888)

The Mitteler Rebbe's daughter, **Menucha Rochel**, married **R' Yakov Slonim** and moved to **Hebron, Eretz Yisrael** in **1845**. She became:

- The most prominent Chabad figure in the Holy Land
- A spiritual guide to the Chabad community in Hebron
- A key figure in the Hebron Jewish community more broadly
- The subject of numerous stories about her righteousness and insight

See [[entities/people/menucha-rochel|Menucha Rochel Slonim]] for her full biography.

### Other Children

Several of the Mitteler Rebbe's other children died young, a source of great personal sorrow. His daughter who married the Tzemach Tzedek connected the family lines and ensured dynastic continuity.

---

## Historical Significance

### Consolidation of the Dynasty

The Mitteler Rebbe's leadership established the principle of **family succession** in Chabad leadership. The transition from Alter Rebbe to Mitteler Rebbe to Tzemach Tzedek created a dynasty that would continue for seven generations.

### Intellectual Development

The Mitteler Rebbe expanded and systematized his father's teachings, creating the comprehensive philosophical system that Chabad would build upon in subsequent generations. His works remain foundational texts for Chabad study.

### Lubavitch as Center

By establishing Lubavitch as Chabad's headquarters, the Mitteler Rebbe created a stable institutional base that allowed the movement to grow and flourish for a century.

### Pattern of Persecution

The Mitteler Rebbe's arrest and liberation established the pattern of Chabad Rebbes facing state persecution:

- Alter Rebbe (1798, 1800)
- Mitteler Rebbe (1825–1827)
- Rayatz (1927)
- Multiple arrests of chassidim in Soviet times

This pattern of **mesirus nefesh** (self-sacrifice) became central to Chabad identity.
