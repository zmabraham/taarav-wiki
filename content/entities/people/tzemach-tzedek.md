# Tzemach Tzedek

## Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn (1789–1866)

**Title:** כ"ק אדמו"ר הצמח צדק (His Holiness Our Master and Teacher, the Tzemach Tzedek)
**Named after:** His responsa collection, Tzemach Tzedek ("Righteous Sprout" — from Zechariah 3:8, referring to the Messiah)
**Born:** 29 Elul 5549 (September 20, 1789), Liozna, Belarus
**Died:** 13 Nissan 5626 (March 29, 1866), Lubavitch, Russia
**Age at death:** 76 years old
**Buried:** Lubavitch, Belarus (ohel rebuilt 1989)
**Grandfather:** The [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] (maternal grandfather)
**Father-in-law:** The [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]] (wife's father)
**Role:** Third Rebbe of Chabad Hasidism
**Tenure:** 39 years (1827–1866) — longest tenure of any Chabad Rebbe in Lubavitch

---

## Overview

Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneersohn, known as the **Tzemach Tzedek**, led Chabad for approximately **40 years** — the longest tenure of any Chabad Rebbe based in Lubavitch. His leadership period (1827–1866) coincided with some of the most challenging moments in Czarist Jewish history, and he emerged as perhaps the most politically active of the Chabad Rebbes in his dealings with Russian authorities.

The Tzemach Tzedek was unique among Chabad Rebbes in his **double dynastic connection** to the Alter Rebbe:

- Through his mother, Devorah Leah, he was the Alter Rebbe's grandson
- Through his wife, he was the Mitteler Rebbe's son-in-law

This double connection strengthened his claim to leadership and symbolized the consolidation of the Chabad dynasty.

---

## Early Life and Education

### Birth and Family Background

The Tzemach Tzedek was born on **29 Elul 5549 (1789)** in **Liozna, Belarus** — the town where the Alter Rebbe had established his first court. His parents were:

- **Father**: R' Shlomo Schneersohn
- **Mother: Devorah Leah**, daughter of the Alter Rebbe

Thus, the Tzemach Tzedek was born into the heart of the founding family of Chabad.

### Relationship with the Alter Rebbe

As the Alter Rebbe's grandson, the Tzemach Tzedek had a close relationship with his illustrious grandfather from childhood. The Alter Rebbe took special interest in his education and spiritual development, recognizing the boy's extraordinary gifts.

According to tradition, the Alter Rebbe once said of his grandson: "This child will illuminate the eyes of Israel." This prophecy alluded to both the Tzemach Tzedek's future leadership and the name "Tzemach" (sprout/growth), which suggests illumination and growth.

### Education

The Tzemach Tzedek received his education from the greatest teachers of the generation:

- His grandfather, the Alter Rebbe
- His uncle, the Mitteler Rebbe
- Other leading scholars of the Chabad school

By his teenage years, he had mastered:

- The entire Talmud and its commentaries
- The full range of halachic literature
- Kabbalah and Chassidic philosophy
- Secular knowledge necessary for dealing with government authorities

### Marriage

The Tzemach Tzedek married his cousin — the daughter of the Mitteler Rebbe — creating a family connection that further strengthened his position within the dynasty. This marriage produced several children, including the future Rebbe Maharash.

---

## Succession to Leadership

### The Mitteler Rebbe's Death (1827)

The Mitteler Rebbe died on **9 Kislev 5588 (1827)**. The Tzemach Tzedek, then 38 years old, was the obvious heir:

- As the Mitteler Rebbe's son-in-law
- As the Alter Rebbe's grandson
- As a recognized scholar in his own right
- As someone who had been groomed for leadership

### Smooth Transition

The transition was remarkably smooth, especially compared to previous successions:

- No competing claims emerged
- The community united around him quickly
- His authority was widely recognized both within and beyond Chabad

This smooth succession established the principle of **family dynastic succession** in Chabad leadership, which would continue through seven generations.

### Leadership Challenges

When the Tzemach Tzedek assumed leadership, he faced several challenges:

1. **Recent persecution**: The Mitteler Rebbe's arrest was fresh in memory
2. **Political suspicion**: Russian authorities viewed Chassidic groups with suspicion
3. **Communal reorganization**: The community needed to rebuild after the disruptions of war and persecution
4. **Internal development**: The movement needed institutions to sustain its growth

Despite these challenges, the Tzemach Tzedek's 39-year tenure would see Chabad expand and flourish as never before.

---

## The Cantonist Crisis

### The Decrees

The most serious communal challenge of the Tzemach Tzedek's tenure was the **Cantonist decrees** — Russian imperial laws that required Jewish communities to provide quotas of young men for extremely long military service:

- **Service duration**: 25 years
- **Starting age**: Sometimes as young as 12
- **Pressure to convert**: During service, Jewish conscripts were pressured to convert to Christianity
- **Communal responsibility**: Jewish communities themselves had to select and hand over the conscripts

This system created a moral nightmare for Jewish communities, who were forced to choose which children to hand over to an army designed to convert them.

### The Tzemach Tzedek's Response

The Tzemach Tzedek worked actively to mitigate the impact of these decrees:

- **Intervention with authorities**: He petitioned government officials for relief
- **Legal resistance**: He used whatever legal channels were available to protect Jewish children
- **Communal guidance**: He provided rabbinic guidance on how to navigate the system while minimizing spiritual harm
- **Hidden children**: He supported efforts to hide children from conscription officers

### The Haskalah Connection

The Cantonist decrees were supported by the **Haskalah** (Jewish Enlightenment) movement, which saw traditional Jewish education as backward and believed that military service would "modernize" Jewish youth. The Tzemach Tzedek thus found himself in a three-front battle:

1. Against the Czarist government
2. Against the Haskalah movement
3. Against the temptation to compromise Jewish principles for short-term relief

---

## Opposition to the Haskalah

### The Government-Haskalah Alliance

The Russian government, influenced by Haskalah ideas, sought to "reform" Jewish education by:

- Establishing government-sponsored Jewish schools with secular curriculum
- Staffing these schools with maskilim (Enlightenment advocates) hostile to traditional Judaism
- Undermining traditional cheders (Jewish elementary schools)
- Pressuring communities to adopt "modern" educational approaches

### The 1843 Rabbinical Assembly

In **1843 (5603)**, the Czarist government convened a **Rabbinical Assembly in St. Petersburg** to discuss "reforming" Jewish life. The Tzemach Tzedek was invited as one of the representatives of traditional Jewry.

At this assembly:

- The Tzemach Tzedek represented traditional Jewish education against Haskalah proposals
- He argued that secular education without religious foundation would lead to assimilation
- He defended the cheder system and traditional yeshivos
- He refused to compromise on principles of Torah observance

Although the assembly did not achieve its goals, the Tzemach Tzedek's stance established Chabad as a defender of traditional Judaism against both external and internal threats.

### Educational Philosophy

The Tzemach Tzedek opposed the Haskalah not by rejecting all secular knowledge but by insisting that:

- Secular knowledge must be built upon a foundation of Torah
- Education without religious values produces assimilation, not enlightenment
- Jewish communities have the right to educate their children according to their traditions
- Compromise on education is ultimately self-defeating

This educational philosophy would influence Chabad's approach to education for generations, including the founding of **Tomchei Temimim** by his grandson, the Rashab, in 1897.

---

## Lubavitch as World Center

### Physical Expansion

Under the Tzemach Tzedek's leadership, **Lubavitch** expanded dramatically:

- The Rebbe's court complex grew to accommodate more visitors
- Synagogues and study halls were built
- Housing for chassidim who moved to Lubavitch was constructed
- Infrastructure to support thousands of annual pilgrims was developed

### Institutional Development

The Tzemach Tzedek developed institutions that would sustain Chabad for generations:

- **Kollelim**: Networks of stipend-supported scholars throughout Russia
- **Yeshivos**: Advanced study institutions for training Torah scholars
- **Meshulachim**: Emissaries who collected funds and maintained connections with distant communities
- **Correspondence networks**: Systems for maintaining communication with communities across the Russian Empire

### Pilgrimage System

The Tzemach Tzedek established a system of **pilgrimage to Lubavitch**:

- Chassidim would travel to Lubavitch for **yechidus** (private audiences)
- The Rebbe provided personal guidance to thousands annually
- These pilgrimages strengthened communal bonds and transmitted Chabad teachings
- The practice of **hiskashrus** (spiritual bonding with the Rebbe) was institutionalized

## Scholarly Legacy

The Tzemach Tzedek was extraordinarily prolific, perhaps the most productive author among Chabad Rebbes:

### Responsa: Tzemach Tzedek

His collection of **responsa** (rabbinic legal rulings), titled **Tzemach Tzedek**, covers:

- Thousands of halachic questions across all areas of Jewish law
- Complex legal cases requiring deep analysis
- Applications of halacha to new situations
- Interactions with other rabbinic authorities

This work established the Tzemach Tzedek as one of the great halachic decisors (poskim) of his generation.

### Derech Mitzvosecha

**Derech Mitzvosecha** ("The Way of the Commandments") explores:

- The philosophical and spiritual reasons for the mitzvot (commandments)
- Connections between seemingly unrelated commandments
- The cosmic significance of Jewish observance
- The transformation of consciousness through mitzvah performance

This work is unique in Chabad literature for its systematic philosophical approach to the mitzvot.

### Or HaTorah

**Or HaTorah** ("Light of the Torah") is a multi-volume commentary on:

- The Torah (Five Books of Moses)
- Talmudic and Midrashic texts
- Jewish holidays and lifecycle events
- All from a Chassidic perspective

This work demonstrates how Chassidic teachings illuminate traditional texts.

### Discourses and Letters

The Tzemach Tzedek authored hundreds of **Chassidic discourses (maamarim)** on:

- Torah portions and festivals
- Kabbalistic concepts
- Spiritual practices
- Guidance for daily life

His **letters (Igrot Kodesh)** provide:

- Guidance to individuals
- Direction to communities
- Responsa on questions of Jewish law
- Insights into his leadership of the movement

### Translation and Accessibility

The Tzemach Tzedek's works were:

- Widely disseminated in printed form
- Studied in yeshivos throughout the Jewish world
- Translated into multiple languages
- Continuously reprinted to the present day

His scholarly output, achieved simultaneously with his responsibilities as leader of a major religious movement, is virtually unparalleled in Jewish history.

---

## Political Activity

### Diplomacy on Behalf of Russian Jewry

The Tzemach Tzedek was perhaps the most politically engaged of the Chabad Rebbes in Czarist times. He:

- Petitioned government officials on behalf of Jewish communities
- Intervened in cases of persecution or false accusations
- Worked to mitigate harmful decrees
- Represented Jewish interests to authorities

This political activism became part of Chabad's legacy, with subsequent Rebbes (particularly the Rayatz in Soviet times and the Rebbe Maharash in his travels) continuing this pattern.

### Relationship with Authorities

The Tzemach Tzedek navigated a delicate balance with Russian authorities:

- He respected lawful authority and urged his followers to do the same
- He resisted unjust decrees through legal channels
- He maintained relationships with officials who could be helpful
- He avoided confrontation when possible but stood firm on principles

This approach — principled accommodation — became characteristic of Chabad's relationship with governments.

## Family and Succession

### Children

The Tzemach Tzedek and his wife had several children, including:

1. **R' Shmuel (Maharash)** (1834–1882) — who would succeed him as fourth Rebbe
2. **R' Yehuda Leib of Kopust** — who established a court in Kopust
3. **R' Chaim Schneur Zalman of Liadi** — who established a court in Liadi
4. **R' Avraham Schneersohn** — who served in Eretz Yisrael
5. **R' Baruch Shalom** — known for his piety and wisdom
6. **Daughter** — married R' Yosef Yitzchak of Ovruch

### Succession Transition

When the Tzemach Tzedek died on **13 Nissan 5626 (1866)**, leadership passed to his youngest son, **R' Shmuel (Maharash)**. However, this succession was more complex than previous transitions:

- Several of the Tzemach Tzedek's sons were distinguished scholars
- Some brothers established their own courts in other towns
- This created various Chabad-related dynasties (Kopust, Liadi, etc.)

Despite these branches, the main line of succession continued through the Maharash and his descendants.

---

## Character and Personality

### Accessibility

Despite his greatness and the demands on his time, the Tzemach Tzedek remained remarkably accessible. He would:

- Receive visitors for hours daily
- Respond to letters from distant communities
- Provide personal guidance to simple Jews and great scholars alike
- Make time for anyone who sought his help

### Humility

The Tzemach Tzedek was known for extraordinary humility. When praised for his accomplishments, he would attribute everything to:

- His grandfather, the Alter Rebbe
- His father-in-law, the Mitteler Rebbe
- The merits of the community

### Balanced Leadership

The Tzemach Tzedek modeled a balanced approach to Jewish leadership:

- Torah scholarship combined with communal responsibility
- Spiritual depth combined with practical engagement
- Tradition combined with necessary adaptation
- Resistance to compromise combined with diplomatic skill

---

## Historical Context

### The Nicholas I Era (1825–1855)

Most of the Tzemach Tzedek's leadership occurred under Czar **Nicholas I**, known for:

- Autocratic rule
- Conservative policies
- Suspicion of minority groups
- Policies of forced assimilation

This was a difficult period for Russian Jews, and the Tzemach Tzedek's leadership provided stability and guidance.

### The Alexander II Era (1855–1881)

The latter part of the Tzemach Tzedek's tenure coincided with the early reign of **Alexander II**, who implemented some reforms but also maintained restrictive policies toward Jews.

---

## Death and Burial

### Final Illness and Death

The Tzemach Tzedek died on **13 Nissan 5626 (March 29, 1866)** in **Lubavitch**, after nearly 40 years of leadership. His death marked the end of an era — he had led Chabad longer than any Rebbe would in Lubavitch.

### Burial

He was buried in Lubavitch, near the Mitteler Rebbe and the Alter Rebbe. The ohel (burial site) became a pilgrimage destination until the Soviet period, when it was neglected. In **1989**, the ohel was rebuilt, allowing Chabad chassidim to once again visit the graves.

---

## Legacy

### Longest Tenure

The Tzemach Tzedek's 39-year tenure provided Chabad with unprecedented stability and continuity. This long period of consistent leadership allowed:

- Institutions to mature and develop
- Teachings to be systematically codified
- The next generation to be properly trained
- The movement to expand geographically

### Scholarly Output

His enormous scholarly output — halachic responsa, Chassidic discourses, philosophical works, letters — created a literary foundation that continues to sustain Chabad intellectually.

### Political Model

His model of political engagement — principled resistance to unjust decrees combined with diplomacy and legal channels — established a pattern that Chabad would follow in subsequent generations, particularly in Soviet times.

### Institutional Foundation

The institutions he developed in Lubavitch — yeshivos, kolelim, pilgrimage systems, correspondence networks — provided the infrastructure that allowed Chabad to grow into a mass movement.

### Succession Pattern

Although his succession led to some branching (Kopust, Liadi courts), the main line continued smoothly through the Maharash, establishing the principle of dynastic succession that would continue through seven generations.
