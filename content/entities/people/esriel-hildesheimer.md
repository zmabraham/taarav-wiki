# Rabbi Esriel (Azriel) Hildesheimer

**Born:** 1820, Halberstadt, Germany  
**Died:** 1899 (י' אב תרנ"ט), Berlin, Germany  
**Role:** Founder of the Berlin Rabbinical Seminary; leader of German Orthodox Jewry; founder of the _Adass Yisroel_ community in Berlin

---

## Overview

Rabbi Esriel (Azriel) Hildesheimer was one of the most influential leaders of German Orthodox Jewry in the nineteenth century. He founded the Hildesheimer Rabbinical Seminary (_Rabbinerseminar zu Berlin_) in 1873, which trained hundreds of rabbis in an approach that combined rigorous traditional scholarship with engagement with modern academic methods. He also established the _Adass Yisroel_ congregation in Berlin as a separatist Orthodox community, breaking away from the mainstream Liberal-dominated Jewish community structure.

His significance in Chabad history lies in the relationship his family developed with the Chabad Rebbes across three generations — from the [[entities/people/rashab|Rashab]]'s visit to Berlin in 1885, through the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]'s intensive cooperation with his son Rabbi Meir Hildesheimer during the 1920s, to continued correspondence with his grandson Rabbi Azriel Hildesheimer in the 1930s.

---

## Biography and the Rabbinical Seminary

Born in Halberstadt in 1820, Hildesheimer studied under the foremost rabbinic authorities of his day, including Rabbi Jacob Ettlinger in Altona. He served as rabbi of Eisenstadt (Hungary) from 1851 to 1869, where he established a yeshiva that attracted students from across Austria-Hungary. His willingness to incorporate secular studies alongside traditional Torah learning was controversial in some Orthodox circles but attracted a new generation of students.

In 1869 he was invited to Berlin, and in 1873 he formally established the Rabbinical Seminary — the first Orthodox institution of its kind in Germany. The seminary produced generations of rabbis who served communities across Europe and beyond. After his death in 1899, his son **Rabbi Dr. Meir Hildesheimer** succeeded him as director of the seminary and as rabbi of the _Adass Yisroel_ community.

## Connection to Chabad

### The Rashab's Visit (c. 1885)

The earliest documented contact between the Hildesheimer family and Chabad occurred around 1885 (תרמ"ה), when the [[entities/people/rashab|Rashab]] (Rabbi Shalom Dovber Schneersohn, then approximately 24 years old) visited Berlin and met personally with Rabbi Esriel Hildesheimer. The Rayatz later recorded that Hildesheimer received the Rashab with great honor, explaining that he had known and esteemed the Rashab's father, the [[entities/people/maharash|Maharash]]. This meeting represented one of the earliest direct connections between the Chabad Rebbes and the leadership of German Orthodoxy.

### The Rayatz and Rabbi Meir Hildesheimer (1927–1929)

The connection deepened dramatically during the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]'s years in Riga and Berlin (1927–1933). After the Rayatz's release from Soviet imprisonment and arrival in Riga, Rabbi **Meir Hildesheimer** — Esriel's son and successor — became one of his most important allies in the German-speaking world. Together with Dr. Leo Baeck (the Reform leader), Hildesheimer appealed to German government officials, including Vice-Chancellor Weissmann, to press for the Rayatz's freedom and for the rights of Soviet Jewry.

When the Rayatz organized the historic [[events/matzah-campaign-1929|1929 Matzah Campaign]] to ship Passover matzah into starving Soviet Russia, Rabbi Meir Hildesheimer served as a key operational partner in Berlin. He and the Rayatz's son-in-law Rabbi Shmaryahu Gurary negotiated directly with the Soviet ambassador in Berlin to secure import permits for fifty railroad cars of matzah. The Rayatz wrote to Hildesheimer on 13 Nissan 1929, expressing profound gratitude and sending him a unique gift connected to his role.

### Three Generations of Correspondence

The relationship extended to a third generation. Rabbi **Azriel Hildesheimer** (Meir's son, named for his grandfather Esriel) corresponded with the Rayatz from Berlin in 1936. The Rayatz stayed in the Hildesheimer home during at least one of his Berlin visits, and the young [[entities/people/rebbe|Lubavitcher Rebbe]] (Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson) was present on at least one such occasion. This multigenerational connection between Chabad and the Hildesheimer family exemplifies the network of Orthodox solidarity that spanned the divides between Eastern and Western European Jewish communities.

---

## Legacy

Rabbi Esriel Hildesheimer's vision of Torah Judaism engaged with the modern world — without capitulating to it — resonated with Chabad's own approach of utilizing contemporary means for traditional ends. The Rabbinical Seminary he founded trained hundreds of rabbis before its forced closure under Nazi rule in 1938. His son Meir died in 1934, and the family's connection to Chabad stands as a notable example of cross-communal Orthodox cooperation in the face of shared crisis.
