# Rabbi Binyamin Kletzker

**Born**: c. 1770, Kletzk, Belarus  
**Died**: c. 1830  
**Role**: Major disciple of the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]]; celebrated model of the _beinoni_ (intermediate person) described in Tanya

---

## Overview

Reb Binyamin of Kletzk is one of the most famous chassidim of the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]]. He is remembered primarily for his extraordinary _yiras shamayim_ (awe of heaven) — an intense, almost agonizing awareness of his own spiritual shortcomings and a relentless drive toward self-improvement. In Chabad lore, he is considered a living embodiment of the _beinoni_ — the spiritual archetype at the heart of the Alter Rebbe's Tanya.

---

## The Beinoni Ideal

The Tanya, the Alter Rebbe's foundational work, describes the _beinoni_ as a person who:

- Never sins in thought, speech, or action — not because evil impulses are absent, but because they are never expressed
- Engages in constant inner struggle between the divine soul and the animal soul
- May feel far from God even while behaving righteously

Reb Binyamin embodied this. He was known for his intense inner turmoil — he felt himself to be deeply flawed even as others perceived him as a righteous man. This gap between self-perception and outward conduct is precisely the _beinoni_'s condition.

---

## Correspondence with the Alter Rebbe

Several classic Chabad stories involve Reb Binyamin's letters to the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]], in which he pours out his spiritual anguish — confessing feelings of distance from God, fear of inadequacy, and despair over his spiritual state. The Alter Rebbe's replies to him are considered models of Chassidic guidance, addressing the universal challenge of spiritual self-doubt.

These exchanges illustrate a key Tanya principle: the _beinoni_'s feelings of failure are not signs of actual failure but are the natural condition of a person who takes spiritual life seriously.

---

## Legacy

Reb Binyamin Kletzker is less famous for any public accomplishments than for his _inner life_. He represents the deeply private dimension of Chabad Chassidism — the idea that the most important battles are fought within the human heart, invisible to the outside world. His stories continue to be told in Chabad as illustrations of sincere, honest spiritual struggle.
