# Rabbi Yechiel Yaakov Weinberg — ר' יחיאל יעקב וויינברג

**Born**: 1885, Koniuchy (near Grodno), Russian Empire  
**Died**: 1966, Montreux, Switzerland  
**Role**: Head of the Hildesheimer Rabbinical Seminary in Berlin (Hochschule für die Wissenschaft des Judentums); major halakhic authority  
**Major Work**: _Seredei Eish_ (שרידי אש) — responsa on modern halakhic questions

---

## Overview

Rabbi Yechiel Yaakov Weinberg was one of the leading Orthodox rabbinical figures of twentieth-century European Jewry, combining traditional Eastern European yeshiva training with advanced secular academic education. As director of the [[entities/places/berlin|Berlin]] Rabbinical Seminary (the Hildesheimer Seminary), he shaped German Orthodox rabbinical training during the Weimar years. His responsa collection _Seredei Eish_ remains a standard reference in modern Orthodox halakhic literature.

---

## Background and Education

Weinberg was born in Koniuchy near Grodno (then Russian Empire, now Belarus/Lithuania border area) in 1885 — placing him in the Eastern European yeshiva world. He received classic yeshiva training before moving westward for academic study. He eventually obtained a doctorate and combined this with rabbinic scholarship in a manner characteristic of the German Neo-Orthodox tradition.

His background bridged two worlds:
- **Eastern European**: Traditional Talmudic training, yeshiva methodology
- **German Neo-Orthodox**: Academic rigor, engagement with secular scholarship, systematic responsa writing

---

## The Hildesheimer Seminary

The Rabbinical Seminary of Berlin — founded by [[entities/people/azriel-hildesheimer|Rabbi Azriel Hildesheimer]] in 1873 and continued by his son [[entities/people/meir-hildesheimer|Rabbi Meir Hildesheimer]] — trained generations of modern Orthodox rabbis for communities throughout Germany and beyond. It combined traditional Talmudic study with academic Jewish studies (_Wissenschaft des Judentums_) and modern languages.

Rabbi Weinberg became head of this institution in the 1920s and led it through the increasingly dangerous years of the Weimar Republic and into the Nazi period. The seminary was ultimately closed after 1938.

---

## Seredei Eish

Weinberg's _Seredei Eish_ (שרידי אש — "Embers of Fire") is a collection of responsa addressing the modern halakhic questions of the 20th century. The title itself — "embers of fire" — reflects the destruction of the European Jewish world in which he worked. The responsa address topics including:

- Use of microphones on Shabbat
- Women's hair covering in the modern era
- Various medical and technological questions
- The status of movements within Judaism

The _Seredei Eish_ is distinguished by its blend of rigorous halakhic analysis and deep awareness of the social realities of modern Jewish life. Weinberg engaged seriously with sociological factors in his legal reasoning, which made his work both influential and sometimes controversial in more conservative Orthodox circles.

---

## The Holocaust and Aftermath

During World War II, Rabbi Weinberg was taken to a Nazi labor camp. He survived the war, but his world — the world of German Jewish Orthodoxy — was destroyed. After liberation, he settled in Montreux, Switzerland, where he lived the remainder of his life in relative isolation, continuing to write responsa and correspond with halakhic authorities worldwide.

His postwar letters reveal profound trauma, grief, and difficult questions about theodicy and the nature of Orthodox engagement with modernity. Some of these letters, published posthumously, have generated significant scholarly discussion.

---

## Relationship to Chabad History

Rabbi Weinberg heads the list of German rabbis surveyed in the Chabad-Germany volume (Book 13 of the Mesibbv L'Chassidus series), reflecting his prominence as a Berlin Orthodox figure during the years when [[entities/people/rayatz|the Rayatz]] (1928–1933) and the future [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]] (1928–1931) were in Berlin. While no direct Chabad-Weinberg correspondence is documented in the available sources, his institutional leadership placed him squarely in the world of Berlin Jewish life that intersected with Chabad during this critical period.
