# Dr. Oskar Cohn — ד"ר אוסקר קאהן

**Role**: German socialist Reichstag member (Bundestag delegate); lawyer; key figure in the campaign to free the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] in 1927  
**Period**: Active early 20th century  
**Affiliation**: German Social Democratic / Independent Social Democratic circles; personal friendship with Soviet Commissar for Foreign Affairs Chicherin

---

## Overview

Dr. Oskar Cohn was a Jewish German socialist politician who played a decisive role in the international campaign to free [[entities/people/rayatz|Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (the Rayatz)]] from Soviet imprisonment in 1927. His unique combination of political connections — as a Bundestag/Reichstag member with access to the German Foreign Ministry, and as a personal friend of the Soviet Foreign Commissar Georgy Chicherin — made him an indispensable intermediary.

---

## The Campaign to Free the Rayatz (1927)

When the Rayatz was arrested by the Soviet GPU in June 1927 and sentenced to death (later commuted to exile in Kostroma), the international Jewish community mobilized a rescue campaign. German Jewry played a notable role in this campaign.

### The Joint Intervention of Hildesheimer and Leo Baeck

In an extraordinary act of cross-denominational cooperation, **Rabbi Dr. Meir Hildesheimer** (head of the Orthodox Berlin Rabbinical Seminary) and **Dr. Leo Baeck** (the leading liberal/Reform rabbi of Berlin) jointly petitioned Dr. Oskar Cohn to use his political connections on the Rayatz's behalf.

This collaboration — Orthodox and Reform leaders acting together — illustrated both the urgency of the situation and the overriding Jewish solidarity that transcended religious divisions when a Jewish leader's life was at stake.

### Cohn's Political Interventions

Dr. Cohn activated his network on multiple levels:

1. **German Foreign Ministry**: Cohn met with German Foreign Minister **Gustav Stresemann** (the Nobel Peace Prize laureate) and Vice-Reichskanzler **Weissmann**, conveying the urgency of the situation
2. **Soviet Diplomatic Channels**: Weissmann used his position to pressure Soviet Ambassador **Krestinsky** in Berlin, who promised to act
3. **Chicherin Connection**: Most crucially, Cohn personally contacted **Georgy Chicherin**, the Soviet Commissar for Foreign Affairs, who was Cohn's personal friend. Chicherin's intervention at the highest Soviet government level was a key factor in the Rayatz's release

### The Frankfurt Orthodox Stratagem

In parallel with Cohn's political interventions, the Frankfurt Orthodox community sent the Rayatz a formal rabbinical appointment letter — essentially creating a legal fiction that the Rayatz was needed as a rabbi in Frankfurt. This gave the Soviet authorities a face-saving rationale for releasing him without appearing to capitulate to religious pressure.

---

## The Rayatz's Gratitude

The Rayatz expressed his gratitude to those who secured his release. His first letter from Riga after liberation (Tishrei 5688/1927) specifically thanked [[entities/people/meir-hildesheimer|Rabbi Dr. Meir Hildesheimer]]. During the subsequent Berlin meetings (Kislev 5688), the Rayatz visited **Vice-Reichskanzler Weissmann** in person to convey thanks for his role.

---

## Later Berlin Meetings

The Chabad-Germany sources document Dr. Cohn's continued involvement in Chabad affairs. During the Rayatz's Kislev 5688 (1927-1928) Berlin meetings, Cohn was among those present at a conference organized to arrange budgetary support for Russian Jews through the JDC (American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee), alongside Hildesheimer and other Berlin communal figures.

---

## Historical Significance

The role of Dr. Oskar Cohn illustrates several important dimensions of early 20th century Jewish life:

- The interconnection between Jewish communal needs and socialist political networks
- The willingness of even socialist/secularist Jews to act in defense of traditional religious leaders
- The German-Soviet diplomatic relationship as a channel for Jewish intercession
- The breadth of the coalition that secured the Rayatz's freedom — from Orthodox rabbis to socialist politicians to Soviet diplomats
