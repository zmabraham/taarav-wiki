# Rabbi Mordechai Dubin

**Born**: 1889, Latvia  
**Died**: 1956 (in Siberian exile or shortly after release)  
**Role**: Latvian MP; Jewish community leader; devoted Chabad Chasid; key diplomatic intermediary for the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]

---

## Overview

Rabbi Mordechai Dubin was the most politically powerful Jew in interwar Latvia and one of the most significant figures in the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz's]] exile years. As a member of the Latvian parliament (_Saeima_) and chairman of the Riga Jewish community, he wielded influence that extended across borders — enabling him to act as the Rayatz's primary diplomatic channel with Soviet and Latvian authorities.

---

## Political Career

Dubin served as a member of the Latvian Saeima (parliament) as a representative of the Jewish community. He was a swing vote whose support was actively courted by competing political factions, giving him leverage disproportionate to the Jewish community's numbers. As chairman of the Riga Jewish community, he controlled significant institutional resources and commanded personal respect from Latvian leaders.

Dubin was a leader of **Agudas Yisrael** in Latvia — the Orthodox Jewish political party that represented traditional Jewry in interwar Europe. Through this role, he built relationships with political leaders across the European spectrum, from Latvian nationalists to German social democrats, from British parliamentarians to Soviet diplomats. His parliamentary immunity and diplomatic passport gave him access that ordinary Jewish community leaders lacked.

He was also a devoted Chabad Chassid — maintaining the dual identity of modern politician and traditional religious Jew with unusual success. He prayed at the Chabad synagogue on Marijas Street in Riga (known as the "Bazaar Minyan"), and participated in Chassidic farbrengens with the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] and the _talmidim hashluchim_ (yeshiva students sent as emissaries).

---

## Securing the Rayatz's Release

Dubin's most consequential achievement came in the summer of 1927, when the Rayatz was imprisoned by the Soviet GPU (secret police) and sentenced to death. Dubin mobilized an international diplomatic campaign from Riga, leveraging every contact at his disposal. The campaign involved Latvian government officials, the German vice-chancellor Weissmann, the Soviet ambassador in Berlin (Krestinsky), and political figures across Europe. See [[events/alter-rebbe-arrest-1798|the pattern of Chabad arrests]].

When the Rayatz arrived in Riga in October 1927 after his expulsion from the Soviet Union, Dubin had already arranged for him to be appointed "Rabbi of the Entire Community" (_Rav HaKlali_) of Riga — a position that provided both a legitimate basis for his residence and an official platform for his activities.

---

## Service to the Rayatz

**Soviet Negotiations:** Dubin served as the Rayatz's channel for negotiations with Soviet authorities — using his parliamentary standing and diplomatic connections to press for better treatment of Jewish religious communities inside the USSR. The Rayatz tasked Dubin and a group of activists with building what became _Vaad Magen uMekhaseh_ — the Committee for the Protection and Covering of Soviet Jewry — to coordinate underground support for religious life behind the Iron Curtain.

**International Diplomacy:** Dubin traveled extensively on the Rayatz's behalf. In early 1928, the Rayatz dispatched Dubin and his son-in-law Rabbi Shmaryahu Gurary to London and Paris for diplomatic missions related to Soviet Jewry, and then on to Berlin for meetings with Rabbi Meir Hildesheimer and German officials.

**The 1929 Matzah Campaign:** Dubin played a central role in the extraordinary 1929 Passover Matzah Campaign — an international effort to ship matzah into starving Soviet Russia despite the famine and official Soviet hostility to religion. Dubin negotiated with the Soviet trade representative in Riga, coordinating a diplomatic solution that allowed matzah to enter. The campaign ultimately delivered fifty railroad cars of matzah to Soviet Jews. This represented the intersection of religious necessity, interwar diplomacy, and Chabad institutional creativity at its most sophisticated.

See [[events/matzah-campaign-1929|Matzah Campaign (1929)]].

---

## Personal Life

Dubin's family was deeply embedded in the Riga Chabad community. In the winter of 5696 (1936), his son became engaged to the daughter of Reb Kalman Pappenheim, and Dubin asked the _mashpia_ Rabbi Mordechai Chefat to study Chassidic discourses with his future daughter-in-law in preparation for the wedding — a remarkable example of how Dubin blended his political persona with genuine Chassidic devotion.

---

## Arrest and Fate

When Soviet forces occupied Latvia in June 1940, Dubin's political career ended. He was arrested by Soviet authorities — the same system he had spent years negotiating with on behalf of Jews. He was deported to Siberia, joining the millions of Baltic citizens consumed by Stalin's terror apparatus.

Dubin died in 1956, either in Siberian exile or shortly after his release — a tragic end for a man who had devoted his career to protecting Jewish interests. His fate illustrated the personal risk taken by those who engaged Soviet authorities, even in ostensibly protective roles. The Soviet Union that had negotiated with Dubin over matzah shipments and exit visas ultimately devoured him, as it devoured so many who believed they could reason with totalitarian power.

---

## Significance

Dubin exemplifies a distinctive type in modern Jewish history: the traditional Jew who masters the secular political world without abandoning religious identity. His ability to move between Chabad court and Latvian parliament, between Hebrew prayer and parliamentary speech, made him uniquely valuable in the interwar period. One contemporary observer noted that while rabbis Hildesheimer and Munk did the practical work in Berlin, Dubin's role was to "lift the Rebbe's spirits" during the darkest hours — a task he performed with humor and warmth even as the fate of Soviet Jewry hung in the balance.
