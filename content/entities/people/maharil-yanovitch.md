# Rabbi Yehuda Leib of Yanovitch (מהרי"ל מיאנאוויטש)

**Younger brother of the Alter Rebbe; moreh tzedek of Yanovitch; primary transcriber and editor of Chabad teachings**

## Biography

Rabbi Yehuda Leib — known by the acronym מהרי"ל (Morenu HaRav Yehuda Leib) — was born around תק"ט (1749), approximately sixteen years younger than his elder brother Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi (the Alter Rebbe). He served as a moreh tzedek (rabbinic decisor) in the town of Yanovitch, located roughly forty kilometers from Vitebsk in the Vitebsk gubernia of the Russian Empire.

From a young age, Rabbi Yehuda Leib was deeply attached to his brother and served as his most devoted disciple and secretary. He traveled with the Alter Rebbe to Mezeritch to study under the Maggid Rabbi Dov Ber, and it was Rabbi Yehuda Leib who took on the critical responsibility of transcribing the Alter Rebbe's oral Torah discourses in writing. This work was not merely clerical — he had to convert spoken chassidic teachings into written form while preserving their depth and precision. The manuscripts he produced formed the basis for much of what was later compiled and printed.

When the Tanya was prepared for its first printing in Slavuta in תקנ"ז (1796/7), Rabbi Yehuda Leib played a central editorial role. The Alter Rebbe trusted him above others to ensure the text's fidelity. It was Rabbi Yehuda Leib who coordinated with the printers, reviewed proof pages, and served as the editorial intermediary.

After the Alter Rebbe's passing in תקע"ג (1812), it fell to Rabbi Yehuda Leib to oversee posthumous publication projects. He edited and prepared for print the Alter Rebbe's halachic magnum opus, the expanded Shulchan Aruch (שולחן ערוך הרב), ensuring that the legal rulings his brother had composed across decades were properly organized and transmitted.

Rabbi Yehuda Leib passed away between תקפ"ה and תקפ"ו (1825–1826), having devoted his entire life to the preservation and dissemination of his brother's Torah legacy.

## Significance

Rabbi Yehuda Leib occupies a quiet but indispensable role in early Chabad history. Without his meticulous transcription work, many of the Alter Rebbe's oral teachings may never have reached written form. His editorial stewardship of the Shulchan Aruch Harav after 1812 ensured that this foundational halachic text was transmitted with accuracy. He represents the archetype of the loyal disciple-sibling who subordinates personal ambition entirely to the mission of preserving his teacher's legacy. His role stands in instructive contrast to the more prominent leadership roles taken by other disciples, illustrating that Chabad's early infrastructure depended on figures working behind the scenes.
