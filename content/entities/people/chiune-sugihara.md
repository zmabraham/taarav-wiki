# Chiune Sugihara

**Born**: January 1, 1900, Yaotsu, Gifu, Japan  
**Died**: July 31, 1986, Kamakura, Japan  
**Role**: Japanese vice-consul in Kaunas (Kovno), Lithuania; issued thousands of transit visas to Jewish refugees, enabling the [[events/shanghai-rescue-1940|Shanghai rescue route]]

---

## Overview

Chiune Sugihara was a Japanese diplomat who, in defiance of his government's orders, issued approximately 2,000–6,000 transit visas to Jewish refugees in Lithuania in 1940. Together with Dutch consul [[entities/people/jan-zwartendijk|Jan Zwartendijk's]] Curacao stamps, his visas created the lifeline that allowed thousands of Jews — including approximately 40 Chabad yeshiva students — to escape through Japan to Shanghai.

---

## The Crisis in Lithuania

In the summer of 1940, Lithuania was occupied by Soviet forces and the situation for the large Jewish refugee population — which included both Lithuanian Jews and thousands who had fled the Nazi occupation of Poland — was desperate. The window of legal escape was rapidly closing.

Hundreds of refugees appeared outside the Japanese consulate in Kaunas, begging for transit visas to pass through Japan on their way to Curacao (the nominal destination provided by [[entities/people/jan-zwartendijk|Zwartendijk's]] stamps).

---

## Defying Orders

Sugihara cabled Tokyo three times asking permission to issue visas. Tokyo refused three times. The established policy was that visa applicants needed confirmed funds, onward passage, and a legitimate destination — criteria none of the refugees could easily meet.

Sugihara decided to act without authorization. Beginning in late July 1940, he spent 18-20 hours a day handwriting visas. He continued issuing visas even as his consulate closed and he was preparing to leave Lithuania. According to witnesses, he was still issuing visas through the train window as his train departed Kaunas.

---

## The Route and Its Beneficiaries

Those with Sugihara visas could board the Trans-Siberian Railway through Soviet-controlled territory, reach Vladivostok, and cross to Japan. From Kobe, Japan (where they were received by the local Jewish community and the Japanese government — which was conflicted about the influx), they eventually moved to Shanghai — then under neither Chinese nor Japanese authority and open to refugees without visas.

Among those who escaped via this route:

- Approximately 40 Chabad yeshiva students
- Approximately 200 students from the Mir Yeshiva (essentially the entire institution)
- Individual refugees from across Eastern Europe

The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] coordinated from Brooklyn — raising funds for transit fees ($500 per student from the Soviet side), negotiating with the US Embassy, and maintaining contact with students stranded at various points.

---

## Career Consequences and Recognition

Sugihara faced career consequences for his disobedience. After the war, he was asked to resign from Japan's foreign service — though the official reason was never stated directly.

He lived in relative obscurity for decades until the late 1960s, when survivors he had helped began searching for him. In 1984, Israel honored him as Righteous Among the Nations — one of a small number of non-Jews so designated for risking themselves to save Jews during the Holocaust.

---

## Legacy

Sugihara's visas — issued in defiance of government orders, driven purely by humanitarian conviction — saved thousands of lives. His story became one of the most celebrated examples of individual moral courage during the Holocaust era, comparable to Oskar Schindler and Raoul Wallenberg.

In Japan, he was honored posthumously and his hometown of Yaotsu built a memorial park in his honor.
