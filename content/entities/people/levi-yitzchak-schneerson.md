# Rabbi Levi Yitzchak Schneerson

**Born**: 18 Nissan 5638 (1878), Podolia
**Died**: 20 Av 5704 (1944), Alma-Ata, Kazakhstan
**Role**: Chief Rabbi of Dnepropetrovsk (Ekaterinoslav); renowned Kabbalist; father of the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]]
**Wife**: [[entities/people/chana-schneerson|Rebbetzin Chana Schneerson]]
**Children**: Menachem Mendel (born 1902), Yisrael Aryeh Leib, and a daughter

---

## Overview

Rabbi Levi Yitzchak Schneerson was the Chief Rabbi of Dnepropetrovsk (formerly Ekaterinoslav), one of the largest Jewish communities in Ukraine. A deeply learned Kabbalist and Chabad chassid, he is known both for his extraordinary scholarship — particularly in the mystical dimensions of Torah — and for his heroic resistance to Soviet religious persecution, which ultimately cost him his life.

He was the father of Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Lubavitcher Rebbe]]. The Rebbe's profound knowledge of Kabbalah and his approach to finding mystical dimensions in all aspects of Torah reflect the influence of his father's teachings.

---

## Early Life and Education

**Birth and Family**: Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was born in 1878 in Podolia, then part of the Russian Empire, to a distinguished Chabad family. From an early age, he exhibited extraordinary scholarly gifts and a particular affinity for Kabbalah — the mystical tradition of Judaism.

**Education**: He studied under some of the great Torah scholars of his generation, developing expertise in both the revealed (_nigleh_) and hidden (_nistar_) dimensions of Torah. His approach to Torah study was distinctive: he found Kabbalistic dimensions in every area of Jewish law and practice, producing innovative insights that connected these two spheres.

**Relationship with the Rebbes**: Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was deeply connected to the Chabad Rebbes of his generation. He maintained a close relationship with the [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rashab]] and later with the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]. These relationships shaped his approach to Torah and leadership.

---

## Marriage and Family

**Marriage to Chana Yanovsky**: Rabbi Levi Yitzchak married Chana Yanovsky, who came from a distinguished rabbinical family. She would become his life partner in every sense — supporting his work, bearing his children, and ultimately following him into exile.

**Children**: Their most famous child was Menachem Mendel, born in 1902, who would become the seventh Lubavitcher Rebbe. They also had a son named Yisrael Aryeh Leib and a daughter. The family lived in Dnepropetrovsk, where Rabbi Levi Yitzchak served as Chief Rabbi.

**The Young Menachem Mendel**: The future Rebbe's early education was heavily influenced by his father. From a young age, Menachem Mendel absorbed his father's approach to Torah study — finding the Kabbalistic underpinnings of even the most mundane halakhic details. This approach would become a hallmark of the Rebbe's own teachings.

---

## Chief Rabbi of Dnepropetrovsk

**Appointment**: Rabbi Levi Yitzchak became the Chief Rabbi of Dnepropetrovsk (then called Ekaterinoslav), one of the largest and most important Jewish communities in Ukraine. The city had a population of over 100,000 Jews and was a center of Jewish life in the region.

**Leadership Style**: As Chief Rabbi, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was known for his accessibility and his commitment to every member of the community, regardless of their level of observance. He maintained traditional Jewish life while also engaging with the challenges of modernity.

**Community Institution**: Under his leadership, Dnepropetrovsk maintained a robust Jewish communal life despite the surrounding upheavals. He supported Jewish education, kosher facilities, and religious observance — all of which would come under threat with the rise of Soviet power.

---

## The Soviet Period: Defiance and Persecution

Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's tenure as Chief Rabbi spanned the most turbulent period in Russian Jewish history: the final years of Czarist rule, the Russian Revolution, the Civil War, and the establishment of Soviet power.

**Maintaining Jewish Life**: Through all these upheavals, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak refused to abandon his post or compromise on Jewish observance. When the Soviets began closing synagogues and banning religious education, he continued to serve the community openly despite mounting pressure.

**Underground Activities**: As Soviet persecution intensified, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak supported underground religious activities. He secretly authorized circumcisions, performed marriages, and provided religious education — all at great personal risk. His home became a center of clandestine Jewish life.

**Defiance of Soviet Authorities**: Rabbi Levi Yitzchak refused to register as a "clergyman" with the Soviet authorities, which would have subjected him to state control. He refused to close the mikveh (ritual bath). He refused to compromise on kashrut standards. His defiance made him a target.

---

## Arrest (1939)

In 1939, the Soviet secret police (NKVD) finally moved against Rabbi Levi Yitzchak. His arrest was part of a broader campaign against religious leaders as Stalin tightened control over Soviet society.

**The Arrest**: Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was arrested by the NKVD in Dnepropetrovsk. The charges included "anti-Soviet activities" — essentially, continuing to serve as a rabbi and maintain Jewish religious life.

**Interrogation**: During interrogation, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was pressured to confess to various crimes and to denounce religion. He refused to compromise his principles despite harsh treatment. His interrogators reportedly could not understand why he would suffer rather than abandon what they considered superstition.

**The Sentence**: Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was sentenced to exile in Chi'ili (also spelled Chilli or Shilli), a remote settlement in Kazakhstan. This was effectively a death sentence — the harsh conditions and deprivation were intended to destroy him.

---

## Exile in Kazakhstan (1939-1944)

**Rebbetzin Chana's Devotion**: When Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was sentenced to exile, [[entities/people/chana-schneerson|Rebbetzin Chana]] made the extraordinary decision to follow him voluntarily into exile. Most people in her position would have stayed behind to care for their children and maintain some semblance of normal life. She chose instead to share her husband's fate.

**Conditions in Exile**: The settlement of Chi'ili was desperately poor, located in the vast steppe of Kazakhstan. There was little food, inadequate shelter, and no religious infrastructure. Rabbi Levi Yitzchak and Rebbetzin Chana endured tremendous hardship.

**The Homemade Ink**: One of the most celebrated stories of their exile concerns the making of ink. Rabbi Levi Yitzchak was determined to continue his scholarly work despite his circumstances, but he lacked ink. Rebbetzin Chana gathered herbs and berries from the surrounding steppe and prepared homemade ink so that he could write. The manuscripts produced with this ink — later published as _Likkutei Levi Yitzchak_ — represent one of the great treasures of Kabbalistic literature.

**Scholarly Work in Exile**: Despite the deprivations of exile, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak continued his scholarly work. He wrote commentaries on the Zohar, Tanya, and other foundational texts. These writings demonstrate extraordinary intellectual achievement under conditions that would have broken most people.

**Visitors**: Remarkably, even in exile, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak received visitors. Jewish soldiers stationed in the region during World War II came to see him, seeking blessings and guidance. Soviet Jews who knew of his whereabouts made dangerous journeys to consult with him on religious matters.

**Health Decline**: The years of deprivation took their toll on Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's health. He suffered from malnutrition and the effects of harsh conditions. Yet his spirit remained unbroken — he continued to write and teach until the very end.

---

## Death and Burial (1944)

Rabbi Levi Yitzchak passed away on 20 Av 5704 (1944) in Alma-Ata, Kazakhstan, weakened by the deprivations of exile. He was buried there in accordance with Jewish law, despite the difficulties of arranging a proper Jewish funeral under Soviet conditions.

**The Gravesite**: His grave in Alma-Ata (now Almaty) later became a place of pilgrimage. After the fall of the Soviet Union, Jews from around the world began visiting his gravesite. An _ohel_ (structure) was built over the grave, and it has become a destination for those seeking blessing and connection to this holy figure.

**Reburial Controversy**: There have been discussions over the years about reburying Rabbi Levi Yitzchak in Israel or at the _Ohel_ in New York (where the seventh Rebbe is buried). However, the seventh Rebbe reportedly opposed this, saying that his father's gravesite in Alma-Ata was sacred and should remain where it was.

---

## Likkutei Levi Yitzchak

Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's collected works, _Likkutei Levi Yitzchak_, were published posthumously by his family. They contain brilliant Kabbalistic commentaries on the Zohar, Tanya, and other foundational texts.

**Unique Approach**: The works demonstrate a unique approach: finding the Kabbalistic superstructure that underlies every detail of Jewish law and practice. Rabbi Levi Yitzchak showed how the mystical dimensions of Torah are not separate from the practical commandments but infuse them with meaning.

**The Zohar Commentary**: His commentary on the Zohar is particularly noteworthy. The Zohar, written in Aramaic, is one of the foundational texts of Kabbalah. Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's commentary illuminates obscure passages and connects them to broader Jewish themes.

**Influence on the Seventh Rebbe**: The [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]] frequently quoted his father's teachings in his own discourses, drawing particular attention to the Kabbalistic insights in _Likkutei Levi Yitzchak_. The Rebbe's own approach to Torah — finding mystical dimensions in every aspect of Jewish practice — clearly reflects his father's influence.

**Continued Publication**: New volumes of _Likkutei Levi Yitzchak_ continue to be published from Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's unpublished manuscripts. These writings, preserved by [[entities/people/chana-schneerson|Rebbetzin Chana]] during the exile years, continue to yield new insights.

---

## Relationship with the Seventh Rebbe

**Early Influence**: The seventh Rebbe's profound knowledge of Kabbalah and his approach to Torah study were heavily influenced by his father. From a young age, Menachem Mendel absorbed his father's teachings and methodology.

**Correspondence**: Even after the seventh Rebbe left the Soviet Union, he maintained correspondence with his parents in exile (when possible). The letters express deep love and reverence for his father's scholarship and spiritual example.

**The Yahrzeit**: After Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's death, the seventh Rebbe observed the yahrzeit (anniversary of passing) each year on 20 Av. He would often say a maamar (Chassidic discourse) connected to themes from his father's teachings.

**Request for Blessings**: Throughout his life, the seventh Rebbe would ask people to pray at his father's gravesite in Alma-Ata for blessings. This demonstrated his continuing connection to and reverence for his father.

## Stories and Anecdotes

**The Wedding**: At the wedding of his son Menachem Mendel to [[entities/people/rebbetzin-moussia|Rebbetzin Moussia]] in Warsaw (1928), Rabbi Levi Yitzchak reportedly danced with extraordinary joy. Witnesses said that his face radiated light as he celebrated this union.

**The NKVD Search**: When the NKVD came to arrest Rabbi Levi Yitzchak, they searched his house for contraband. They found his Torah writings but could not understand them. One officer reportedly said, "We can't charge him with this — we don't even know what it is."

**The Soldier's Visit**: During the war years in exile, a Jewish soldier visited Rabbi Levi Yitzchak. The soldier was amazed to find a great Torah scholar in such a remote place. Rabbi Levi Yitzchak told him, "Wherever Jews are, even in exile, Torah is there too."

**The Dream**: A story is told that Rabbi Levi Yitzchak had a dream in which he was shown that his son would one day become a great leader who would illuminate the world with Torah. He reportedly said nothing about this to anyone, but it gave him strength during the difficult exile years.

## Legacy

Rabbi Levi Yitzchak Schneerson is remembered as both a great scholar and a Soviet-era martyr — one who gave his life rather than abandon Torah and the Jewish community. His personal example of courage under persecution became a model for Jewish resilience.

**Scholarly Legacy**: His works continue to be studied for their Kabbalistic depth. The publication of previously unpublished manuscripts continues to this day, revealing new dimensions of his thought.

**Spiritual Legacy**: His example of _mesiras nefesh_ (self-sacrifice) for Torah continues to inspire Jews facing persecution. The story of his exile and [[entities/people/chana-schneerson|Rebbetzin Chana]]'s devotion has become legendary.

**Family Legacy**: Through his son, the seventh Rebbe, Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's influence has extended globally. The Chabad-Lubavitch movement, which the seventh Rebbe transformed into a global force, carries forward the spiritual legacy of Rabbi Levi Yitzchak's scholarship and dedication.

**The Yahrzeit**: The date of his passing, 20 Av, is observed annually in Chabad as a day of remembrance. Chassidim around the world study from _Likkutei Levi Yitzchak_ on this day, and many visit his gravesite in Alma-Ata.
