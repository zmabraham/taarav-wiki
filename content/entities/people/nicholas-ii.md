# Czar Nicholas II of Russia

**Born**: May 18, 1868, Tsarskoye Selo, Russia  
**Died**: July 17, 1918, Yekaterinburg (executed)  
**Reign**: 1894–1917 (abdicated)  
**Role**: Last Emperor of Russia

---

## Overview

Czar Nicholas II was the final Russian Emperor, whose reign (1894–1917) encompassed some of the most traumatic events in Russian Jewish history. For Chabad, his reign forms the backdrop against which the [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]] operated and the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] grew to maturity.

---

## Impact on Jewish Life

Nicholas II's reign was marked by severe antisemitism, both popular and state-sponsored:

- **Kishinev Pogrom (1903)**: One of the most devastating pogroms of the modern era, it shocked the Jewish world and galvanized international Jewish activism. The government's failure to prevent the violence — and suspicions of official complicity — deepened Jewish mistrust of the Czarist regime.
- **1905 Pogroms**: Following the failed revolution of 1905, a wave of pogroms swept through the Pale of Settlement, killing thousands and destroying communities.
- **The Beilis Affair (1911-1913)**: The Russian government prosecuted Mendel Beilis on fabricated charges of ritual murder ("blood libel"), revealing the depth of official antisemitism.
- **Pale of Settlement**: Jews remained confined to the Pale, with limited rights to reside in Russian cities proper.

## Chabad During Nicholas II's Reign

The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]] operated during much of Nicholas II's reign, navigating the complex realities of Jewish life under Czarist rule:

- The Rashab founded Tomchei Temimim (1897) partly in response to the cultural threats facing Jewish youth
- He engaged in political advocacy on behalf of Jewish communities
- He traveled to Western Europe (notably the [[events/wurzburg-year-1906|Würzburg year, 1906-1907]]) partly to build international support

---

## Revolution and Aftermath

Nicholas II abdicated in March 1917 following the February Revolution. He and his family were executed by the Bolsheviks on July 17, 1918, in Yekaterinburg.

The revolution that toppled Nicholas brought initial hope for Jewish emancipation but quickly gave way to the Soviet persecution that would prove even more destructive to Jewish religious life than the Czarist regime had been.
