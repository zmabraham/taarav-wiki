# Rabbi Shmaryahu Gurary (Rashag)

**Born**: 1898, Russia  
**Died**: 1989, Brooklyn, New York  
**Role**: Son-in-law of the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]]; Chabad administrator; known by the acronym "Rashag"

---

## Overview

Rabbi Shmaryahu Gurary — universally known by his acronym **Rashag** (Rabbi Shmaryahu Gurary) — was the husband of Chana Schneersohn, the elder daughter of the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] (sixth Rebbe). As the Rayatz's elder son-in-law, he occupied a significant position in the Chabad dynasty and served as a senior administrator of Chabad institutions in Brooklyn for decades.

His relationship with the seventh Rebbe — who married the Rayatz's younger daughter, [[entities/people/rebbetzin-moussia|Chaya Mushka]] — was complex, shaped by questions of dynastic succession, institutional authority, and personal temperament.

---

## Marriage and Family

Rashag married Chana Schneersohn, the Rayatz's eldest daughter. In Chabad dynastic tradition, the elder son-in-law typically held a position of prominence. Their son, Barry (Sholom Ber) Gurary, would later become a controversial figure in Chabad history.

The Rayatz's two daughters married two remarkable men: Chana married Shmaryahu Gurary, and [[entities/people/rebbetzin-moussia|Chaya Mushka]] married Menachem Mendel Schneerson. This created a dynastic situation with two potential successors — a pattern not uncommon in Hasidic history.

---

## Role Under the Rayatz

During the Rayatz's years in America (1940–1950), Rashag served in senior administrative roles within Chabad's institutional structure. He was involved in the management of Chabad's educational institutions and organizational affairs, working alongside figures like [[entities/people/hodakov|Rabbi Hodakov]] and [[entities/people/nissan-mindel|Dr. Nissan Mindel]].

He was a respected Torah scholar and chassid in his own right, though his temperament and approach differed markedly from his brother-in-law, the future seventh Rebbe.

---

## The Succession Question (1950–1951)

When the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] died on 10 Shevat 5710 (January 28, 1950), the question of succession was not immediately resolved. As the husband of the elder daughter, Rashag was considered by some as a natural candidate for the leadership. Chabad dynastic precedent was ambiguous — sometimes leadership passed to a son, sometimes to a son-in-law, and sometimes to a student.

During the year between the Rayatz's passing and the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe's]] formal acceptance of leadership (10 Shevat 5711 / January 1951), there was a period of uncertainty. Ultimately, the overwhelming consensus of the chassidim — and the weight of the Rayatz's own signals during his lifetime — pointed to Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson as the next Rebbe.

Rashag accepted the seventh Rebbe's leadership and continued serving Chabad institutions. However, the dynamics between the two families carried an undercurrent of tension that occasionally surfaced in subsequent decades.

---

## Institutional Service

After 1951, Rashag continued to serve in administrative and educational roles within Chabad. He was involved in the management of Chabad's yeshiva system and maintained a presence at 770 Eastern Parkway. He was respected as a senior figure in the community — a talmid chacham (Torah scholar) and elder chassid — even as the seventh Rebbe's charismatic leadership increasingly defined the movement.

---

## The Library Case

In the 1980s, a painful legal dispute known as "the library case" (_Agudas Chasidei Chabad v. Gourary_) brought the tension between the two families into public view. Rashag's son Barry Gurary had removed rare books and manuscripts from the Chabad library at 770, claiming they were personal family property inherited through the Rayatz. The Chabad organization argued they were communal property belonging to the movement.

The case was decided in 1987 in favor of Agudas Chasidei Chabad — the court ruled that the library belonged to the movement, not to the family. The Rebbe interpreted the court's decision as a spiritual vindication: the day of the ruling (5 Teves) became a day of celebration in the Chabad calendar, marked as "Didan Notzach" ("Our side won").

The case was deeply painful for all involved and represented one of the most difficult internal episodes of the seventh Rebbe's leadership.

---

## Passing

Rashag died in 1989 in Brooklyn. Despite the tensions that marked aspects of his relationship with the seventh Rebbe's leadership, he was mourned as a Torah scholar, devoted chassid, and member of the Chabad dynasty who served the movement for decades.
