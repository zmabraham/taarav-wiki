# Rabbi Mordechai Halevi Horwitz

**Printer and publisher; produced nearly all of the Alter Rebbe's printed works from 1796 to 1826**

## Biography

Rabbi Mordechai Halevi Horwitz was a pivotal figure in the material production and dissemination of Chabad literature during the formative period of the movement. From תקנ"ז (1796/7) — the year the Tanya was first printed in Slavuta — through approximately תקפ"ו (1826), Rabbi Mordechai Halevi Horwitz was the primary printer involved in publishing the Alter Rebbe's works and the works associated with the early Chabad movement.

His printing work encompassed the Alter Rebbe's major publications:
- **The Tanya** (Likkutei Amarim) — subsequent editions after the Slavuta first printing
- **Torah Or** — the Alter Rebbe's collected Torah discourses, published posthumously
- **Shulchan Aruch HaRav** — the Alter Rebbe's comprehensive halachic code, published after his death
- Various other chassidic texts associated with the Chabad movement

The printing of chassidic literature in this period was not merely a commercial enterprise — it was an act of religious mission. Each printed book was a vehicle for spreading the Alter Rebbe's teachings to Chabad communities across White Russia, Ukraine, and beyond. The quality and reliability of the printer mattered enormously: a printer who was committed to accuracy and who maintained a relationship of trust with the author's family would produce texts that could be relied upon, while careless printing led to the kind of manuscript corruption that had necessitated the Tanya's original publication.

The sources in Toldos Chabad BeRussia identify Rabbi Mordechai Halevi Horwitz as having been involved in virtually all major Chabad publications of the period, making him the de facto official printer of the Chabad movement across three decades. The printing center associated with his work was Kapust (Kopys), a town on the Dnieper River in White Russia that served as a major printing hub for Chabad literature.

## Significance

Rabbi Mordechai Halevi Horwitz represents the often-invisible infrastructure that made the Chabad intellectual and spiritual enterprise possible. The Alter Rebbe wrote; Rabbi Yehuda Leib of Yanowitz transcribed; but someone had to set the type, run the press, and ensure that accurate editions reached communities across a vast geographic area. Horwitz played that role across three decades, and his long partnership with the Alter Rebbe's family is testimony to the trust placed in him. The Kapust printing tradition he embodied became so central to Chabad that the town itself became associated with Chabad identity.
