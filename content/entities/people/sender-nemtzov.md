# Rabbi Sender Nemtzov

**Born**: c. 1890s, Russia  
**Died**: c. 1960s  
**Role**: Elder Chabad Chasid; proclaimed "Chassidim, we have a Rebbe!" at the seventh Rebbe's acceptance farbrengen (1951)

---

## Overview

Rabbi Sender Nemtzov was an elder Chabad Chasid who played a pivotal role at one of the most consequential moments in Chabad history — the acceptance of leadership by [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson]] as the seventh Lubavitcher Rebbe on 10 Shevat 5711 (January 17, 1951).

---

## The Farbrengen of 10 Shevat 5711

After the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] died on 10 Shevat 5710 (1950), a year of uncertainty followed. His son-in-law, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson — widely regarded as the most qualified successor — adamantly and repeatedly refused calls to become Rebbe.

On the first yahrzeit of the Rayatz's passing, a farbrengen (Chassidic gathering) was held at [[events/770-eastern-parkway|770 Eastern Parkway]]. The atmosphere was charged with expectation and grief.

Rabbi Sender Nemtzov — an elder Chasid whose seniority gave him standing to act — rose and called out to the Ramash: **"Reb Mendel, say a maamar!"** (a Chassidic discourse — the act that would constitute acceptance of the Rebbe's role).

After a moment of profound silence, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson began to speak. He delivered his first maamar as Rebbe, building his discourse on the last maamar the Rayatz had published before his death — a direct spiritual chain of transmission.

Rabbi Nemtzov leaped onto a table and proclaimed: **"Chassidim, we have a Rebbe!"**

See [[events/seventh-rebbe-acceptance-1951|Seventh Rebbe's Acceptance (1951)]].

---

## Significance

Nemtzov's action was not a spontaneous outburst but a deliberate act — he understood that someone had to take responsibility for creating the moment of crystallization. The elder Chasid, with his years of closeness to the previous Rebbe and his standing in the community, chose to be that person.

His proclamation — "Chassidim, we have a Rebbe!" — echoed as one of the most celebrated declarations in Chabad history, the moment when uncertainty resolved into joyful recognition.
