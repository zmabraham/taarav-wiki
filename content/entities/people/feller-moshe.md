# Rabbi Moshe Feller

**Born**: c. 1935  
**Role**: Chabad shliach to Minnesota; pioneer of Chabad in the American Midwest

---

## Overview

Rabbi Moshe Feller is one of the iconic shluchim of the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|Seventh Rebbe]], sent to Minneapolis-St. Paul, Minnesota in 1962 to establish a Chabad presence in the American Midwest. His story — building a thriving Chabad community in a small, isolated Jewish community in a cold and distant city — became emblematic of the entire [[concepts/shlichus|shlichus]] enterprise and its ethos of reaching every Jew, no matter how far from traditional Jewish centers.

---

## The Mission (1962)

In 1962, the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|Seventh Rebbe]] sent Feller to Minneapolis-St. Paul. Minnesota was about as far from the world of Crown Heights as one could imagine: a small Jewish community, brutally cold winters, and virtually no infrastructure for traditional Jewish life. The assignment embodied the Rebbe's principle that no Jewish community was too small or too remote to deserve a full-time emissary.

---

## Building Chabad of the Upper Midwest

Starting from nothing, Feller built Chabad of the Upper Midwest into a major network:

- **Chabad Houses** across the Twin Cities metropolitan area
- **Campus outreach** at the University of Minnesota and other institutions
- **Schools and educational programs** for children and adults
- **Holiday programming** and community events reaching thousands of Jews across Minnesota and neighboring states

His work extended beyond Minnesota itself, as the Upper Midwest region eventually encompassed Chabad centers across several states.

---

## Emblematic Story

Feller's shlichus became one of the most frequently cited examples of what the Rebbe's vision could accomplish. The image of a young Chassidic rabbi arriving in frozen Minnesota with nothing but the Rebbe's blessing and building a vibrant Jewish community captured the imagination of the Chabad world. His story was told and retold at [[entities/orgs/kinus-hashluchim|Kinus HaShluchim]] gatherings and in Chabad literature as proof that the shlichus model could work anywhere.
