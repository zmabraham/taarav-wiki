# Rebbe Rashab — Rabbi Shalom DovBer of Lubavitch

**כ"ק אדמו"ר מוהרש"ב נ"ע — The Fifth Rebbe**

**Born**: 20 Cheshvan 5621 (1860), Lubavitch
**Died**: 2 Nissan 5680 (1920), Rostov-on-Don
**Rebbe years**: 1882–1920
**Father**: [[entities/people/rebbe-maharash|Maharash]] (Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn)
**Mother**: Rebbetzin Rivkah
**Son**: [[entities/people/rayatz|Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak (Rayatz)]]
**Successor**: Son, [[entities/people/rayatz|Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak (Rayatz)]]

---

## Overview

Rabbi Shalom DovBer Schneersohn, the fifth Chabad Rebbe, presided over one of the most turbulent periods in Chabad history: the late Czarist era of pogroms, World War I, and the Russian Revolution. He is known both as a prolific author of deep Chassidic teachings — most notably the _Hemshech Samech-Vav_ (5666/1905) — and as a decisive communal leader who reshaped Chabad's institutional infrastructure in response to the challenges of modernity.

The Rashab (acronym for _Moreinu HaRav Shalom Ber_) led Chabad for 38 years, from 1882 until his death in 1920. His tenure saw the founding of the Tomchei Temimim yeshiva network, the establishment of Chabad institutions in the Holy Land, and the displacement from Lubavitch — the movement's spiritual home for over a century.

---

## Early Life and Education

**Birth and Family**: The Rashab was born on 20 Cheshvan 5621 (1860) in Lubavitch to the Maharash (Rabbi Shmuel Schneersohn, the fourth Rebbe) and Rebbetzin Rivkah. He was named Shalom DovBer after his great-grandfather the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]] (DovBer) and to embody peace (shalom).

**Childhood Stories**: One famous story from his childhood illustrates his early spiritual sensitivity. At age five, he cried during his father's _Tish_ (Chasidic gathering). When asked why, he explained that he saw his father's words ascending to heaven but was concerned whether they would have the desired effect. His father reportedly embraced him and said, "You will indeed illuminate the world with your Torah."

**Relationship with His Father**: The Rashab had an exceptionally close relationship with his father the Maharash. From an early age, he was privy to his father's private teachings and concerns. This intimacy prepared him for leadership but also made his father's passing in 1882 particularly difficult.

**Becoming Rebbe**: When the Maharash died on 13 Tevet 5643 (1882), the Rashab was just 21 years old. Despite his youth, he accepted leadership — though initially some Chasidim gravitated toward his uncle, Rabbi Yehuda Leib of Kopust (son of the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]]), who established a separate Chabad court in Kopust. The Kopust branch would continue for two generations before reuniting with the main Lubavitch line.

## The Founding of Tomchei Temimim (1897)

The Rashab's most lasting institutional achievement was founding the **Tomchei Temimim** yeshiva in Lubavitch in 5657 (1897). This revolutionary institution would transform Chabad and ultimately reshape world Jewry.

**The Request**: The yeshiva was requested by his son [[entities/people/rayatz|the Rayatz]] as "a wedding gift" at his marriage on 13 Elul 5657 (September 10, 1897). The Rashab convened 50 distinguished guests the following day and announced the plan, despite widespread skepticism. On Simchat Torah 1898, he named it _Tomchei Temimim_, from the verse recited during the seventh hakafah.

**Naming the Yeshiva**: The name _Tomchei Temimim_ ("Supporters of the Sincere") comes from Proverbs: "The sincere (temimim) are surrounded by His protection, He supports (tomchei) the temimim." This reflected the Rashab's vision of producing not just scholars but _temimim_ — whole, integrated human beings whose Torah learning permeated every aspect of their character.

**Curriculum Innovation**: Unlike Lithuanian yeshivas (Talmud only) or Musar institutions (ethics), Tomchei Temimim introduced a revolutionary curriculum:

- 8 hours daily of Talmud and halakha (Jewish law)
- 4 hours daily of Chassidic philosophy (_Chassidus_)
- A _mashpia_ (spiritual guide) as central educator, not merely a _rosh yeshiva_
- Aim to produce a _penimi_ (one who has internalized values), not merely a scholarly _gadol_
- In-house meals rather than the traditional _essen teg_ (day-eating) system
- Emphasis on character development and spiritual service

**Growth**: The yeshiva grew from 18 students in its first year to 200 within five years. This growth was all the more remarkable given the context of rising secularism and assimilation in Russian Jewry at the time.

**Ideological Context**: The Rashab justified the yeshiva as an antidote to the Enlightenment (_Haskalah_) and Zionist movements drawing Jewish youth away from tradition. "I am deeply apprehensive and pained by the frozen and parched spiritual level of religious Jews," he wrote. He also cited the need to counter _Chamah_ (Society to Promote the Enlightenment), funded by Baron Gunzburg, which had opened 150 secular Jewish schools by 1900 — including one in Lubavitch itself.

**The Temimim**: Graduates of the yeshiva were called _temimim_ — "sincere ones." This was not merely an educational designation but a spiritual identity. The Rashab saw in the students "soldiers of King David" who would "stand strong" for Jewish principles "in the four corners of the world." This proved prophetic — Tomchei Temimim alumni led the Soviet underground, survived Shanghai, and became the seventh Rebbe's army of global emissaries.

**Toras Emes in Hebron (1911)**: In 1911, the Rashab purchased Beit Romano in Hebron and established _Toras Emes_ — the first Chasidic yeshiva in the Land of Israel. See [[events/toras-emes-hebron-1911|Toras Emes Hebron]] for the full story. This extended the Tomchei Temimim network to the Holy Land and established Chabad's permanent presence there.

**Soviet Era**: During the Soviet years, Tomchei Temimim became a clandestine network operating across dozens of cities. These graduates — with their combination of Talmudic scholarship and Chassidic commitment — became the leadership core of Chabad in the Soviet era, exhibiting extraordinary dedication (_mesiras nefesh_) that would become legendary.

## Opposition to Zionism

The Rashab was the most vocal Chabad opponent of political Zionism, writing against it in strong terms that generated significant controversy. His position is extensively documented in his writings and correspondence.

**Theological Opposition**: The Rashab argued that Zionism substituted national political identity for the deeper spiritual essence of Judaism. He saw it as a secular movement that would distract Jews from their true mission — serving G-d through Torah and mitzvot — and replace it with nationalist aspirations.

**The Three Oaths**: Like many Orthodox leaders of his time, the Rashab invoked the "Three Oaths" from the Talmud (Ketubot 111a), in which the Jewish people swore not to ascend to Eretz Yisrael "as a wall" — by force or political means — and the nations swore not to oppress them excessively. Political Zionism, in his view, violated this oath.

**Distinction from Eretz Yisrael**: Importantly, the Rashab's opposition to Zionism did not translate into opposition to living in Eretz Yisrael. He founded Toras Emes in Hebron and encouraged Chassidim to settle there. His objection was specifically to political Zionism as a secular nationalist movement.

**Letters and Writings**: The Rashab's opposition to Zionism appears in many of his letters and public statements. He argued that the movement's secular leadership would corrupt Jewish youth and turn them away from Torah — a concern that seemed borne out by the subsequent history of secular Zionism.

**Controversy**: His anti-Zionist stance generated significant controversy, even within Chabad. Some Chassidim supported the Zionist movement. The Rashab maintained his position despite opposition, seeing it as a matter of principle.

## World War I and the Displacement from Lubavitch

When World War I reached the Russian-German front in 1915, the Rashab faced an agonizing decision. For over a century (since 1813), Lubavitch had been the spiritual center of Chabad under the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]], the [[entities/people/tzemach-tzedek|Tzemach Tzedek]], the Maharash, and himself. But now it was in the war zone.

**The Decision to Leave**: As German forces approached and the Russian army retreated, the Rashab made the painful decision to evacuate Lubavitch. The town was burned during the hostilities, ending over a century of its role as the Chassidic world center.

**Journey to Rostov**: The Rashab, his family, and many Chassidim undertook a perilous journey eastward to Rostov-on-Don, a city in southern Russia far from the front lines. The journey was difficult — food was scarce, refugees clogged the roads, and there was constant danger from military movements.

**Establishment in Rostov**: In Rostov, the Rashab re-established the Chabad court. Though the city had no history as a Chassidic center, it now became the movement's headquarters — the first time since 1813 that Chabad's center was not in Lubavitch. The transition marked a symbolic end to an era.

**The Synagogue in Rostov**: The Rashab established the _Choral Synagogue_ in Rostov, which became the center of Chabad life. This synagogue would later play an important role in Soviet-era Chabad history. See [[events/lubavitch-abandoned-1915|Lubavitch Abandoned 1915]] for more on this transition.

## The Russian Revolution and Final Years

The Rashab lived to see the Russian Revolution of 1917 but did not live to see its full consequences for Jewish religious life. His final years coincided with one of the most tumultuous periods in Russian history.

**The Bolshevik Seizure of Power**: When the Bolsheviks seized power in October 1917, the Rashab understood immediately that this represented an unprecedented threat to Jewish religious life. Unlike previous regimes that had persecuted Jews, the Communists sought to eliminate religion entirely.

**Preparing the Next Generation**: The Rashab spent his final years preparing his son and successor, the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], for the coming challenges. He transmitted prophecies and guidance about what would be required of Jewish leadership in the Soviet period.

**The Prophecy About Communism**: According to the Rayatz's diary, the Rashab made a prophecy in 1926: "There will be difficult times; you will need to have _mesirat nefesh bepoel_ (actual self-sacrifice). But eventually, the Communist government will disintegrate." This prophecy was fulfilled in 1990, though neither the Rashab nor the Rayatz would live to see it.

**Passing**: The Rashab died on 2 Nissan 5680 (March 21, 1920) in Rostov-on-Don. He had witnessed the Russian Revolution and the beginning of the Soviet assault on religion, but not the full horrors that would follow. His son [[entities/people/rayatz|Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak]] inherited leadership under dramatically more difficult conditions.

## The Hemshech Samech-Vav (5666)

The Rashab's most significant literary contribution was the _Hemshech Samech-Vav_ — an extended series of Chassidic discourses delivered during the year 5666 (1905-1908). This work is considered by many scholars to be the deepest sustained Chassidic discourse series in Chabad history.

**Structure**: The _Hemshech Samech-Vav_ consists of over 150 discourses delivered over approximately three years. Unlike earlier Chabad works which focused on specific topics, this series systematically explored the concept of _G-d's essence_ (_Etzem_) and its relationship to creation.

**Philosophical Depth**: The discourses explore profound metaphysical questions: How can finite creation relate to infinite G-d? What is the nature of divine transcendence vs. immanence? How do human beings access G-d's essence? The Rashab wove together sources from across the Chabad tradition, creating a systematic philosophical edifice.

**Impact**: The _Hemshech Samech-Vav_ has been studied intensively by subsequent generations. Both the Rayatz and the seventh Rebbe frequently cited it in their own discourses. It represents the apex of Chabad philosophical exposition — a work of such depth that it continues to yield new insights even after more than a century of study.

**Other Works**: In addition to the _Hemshech Samech-Vav_, the Rashab authored:

- _Kuntresim_ — multiple treatises on Chassidic service
- _Igrot Kodesh_ — extensive correspondence on halakha, communal issues, and spiritual guidance
- _Hemshech Ayin Beis_ — another significant discourse series (less extensive than Samech-Vav)

## Relationship with the German Jewish Community

The book [[summaries/chabad-germany|אדמו"רי חב"ד ויהדות גרמניה]] contains a section specifically on "the Rashab and World War One" — documenting his activities during the war related to German Jewry and the Chabad-German Jewish community relationship.

**The 1906 Würzburg Year**: During 1906, the Rashab spent time in Würzburg, Germany, where he developed relationships with German Jewish leaders. See [[events/wurzburg-year-1906|The Würzburg Year 1906]] for details on this significant period of cross-cultural exchange.

**German Rabbis**: The Rashab corresponded with prominent German rabbis, including Rabbi [[entities/people/azriel-hildesheimer|Azriel Hildesheimer]] and Rabbi [[entities/people/esriel-hildesheimer|Esriel Hildesheimer]]. These relationships demonstrated Chabad's ability to bridge different Jewish cultural worlds.

**World War I**: During World War I, the Rashab was in contact with German Jewish organizations about relief efforts for Jewish refugees. The war created complex dynamics between Russian and German Jewry, and the Rashab navigated these carefully.

## Stories and Anecdotes

**The Story of the Poor Man**: A famous story illustrates the Rashab's sensitivity. A poor man came to him complaining that he had no money to buy etrogim for Sukkot. The Rashab gave him his own personal etrog, saying, "Take mine — your joy in the mitzvah is greater than mine."

**His Attachment to Lubavitch**: Despite leaving Lubavitch in 1915, the Rashab never emotionally abandoned the town. He reportedly said, "Lubavitch is not a place — it is a state of being. Wherever the Rebbe is, there is Lubavitch."

**Relationship with His Son**: The Rashab had an exceptionally close relationship with his son and successor, the Rayatz. He began preparing him for leadership from an early age, sharing teachings and responsibilities. The famous "akidah" moment when the Rashab designated the Rayatz as his successor has become legendary in Chabad.

**The Dream About His Father**: The Rashab once related a dream in which he saw his father the Maharash, who told him: "You are succeeding in your mission — but the next generation will face challenges that will require even greater self-sacrifice." This proved prophetic.

## Legacy and Influence

The Rashab's influence on Chabad cannot be overstated. His institutional innovations — especially Tomchei Temimim — created the infrastructure that allowed Chabad to survive the Soviet period and later expand globally. His philosophical writings, particularly the _Hemshech Samech-Vav_, represent some of the deepest Chassidic teachings ever expounded.

**Temimim as Leaders**: The _temimim_ educated under the Rashab's system became the leadership core of Soviet-era Chabad. Their combination of Talmudic scholarship and Chassidic commitment enabled them to maintain Jewish life underground under conditions of extreme persecution.

**Global Expansion**: The Tomchei Temimim model — training young scholars in both revealed and hidden Torah — became the template for Chabad educational institutions worldwide. Every Chabad yeshiva today traces its pedagogical approach to the Rashab's innovations.

**Philosophical Foundation**: The Rashab's deep exploration of G-d's essence in the _Hemshech Samech-Vav_ provided the philosophical foundation for later developments in Chabad thought. Both the Rayatz and the seventh Rebbe built on this foundation.
