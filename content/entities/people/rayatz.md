# Rayatz — Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn

**כ"ק אדמו"ר מוהריי"צ נ"ע — The Previous Rebbe (Sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe)**

**Born**: 12 Tammuz 5640 (1880), [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]]
**Died**: 10 Shevat 5710 (1950), Brooklyn, New York
**Rebbe years**: 1920–1950
**Liberation date**: 12-13 Tammuz 5687 (1927) — celebrated as _Chag HaGeulah_ (Yud-Beis Tammuz)
**Father**: [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rebbe Rashab]] (Rabbi Shalom DovBer)
**Mother**: Rebbetzin Shterna Sara
**Wife**: [[entities/people/nechama-dina|Rebbetzin Nechama Dina]] (died 1922)
**Sons**: Israel Aryeh Leib (died young), Shalom Ber (died young)
**Daughters**: [[entities/people/sheina-horowitz|Sheina]] (died in Holocaust), Chaya Mushka (married [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]])
**Successor**: Son-in-law, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson (seventh Rebbe)

---

## Overview

Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn, the sixth Chabad Rebbe, presided over the most catastrophic and heroic period in Chabad history. His 30-year tenure (1920–1950) spanned Soviet persecution, arrest and near-execution, exile, the Holocaust, and the rebuilding of Chabad in America and globally. Chasidic historian Menachem Zigelbaum observed that the Rayatz "started from scratch three times": after his father's death in 1920, after arriving in Riga in 1927, and after reaching New York in 1940.

He is central to nearly every book in this wiki corpus and is the central subject of the English biography [[summaries/rayatz-biography|*Undaunted*]].

---

## Childhood and Formation in Lubavitch

The Rayatz was the eldest son of the Rashab, born into the small town of Lubavitch in White Russia. His childhood was marked by an exceptionally close relationship with his father and early indicators of extraordinary character.

**Birth and Early Years**: Born on 12 Tammuz 5640 (1880), he was named Yosef Yitzchak after his great-grandfather the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]]. From an early age, his father took a personal interest in his education and character development, recognizing in him spiritual gifts that would one day serve the entire Chabad community.

**The Jail Incident (age 11)**: One famous story from his childhood demonstrates his early sense of justice and leadership. When a Jewish blacksmith was unjustly imprisoned on Shabbat, the young Rayatz organized other children to break him out, having exhausted all legal options. The Rashab disciplined him publicly — but later acknowledged privately that the act showed unusual moral courage for a boy of eleven.

**The Akedah (age 15, 1895)**: The most significant moment of his youth came when the Rashab called his son into his private chambers and performed what he termed "the akedah" (binding of Isaac). The Rashab told the Rayatz that G-d had demanded he be "bound" as a sacrifice for the generation, and designated him as the next Rebbe. Both father and son wept at the gravity of this moment. The Rayatz later described this as the moment his life's mission became clear — he was no longer merely a son, but a leader designated for the Jewish people.

**Private Secretary (age 15 onward)**: From that moment in 1895, the Rayatz served as his father's private secretary, representing him at conferences in Kovno (1895) and Vilna (1896). He managed correspondence with Jewish leaders across Russia, traveled repeatedly to St. Petersburg to lobby government officials against anti-Semitic decrees, and accompanied the Rashab on missions across the empire. This early exposure to communal leadership prepared him for the responsibilities he would shoulder.

## Marriage and the Founding of Tomchei Temimim

**Engagement to Nechama Dina**: In 1896, the Rayatz became engaged to Nechama Dina Schneersohn, a second cousin. The Rashab bore all wedding expenses — an unusual arrangement that demonstrated his joy at this union.

**The Wedding Gift Request**: At the wedding itself on 13 Elul 5657 (September 10, 1897), the Rayatz made a special request of his father: open a yeshiva combining Talmud and Chassidic philosophy. He called this "one of the gifts I requested for my wedding."

**Founding of Tomchei Temimim**: In response to this request, the Rashab founded the [[events/tomchei-temimim-founding|Tomchei Temimim]] yeshiva, which opened 15 Elul 5657 (September 12, 1897) — just two days after the wedding. At age 18, the Rayatz became its director on 24 Elul 5659 (September 11, 1898). He served as director for 22 years, building it from 18 students to hundreds, establishing branches across Russia, and later expanding into Poland, Lithuania, Eretz Yisrael (as _Toras Emes_ in Hebron/Jerusalem), and eventually America. He considered the yeshiva his lifetime mission, entrusted to him by his father.

**Personal Tragedy**: Despite his public success, the Rayatz faced personal hardship. His first wife Nechama Dina passed away in 1922, leaving him a widower with young children. He later married Rebbetzin Moussia, who would accompany him through the Soviet years and beyond.

## Early Leadership and Soviet Resistance (1920–1927)

After inheriting leadership upon the Rashab's death in Rostov on 2 Nissan 5680 (1920), the Rayatz immediately confronted the Soviet state's systematic war on Jewish religion, led by the _Yevsektsiya_ (Jewish Section of the Communist Party).

**Rebuilding from Ruin**: The Rayatz began his leadership under devastating conditions. His father had just died. World War I and the Russian Revolution had destroyed Jewish communal infrastructure. The Bolsheviks were actively persecuting religion. The Chabad community was scattered and demoralized. The Rayatz had to rebuild everything from nothing.

**Underground Network**: From his base in Rostov, and later from Leningrad where he moved in 1924, the Rayatz organized an extraordinary underground network of Jewish life. This included:

- Secret yeshivos operating in cellars and hidden rooms
- Coded correspondence with chassidim across the Soviet Union
- Covert funding channels to support religious activity
- A network of messengers who traveled between communities at great risk
- Underground matzah baking operations for Passover
- Secret circumcisions and religious education

**Defiance of Soviet Authorities**: The Rayatz refused to compromise with Soviet demands to close religious institutions. When Soviet officials pressured him to register as a "clergyman" subject to state control, he refused. When they demanded he close his schools, he went underground. His defiance made him a target.

**Battle with the JDC**: The Rayatz also battled the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee (JDC) over its policy of supporting Jewish agricultural colonies instead of religious infrastructure. He argued that Jewish survival required spiritual life, not just economic survival.

**The Rashab's Prophecy**: The Rashab had made a prophecy, recorded in the Rayatz's diary in 1926: "There will be difficult times; you will need to have _mesirat nefesh bepoel_ (actual self-sacrifice). But eventually, the Communist government will disintegrate." This prophecy was fulfilled in 1990 with the collapse of the Soviet Union, though the Rayatz would not live to see it.

## Arrest and Liberation (1927) — The Shpalerka Prison

In 1927, the Soviet authorities decided to crush the Rayatz's underground network. On [[events/rayatz-arrest-1927|15 Sivan 5687 (June 15, 1927)]], GPU agents arrested him in Leningrad while he was reviewing the proofs of a new Chassidic work. This began the most dramatic chapter of his life.

**Shpalerka Prison**: The Rayatz was taken to the infamous Shpalerka prison in Leningrad (formerly the Spaso-Preobrazhensky Barracks). Conditions were horrific — he was placed in a tiny cell with no bed, given meager rations, and subjected to brutal interrogations. Yet he maintained his dignity and observance.

**The Death Sentence**: On 6 Tammuz 5687 (July 6, 1927), the Rayatz was brought before a tribunal of three GPU officers and sentenced to death. He accepted the sentence with equanimity, reciting Psalms. According to his account, he felt no fear — only a sense of having done his duty to the Jewish people.

**The International Campaign**: News of the arrest triggered an international outcry. Major Jewish organizations, Western governments, and prominent figures protested. Rabbi [[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Mordechai Dubin]] of Latvia lobbied Soviet officials. American Jewish leaders pressed the U.S. government. The Soviet authorities, facing international pressure at a delicate diplomatic moment, commuted the death sentence to 10 years in Solovki labor camp — then commuted it again to three years exile in Kostroma.

**Liberation**: The Rayatz was freed on [[events/rayatz-liberation-1927|12-13 Tammuz 5687 (1927)]] and allowed to leave the Soviet Union. His liberation is celebrated as _Yud-Beis/Yud-Gimmel Tammuz_, a Chabad holiday parallel in significance to 19 Kislev (Alter Rebbe's liberation) and 9-10 Kislev (Mitteler Rebbe's liberation).

---

## Riga (1927–1933): The Launchpad for Global Activity

The Rayatz arrived in Riga, Latvia on 25 Tishrei 5688 (October 21, 1927) — receiving a joyous reception from thousands of Chassidim who had been unable to see him for 14 years. But Riga was not home. The word "temporary" appeared on his stationery. "I fled the bad, but I have not yet found the good," he said.

**Letters to Russian Chassidim**: From Riga, the Rayatz sent 70 letters to Russian Chassidim within his first month of arrival — maintaining his connection to the community he had been forced to leave. These letters, smuggled across the border, provided spiritual sustenance to Jews living under Soviet persecution.

**Vaad Magen UMekhaseh**: The Rayatz founded **Vaad Magen UMekhaseh** (Committee for Protection of Russian Jewry) with major rabbis including the Rogotchover Gaon and Berlin's Chief Rabbi Hildesheimer. This organization advocated for Soviet Jews and documented religious persecution.

**The 1929 Passover Matzah Campaign**: One of the Rayatz's most remarkable achievements from Riga was the **1929 Passover Matzah Campaign** — an international diplomatic and fundraising effort to ship matzah into starving Soviet Russia. The operation involved:

- Coordinating with Ambassador Dubin in Riga
- Working with Germany's Jewish community in Berlin
- Organizing chief rabbis across Europe
- Smuggling matzah across the Soviet border at enormous risk
- Raising funds from Jewish communities worldwide

This campaign not only provided physical sustenance but sent a powerful message to Soviet Jews: they had not been forgotten.

**Diplomatic Initiatives**: The Rayatz developed a philosophy of "principled engagement" with Western society. He made extended trips to Berlin, Vienna, Paris, and Marienbad (for health). He embarked on a yearlong trip to Eretz Yisrael and America (1929–30) to build support for Soviet Jewry and strengthen international Chabad networks.

**Health Crisis**: During the Riga period, the Rayatz was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis — a degenerative disease that would gradually rob him of his physical strength but never dim his spiritual resolve.

**Key Ally**: Rabbi [[entities/people/mordechai-dubin|Mordechai Dubin]] of Latvia became a crucial ally — a devoted Chasid, member of Latvia's parliament, and chairman of Riga's Jewish community. Dubin served as the Rayatz's primary diplomatic channel with Russian and Latvian authorities, using his political influence to advocate for Soviet Jewry.

---

## Poland (1933–1939): The Last Haven in Europe

After six years in Riga, the Rayatz relocated to Warsaw in 1933 to be closer to Tomchei Temimim's Polish branch. He expanded a network of yeshivos across Poland and Lithuania, preparing — though he did not know it — for the coming catastrophe.

**The Warsaw Community**: In Warsaw, the Rayatz re-established a vibrant Chabad community. His daughter Sheina married Rabbi Menachem Mendel Wolff in a wedding held in Landwarow, Poland. It seemed like a time of rebuilding and growth.

**Nazi Invasion**: When Nazi Germany invaded Poland on September 1, 1939, the Rayatz was in Warsaw. As the city was bombed and the Nazi army approached, Chassidim desperately sought to evacuate him. With American diplomatic intervention, he was evacuated — reaching New York on March 19, 1940, after a harrowing journey through Latvia, Sweden, and aboard a ship that dodged German U-boats.

---

## America (1940–1950): "America Iz Nisht Anderesh"

**Arrival**: The Rayatz arrived on the SS _Drottningholm_, docking at Pier 97 in Manhattan in a wheelchair on March 19, 1940. When a reporter asked why he came to America, he rose from his wheelchair and declared: **"America iz nisht anderesh — America is no different. I came to make America a center of Torah."**

This statement was revolutionary. At the time, many Jewish leaders viewed America as a _treifene medina_ (unkosher land) where authentic Judaism could not survive. The Rayatz disagreed — and would prove it through action.

**770 Eastern Parkway**: The Chassidim searched for a suburban location for the Rayatz's headquarters; the Rayatz insisted on visible Crown Heights, Brooklyn. The three-story brownstone at 770 Eastern Parkway was purchased on 12 Av 5700 (August 16, 1940). It became the iconic Chabad world headquarters, with replicas built in 30+ locations worldwide from Jerusalem to Sydney.

**The State of American Orthodoxy**: The Rayatz arrived when Orthodoxy was in steep decline — 75% of New York Jews hadn't attended synagogue in the past year; Reform and Conservative movements dominated Jewish institutional life; intermarriage rates were rising. The prevailing wisdom was that America was a spiritual graveyard.

**Tomchei Temimim USA**: On his very first day in America, the Rayatz insisted on opening a yeshiva immediately: "If you do not promise me that you will open the yeshivah, I will not go to bed." Studies began one week later in Congregation Oneg Shabbat, East Flatbush, Brooklyn. This became the nucleus of what would grow into a global network of Chabad educational institutions.

**Shanghai Rescue**: The Rayatz coordinated the rescue of yeshiva students trapped in Europe during the Holocaust. Using the Curacao-Japan transit route (visas from Dutch consul [[entities/people/jan-zwartendijk|Jan Zwartendijk]] and Japanese consul [[entities/people/chiune-sugihara|Chiune Sugihara]]), Chabad students traveled Vilna → Moscow → trans-Siberia → Kobe → Shanghai. The Rayatz secured US visas, raised $500/student for Russian transit fees, and took personal loans when funds ran short. Approximately 40 Chabad students survived the war in Shanghai under Rabbi [[entities/people/meir-ashkenazi|Meir Ashkenazi]], a Chabad Chasid who served as Ashkenazi chief rabbi of Shanghai.

**The Holocaust**: The Rayatz lost his daughter [[entities/people/sheina-horowitz|Sheina]] and son-in-law Rabbi Menachem Mendel Wolff in the Holocaust. After the war, when photographs of fallen Chassidim surfaced, he wept uncontrollably — the burden of loss upon loss becoming almost too much to bear. Yet he found the strength to continue building.

**The Great Escape (1946–47)**: One of the most remarkable operations of the Rayatz's later years was the escape of approximately 1,000 Chabad Chassidim from the Soviet Union using forged Polish passports. The operation involved:

- A secret rabbinical court of 23 in Lvov setting guidelines
- Black-market passports and women serving as messengers (less conspicuous)
- Cattle cars from Lvov across the Polish border
- Coded communication with the Rayatz in Brooklyn
- The Rayatz personally providing $7,000 in bribes to free arrested Chassidim

During one border crossing on December 2, 1946, the Rayatz was found by his nurse staring into space, singing _Az Yashir_ (Song of the Sea), swaying as if walking. He suddenly said "They got through, they got through" — moments before a telegram arrived confirming 232 people had crossed safely. His spiritual connection to his flock was so deep that he sensed their liberation before receiving word.

**Global Dispersion**: The Rayatz choreographed the resettlement of refugee Chassidim around the world — France, Australia, Canada, South Africa, Cuba, Sweden. Each placement was understood not as finding haven but as a mission to transform that community. These refugees would become the seeds of future Chabad communities worldwide.

**Campus Outreach (1949)**: The Rayatz sent two yeshiva students to Brandeis University — the first formal Chabad campus outreach. This initiative would be expanded dramatically by the seventh Rebbe, starting at UCLA in 1969 and eventually reaching thousands of campuses globally.

**Kfar Chabad (1949)**: Just before his death, the Rayatz oversaw the establishment of Kfar Chabad in Israel for Soviet-era survivors settling in the Holy Land. See [[events/kfar-chabad-founding|Kfar Chabad Founding]] for the full story of how this village became a thriving center of Chabad life.

**The Last Years**: By the late 1940s, the Rayatz's multiple sclerosis had confined him largely to his home and wheelchair. Yet his mind remained sharp, his leadership undiminished. He continued to direct Chabad's global operations through written correspondence and meetings with key aides.

## Passing and Succession

**Death**: The Rayatz died on **10 Shevat 5710 (January 28, 1950)** in Brooklyn. He had lived through one of the most turbulent periods in Jewish history, witnessing the destruction of European Jewry and laying the foundations for its renewal.

**The Ohel**: He was interred at Montefiore Cemetery in Queens, New York, in a plot next to his father-in-law (the previous Rebbe). This gravesite, known as _the Ohel_, became a place of pilgrimage. In recent years, nearly one million people have visited the site annually, seeking blessings and connection to the Rayatz's spiritual legacy.

**Succession Crisis**: The Rayatz left no explicit written succession directive. After a year of uncertainty, his son-in-law Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson (the Ramash) accepted the Chabad leadership on **10 Shevat 5711 (1951)**. At a farbrengen at 770, the new Rebbe — who would become known as the seventh Rebbe — said his first maamar (Chassidic discourse), building on the Rayatz's final published maamar. Both are interred at Montefiore Cemetery; their Ohel has become one of the most visited Jewish sites in America.

---

## His Directive: Write Your Memoirs

One of the Rayatz's most important historical contributions was his instruction, after the mass emigration of Chassidim from Russia (1946–47), that survivors write down everything they remembered about Chabad underground activity. He understood that history was being lost and that future generations would need to know about the mesiras nefesh (self-sacrifice) of Soviet-era Jews.

This directive generated the memoirs that make up several books in this corpus, including _Zichronosai_ and _Zikaron LeBnei Yisrael_. Without this directive, much of what we know about Soviet-era Chabad would have been lost forever.

## Stories and Anecdotes

**The Maamar on the Train**: During his arrest in 1927, while being transferred by train, the Rayatz composed a Chassidic discourse (maamar) in his mind. When guards confiscated his writing materials, he recited it to himself, preserving it for later transcription. This demonstrated his complete focus on Torah even under extreme pressure.

**The Child Who Asked for Blessings**: A story is told of a young child who came to the Rayatz in Riga asking for blessings. The Rayatz reportedly asked the child what blessing he wanted. The child replied: "I want you to bless me that I should be a Chasid like you." The Rayatz wept.

**His Love for Children**: Throughout his life, the Rayatz had a special connection to children. He once said that he envied those who had the merit to attend his father's Torah classes as children — because he himself had been born late in his parents' lives and didn't have that experience. This made him particularly attentive to the education of Jewish youth.

**The Final Letter**: Shortly before his death, the Rayatz wrote a letter to the Chassidim saying that his work would continue through them. "I have not disappeared," he wrote. "I have only moved to a different place from which to help you."

## Key Teachings

**Hiskashrus (Connection)**: The Rayatz emphasized the importance of hiskashrus — spiritual connection between Chasid and Rebbe. He taught that this connection was not merely emotional but a real spiritual bond that transcended physical distance.

**L'chat'chilla Aribber (From Above)**: Building on his father's teaching, the Rayatz emphasized serving G-d from a place of transcendence ("from above") rather than merely overcoming obstacles ("from below"). This became a central theme in his later years.

**Shlichus (Emissary Work)**: Although the seventh Rebbe would dramatically expand the shlichus system, the Rayatz laid its foundations by sending emissaries to remote communities and establishing the principle that no Jewish community should be without Torah leadership.
