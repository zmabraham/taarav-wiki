# Kostroma (Designated Soviet Exile)

**City in north-central Russia; location designated as the Rayatz's place of exile after his death sentence was commuted in 1927; the Rayatz was eventually permitted to leave the USSR entirely instead.**

---

## Overview

Kostroma is a historic city in north-central Russia, situated on the Volga River northeast of Moscow. In the context of Chabad history, it is the city to which the Rayatz was sentenced to serve three years of internal exile after his death sentence was commuted following the international campaign for his release in 1927.

Soviet internal exile (ssylka) was a common tool of political repression. Rather than executing or imprisoning an individual indefinitely, the regime would assign them to a remote, difficult location where they were required to report regularly to authorities, had limited freedom of movement, and were separated from their home communities and support networks. Kostroma — northern, cold, remote from Moscow and Leningrad — would have been a challenging assignment.

In the event, the Rayatz never served his full Kostroma exile. The continued international pressure, diplomatic intervention, and broader political calculations of the Soviet government led to an additional commutation: the internal exile was converted to expulsion from the Soviet Union entirely. On October 3, 1927 (5 Tishrei 5688), the Rayatz left Soviet territory and traveled to Riga, Latvia, where he began a new phase of Chabad leadership in relative freedom.

## Significance in Chabad History

Kostroma represents a counterfactual in the Rayatz's biography: what might have happened. Had the death sentence been his final fate, the story would have ended in 1927. Had the exile to Kostroma proceeded without further intervention, the Rayatz might have served three years in a remote northern city, his movement leaderless or led at a distance under extreme constraint. The further commutation — expulsion rather than exile — was the outcome that enabled the remarkable Riga and Warsaw chapters of his leadership.

The escalating series of commutations (death sentence → three-year exile to Kostroma → expulsion from USSR → freedom in Riga) demonstrates the power of sustained international advocacy and the Soviet government's ultimate sensitivity to foreign opinion during this period. It also reflects the significance that the Soviet authorities attributed to the Rayatz's network — significant enough to warrant such extreme initial measures, but ultimately not worth the international cost of martyrdom.
