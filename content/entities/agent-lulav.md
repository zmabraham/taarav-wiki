# Agent Lulav (GPU Interrogator)

**GPU interrogator who conducted sessions with the Rayatz during his imprisonment in Spalerno Prison in 1927; famous for the encounter in which the Rebbe gave his celebrated response about having no fear.**

---

## Overview

Agent Lulav was the GPU interrogator who conducted interrogation sessions with Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (the Rayatz) during his imprisonment in Spalerno Prison in Leningrad following his arrest in June 1927. The encounters between Lulav and the Rayatz produced some of the most famous episodes in Chabad biographical tradition.

The most celebrated of these encounters involves Lulav threatening the Rebbe with a pistol — a standard Soviet intimidation tactic — to which the Rayatz reportedly responded: "This toy [the pistol] can only frighten someone who has one world and many gods. I have two worlds and one G-d, so this toy does not frighten me." The statement — expressing the Chassidic conviction that the spiritual world of the afterlife renders physical threats non-ultimate — became one of the most quoted expressions of Chabad's approach to mesirat nefesh (self-sacrifice).

A remarkable postscript, preserved in Chabad tradition: Agent Lulav is said to have subsequently converted to Judaism — a dramatic reversal attributed to the profound impression the Rebbe made during his imprisonment. Whether or not this report can be verified historically, it is part of the tradition of the 1927 arrest and reflects the impact the Rayatz had even on his interrogators.

## The Interrogations

The interrogations at Spalerno Prison were designed to break the Rayatz — to secure information about his network, to obtain statements that could be used against him or his associates, and to psychologically destroy his resistance. The GPU had substantial experience breaking prisoners through a combination of isolation, sleep deprivation, threats, and offers of leniency.

The Rayatz's response to these interrogations — maintaining complete calm, refusing to divulge information about his network, and meeting threats with principled theological statements — was extraordinary. His account of these sessions, recorded in his diaries and letters, presents a portrait of someone whose internal conviction rendered the GPU's arsenal of fear essentially ineffective.

The pistol incident crystallized this dynamic. The GPU's ultimate threat was death — the weapon represented the state's power to end a life. The Rayatz's response reframed the question entirely: a person who lives only for this world would indeed be terrified by that threat. But a person whose primary orientation is toward eternity has nothing that a pistol can take away. This response, whatever its precise historical form, captures the essence of the Chassidic approach to martyrdom and the courage the Rayatz displayed throughout his imprisonment.
