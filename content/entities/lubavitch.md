# Lubavitch (Historic Town)

**Historic town in White Russia (modern Smolensk Oblast, Russia); center of Chabad-Lubavitch for 102 years (1812–1915); origin of the movement's name.**

---

## Overview

Lubavitch (Lubavitchi in Russian, meaning "city of brotherly love") is a small town in what is now the Smolensk Oblast of Russia, near the Belarusian border. Though geographically obscure, it holds an outsized place in Jewish religious history as the home of the Chabad-Lubavitch movement for over a century. When Rabbi DovBer Schneuri (the Mitteler Rebbe) relocated the Chabad court to Lubavitch after the Alter Rebbe's passing in 1812, he established it as the permanent seat of the movement. Lubavitch gave Chabad its second name — Lubavitch — which persists in the movement's full designation: Chabad-Lubavitch.

For 103 years (1812–1915), Lubavitch was the spiritual and organizational center of Chabad. The courts of the Mitteler Rebbe, the Tzemach Tzedek, the Maharash, the Rashab, and the early years of the Rayatz all operated from Lubavitch. The Tomchei Temimim yeshiva — the flagship Chabad educational institution — was founded there in 1897. Generations of Chassidim made pilgrimages to Lubavitch to be near the Rebbe; thousands settled there permanently; its character as a Chassidic town was total.

World War I ended this era. In 1915, the advancing German army and the disruptions of war forced the Rashab and his family to flee Lubavitch. They settled temporarily in Rostov-on-Don, where the Rashab passed away in 1920. The town never again served as the movement's center.

## Significance in Chabad History

The word "Lubavitch" is not merely a geographical designation — it is a spiritual concept. In Chabad discourse, Lubavitch represents the era of open Chassidic community, of visible leadership, of the Rebbe in accessible relationship with his community. The loss of Lubavitch in 1915 was experienced as more than a physical displacement; it marked the end of an era of relative openness and the beginning of the age of persecution.

The Rayatz grew up in Lubavitch. His earliest memories — including the famous "Yarmarka Incident" of 1891, when a merchant challenged the young Yosef Yitzchak and his father the Rashab responded — are set in Lubavitch. His understanding of what Chabad should be was shaped by the Lubavitch of his childhood: a town organized around Torah study and Chassidic life, with the Rebbe's court at its center.

After 1915, "returning to Lubavitch" became a powerful metaphor in Chabad discourse. Even after the 7th Rebbe established 770 Eastern Parkway in Brooklyn as the movement's new headquarters, the memory of Lubavitch persisted as the paradigm of a Chassidic community fully devoted to the Rebbe and his teachings.
