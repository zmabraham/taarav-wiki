# Leningrad (Site of the 1927 Arrest)

**Soviet-era name for St. Petersburg; site of the Rayatz's arrest by the GPU on June 15, 1927 — one of the defining moments in Chabad history.**

---

## Overview

Leningrad — the Soviet name for the city of St. Petersburg (also known during different periods as Petrograd) — was the site of the Rayatz's arrest on June 15, 1927, and the city where he maintained his primary residence and leadership operations during the Soviet period. The midnight arrest at the Rayatz's Leningrad residence became one of the most iconic moments in the "Undaunted" biography and in Chabad historical memory generally.

Founded by Peter the Great in 1703 as Russia's imperial capital, the city was renamed Petrograd during World War I (to remove the German-sounding "burg") and then Leningrad after Lenin's death in 1924. After the fall of the Soviet Union, it reclaimed its original name, St. Petersburg, in 1991. During the Rayatz's Soviet-era leadership in the 1920s, it was Leningrad.

The city's significance in Chabad history is concentrated primarily in the 1927 arrest. The Rayatz used Leningrad as his base for directing the underground Chabad network — a city large enough to provide some anonymity, historically significant as Russia's cultural capital, and strategically positioned for communication. The GPU chose midnight on June 15, 1927 to raid his residence, arrest him, and take him to Spalerno Prison.

## Significance in Chabad History

The arrest scene in Leningrad on June 15, 1927 (14 Sivan 5687) crystallized the confrontation between Soviet totalitarianism and Chassidic Jewish life. The Rayatz was at home when GPU agents, led by Agent Nachmanson, arrived at midnight. The Rebbe was taken to Spalerno Prison, where he was interrogated by Agent Lulav in sessions that produced the famous exchange about "one who has two worlds and one G-d."

The city's role in the events of 1927 extended beyond the arrest itself. It was in Leningrad's Jewish community that the Rayatz had been most openly leading — conducting his underground network from an apartment in the city. The GPU's decision to arrest him there, rather than in a more remote location, reflected how public his activities had become despite the clandestine nature of the network.

After the events of 1927 — the arrest, the death sentence, the international campaign, the commutation, and the eventual expulsion — Leningrad ceased to be the center of Chabad leadership. The Rayatz's exile took him first to Kostroma, then eventually out of the Soviet Union entirely to Riga. Leningrad remained significant in Soviet Jewish history, but its Chabad chapter was largely complete.
