# Lubavitch — ליובאוויטש

**The Town That Named a Movement**

**Location**: Mogilev Governorate (now Smolensk Oblast, Russia) — approximately 25 km from the border with modern Belarus  
**Coordinates**: 54°18′N 31°27′E  
**Chabad Headquarters**: ~1813–1915 (102 years)  
**Current status**: Small Russian village; a Chabad museum opened there in 2001 (3 Nissan 5761)

---

## Overview

Lubavitch (Russian: Любавичи / Lyubavichi; Yiddish: לאָבאַוויטש) is the small town in the Mogilev district that served as the world headquarters of the Chabad movement for 102 years — from the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe's]] establishment of his court there (approximately 1813) through the [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rashab's]] departure in 1915 when war made the town uninhabitable.

The town's significance is such that the entire Chabad movement is universally known as "Lubavitch" or "Lubavitcher Chassidim" — the adjective has outlasted the town's practical role and become a permanent identity marker.

The book [[summaries/lubavitch|ליובאוויטש]] (published 2001 for the opening of the Lubavitch Museum) traces the town's history across all six Rebbe generations.

---

## Geography and Demographics

### Physical Geography

Lubavitch sits in the rolling plains of the historic Mogilev Governorate (Могилёвская губерния), part of the Pale of Settlement where Jews were permitted to live. The town is situated on small river tributaries in the Dnieper basin. The region is characterized by:

- Mixed forests of birch, pine, and oak
- Fertile agricultural land suitable for grain farming
- Cold continental winters and moderate summers
- Proximity to major trade routes connecting Moscow to Poland

### Pre-Chabad Jewish Community

The town was founded approximately 500 years before the Chabad era (i.e., around the 15th century). Jewish settlement began in the 17th century as part of the broader Jewish population expansion in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth's eastern territories.

By the time the [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]] arrived (~1813), Lubavitch had:

- A well-established Jewish community with synagogue, beis midrash, and mikvah
- Mixed Jewish and Christian population (shtetl structure)
- Agricultural economy supporting the surrounding countryside
- Typical shtetl architecture: wooden houses, dirt streets, central marketplace

## The Mitteler Rebbe's Arrival (~1813)

### Why Lubavitch Was Chosen

After the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] died in 1812 while fleeing Napoleon's armies, his son Rabbi DovBer (the Mitteler Rebbe) needed a new base. Lubavitch offered several advantages:

1. **Refuge from Napoleonic Aftermath** — The town had suffered less disruption than Liadi during the 1812 campaign
2. **Relative Isolation** — Far from major cities, offering privacy for the growing court
3. **Existing Community** — An established Jewish infrastructure could support the Rebbe's household
4. **Central Location** — Within the Pale of Settlement, accessible to chassidim from across Russia
5. **Local Acceptance** — The landowner and local authorities were generally tolerant

### Establishment of the Court

The Mitteler Rebbe established the _chatzer_ (court) — a complex including:

- The Rebbe's residence and study
- A main beis midrash (study hall)
- The _ohel_ (private prayer sanctuary)
- Guest quarters for visiting chassidim
- Support facilities (mikvah, kosher kitchen, offices)

This compound became the model for Chabad courts in subsequent locations.

## Layout of Chabad Lubavitch

### The Court Complex (החצר)

The heart of Lubavitch was the Rebbe's court, where thousands of chassidim gathered:

- **Main Beis Midrash**: Large study hall where the Rebbe delivered _maamarim_ (Chassidic discourses) on Shabbos and Yom Tov
- **The Ohel**: Private sanctuary used by the Rebbe for personal prayer and meditation
- **Rebbe's Residence**: Simple wooden house where the Rebbe lived with his family
- **Guest Inn**: Accommodations for visiting chassidim — often filled to capacity during _yamim noraim_ (High Holidays)
- **Mikvah**: Ritual bath essential to Jewish religious life
- **Printing Press**: Under the [[entities/people/tzemach-tzedek|Tzemach Tzedek]], Lubavitch became a major center for Chabad publications

### The Jewish Quarter

Surrounding the court was the Jewish quarter with:

- Additional synagogues for the local community
- Jewish-owned shops and businesses
- _cheder_ (elementary school) and _yeshiva_ (advanced study)
- Homes of prominent chassidim who lived close to the Rebbe

### Christian Lubavitch

The non-Jewish population lived separately, with:

- Orthodox church (typical of Russian towns)
- Homes of Christian farmers and merchants
- Local administrative buildings
- Generally peaceful coexistence with Jewish community

## Life in Lubavitch Under Each Rebbe

### Mitteler Rebbe (1813–1827)

- **Court Establishment**: Building the physical infrastructure of Chabad headquarters
- **Public Discourses**: Delivered foundational _maamarim_ expounding Chabad philosophy
- **Correspondence**: Directed network of chassidim across Russia through letters
- **Arrest in Vitebsk (1825)**: Even while based in Lubavitch, the Mitteler Rebbe was arrested in nearby Vitebsk on suspicion of subversion — reflecting Czarist suspicion of Jewish religious leadership
- **Community Building**: Established Lubavitch as a pilgrimage destination

### Tzemach Tzedek (1827–1866)

The longest reign in Lubavitch (39 years) saw the town's greatest expansion:

- **Population Growth**: Lubavitch swelled during _yamim tovim_ when thousands arrived
- **Publishing Empire**: Established printing press producing Chabad texts distributed worldwide
- **Tomchei Temimim**: Founded the central yeshiva system (1897, under his successors)
- **Correspondence Network**: Answered halachic and Chassidic questions from across the Jewish world
- **Political Navigation**: Managed relations with Czarist authorities while expanding religious activity
- **Famine Relief (1840s)**: Organized relief during crop failures, demonstrating community leadership

### Maharash (1866–1882)

- **Travels**: Unlike predecessors who rarely left Lubavitch, the Maharash traveled extensively, using Lubavitch as a base
- **Building Projects**: Expanded court facilities to accommodate growing crowds
- **Modernization**: Introduced certain improvements to court operations while maintaining traditional character
- **The Famous Saying**: "Lekach v'tzarah" — "We can do it the easy way or the hard way," reflecting his bold approach

### Rashab (1882–1915)

- **Tomchei Temimim Yeshiva**: Founded 1897 in Lubavitch — the flagship of Chabad education
- **Publishing Expansion**: Increased production of Chabad texts
- **Communal Institutions**: Established _chevra kadisha_ (burial society), free loan society, charity fund
- **Political Challenges**: Navigated increasing revolutionary activity and government suspicion
- **WWI Evacuation (1915)**: Forced to flee when the war front approached

## Famous Visitors and Residents

### Chassidim Who Came to Lubavitch

Lubavitch attracted visitors from across the Jewish world:

- **From Russia**: Moscow, St. Petersburg, Odessa, Kiev — wealthy merchants and scholars
- **From Lithuania**: Vilna, Kovno — centers of Torah learning
- **From Poland**: Warsaw, Lublin — established Jewish communities
- **From Ukraine**: Berdichev, Uman — famous Chassidic centers
- **International**: Even visitors from the Land of Israel and Western Europe made the journey

### Notable Figures Associated with Lubavitch

- [[entities/people/hillel-paritcher|Hillel Paritcher]] — Prominent mashpia (spiritual mentor) who lived in Lubavitch
- [[entities/people/yaakov-rosenheim|Yaakov Rosenheim]] — Later Frankfurt Jewish leader, visited as young man
- The founders of Chabad communities in [[entities/places/hebron|Hebron]] and [[entities/places/safed|Safed]] — received guidance from Lubavitch

## The 1915 Abandonment

### World War I Reaches Lubavitch

In 1915, World War I's eastern front moved toward Mogilev Governorate:

- **German Advance**: The German army pushed into Russian territory
- **Military Operations**: The area became a combat zone
- **Civilian Evacuation**: Russian authorities ordered civilian populations to move east
- **Destruction Risk**: The town faced bombardment and potential occupation

### Rashab's Departure

The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rashab]] made the difficult decision to leave Lubavitch in 1915:

- **Move to Rostov-on-Don**: Temporarily relocated to southern Russia
- **Court Evacuation**: Removed precious manuscripts and documents
- **Community Dispersal**: Chassidim and residents scattered across Russia
- **Hope for Return**: Initially expected to return after the war

### The 1921 Fire

In 1921 (beginning of Nissan 5681), disaster struck Lubavitch:

- The court complex burned down
- Years of Jewish history destroyed
- Symbolic end of the Chabad era in Lubavitch
- Occurred 80 years before the museum's opening (2001)

## Soviet Era and World War II

### Under Soviet Rule

After the Bolshevik Revolution (1917), Lubavitch became:

- Part of the Soviet Union's new territory
- Subject to anti-religious campaigns
- Deprived of its Jewish communal leadership
- A shadow of its former self

### The Holocaust in Lubavitch

During World War II (1941–1943):

- **Nazi Occupation**: Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941
- **Jewish Destruction**: The remaining Jewish community was almost certainly murdered
- **Liberation (1943)**: Red Army recaptured the area
- **Post-War**: Only a handful of Jews returned

## The Ohel (Rebbe's Tomb)

### Which Rebbes Are Buried in Lubavitch

Lubavitch contains the _ohel_ (tomb) of the fifth Chabad Rebbe:

- **Rabbi Shalom Dovber Schneersohn** (the Rashab, 1860–1920)

The Rashab died in [[entities/places/rostov|Rostov-on-Don]] in 1920 but was brought to Lubavitch for burial alongside his ancestors.

### Additional Burials

The ohel also contains the graves of other family members, though not all Rebbes:

- The [[entities/people/mitteler-rebbe|Mitteler Rebbe]] died in [[entities/places/nezhin|Nezhin]] (1827)
- The [[entities/people/tzemach-tzedek|Tzemach Tzedek]] died in Lubavitch (1866) and is buried there
- The [[entities/people/rebbe-maharash|Maharash]] died in Lubavitch (1882) and is buried there
- Earlier Rebbes ([[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]]) are buried in [[entities/places/haditch|Haditch]]

## Modern Pilgrimage to Lubavitch

### The Soviet Era (1920s–1991)

- **Restricted Access**: Soviet authorities made pilgrimage nearly impossible
- **KGB Surveillance**: Those who visited were monitored
- **Neglect**: The ohel fell into disrepair
- **Risk**: Visiting carried political danger

### Post-Soviet Revival

After the Soviet Union collapsed (1991):

- **1990s Visits**: Chabad chassidim began returning
- **Restoration**: The ohel was cleaned and repaired
- **Museum Project**: Plans for a permanent memorial

### The Lubavitch Museum (2001)

On 3 Nissan 5761 (March 2001), a Chabad museum opened in Lubavitch:

- **Building**: Restored structure on historical site
- **Exhibits**: Artifacts, photographs, documents from Chabad's years in Lubavitch
- **Dedication**: Attended by Chabad representatives from around the world
- **Symbolism**: 80 years after the 1921 fire that destroyed the court

### Contemporary Pilgrimage

Today, visitors to Lubavitch find:

- A small village with few remaining Jewish residents
- The ohel of the Rashab, Tzemach Tzedek, and Maharash — still a site of prayer
- The museum commemorating the town's glory days
- Occasional organized pilgrimages by Chabad groups
- Connection to 102 years of Chabad history

## Lubavitch in Memory and Name

### The Name Endures

Though the movement is now headquartered in [[entities/places/770|770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn]], Chabad is universally known as "Lubavitch":

- **Self-Identification**: Chassidim call themselves "Lubavitchers"
- **Institutions**: Schools, synagogues, and organizations worldwide named "Lubavitch"
- **Public Recognition**: Even outsiders recognize "Lubavitch" as synonymous with Chabad
- **Historical Continuity**: The name links contemporary Chabad to its historical heartland

### The Town's Legacy

Lubavitch represents:

- **102 years as world Chabad center** (1813–1915)
- **Four generations of Rebbes** who lived and led from there
- **Thousands of _maamarim_** delivered in the beis midrash
- **Hundreds of thousands** of chassidim who pilgrimage there
- **The birthplace of Tomchei Temimim yeshiva** (1897)
- **The physical location** of the Rashab's ohel
