# Tashkent — טשאַקענט

**Major Central Asian Hub for Chabad Refugees During World War II**

**Location**: Uzbekistan, Central Asia (then Soviet Uzbek SSR)
**Chabad Significance**: Capital of Uzbekistan; major evacuation destination for Chabad families fleeing Nazi invasion (1941-1945)
**Current status**: Capital of independent Uzbekistan; small Jewish community

---

## Overview

Tashkent (Uzbek: Toshkent; Russian: Ташкент / Tashkent; Yiddish: טאַשקענט) was one of the two principal Central Asian cities — alongside [[entities/places/samarkand|Samarkand]] — that sheltered Chabad refugees during World War II. As the capital of the Uzbek Soviet Socialist Republic and the largest city in Soviet Central Asia, Tashkent served as a major reception center for Jews evacuated from the western Soviet Union following Nazi Germany's invasion in June 1941.

Thousands of Chabad chassidim passed through or settled in Tashkent during the war years. The city's existing Jewish community, relatively tolerant local population, and position far from the front lines made it a natural destination for refugees from Belarus, Ukraine, and European Russia. After the war, Tashkent served as a gathering point for Chabad families preparing to participate in [[events/great-escape|the Great Escape]] (1946-47).

---

## Geography and Demographics

### Physical Geography

Tashkent is located in northeastern Uzbekistan:

- **Location**: In the Chirchiq River valley, near the border with Kazakhstan
- **Region**: Historic Uzbekistan; Soviet Uzbek SSR capital (1930-1991); independent Uzbekistan capital (1991-present)
- **Terrain**: Oasis city on the fertile plains at the foot of the Tian Shan mountains
- **Climate**: Continental — hot, dry summers; cold winters; low rainfall
- **Elevation**: Approximately 400 meters (1,300 feet) above sea level

### The City in the 1940s

During the wartime period, Tashkent was:

- **The largest city in Central Asia**, with a population of approximately 500,000
- **Administrative center** of the Uzbek SSR (capital moved from Samarkand in 1930)
- **Industrial and transport hub** with rail connections to Moscow and the rest of the Soviet Union
- **Relatively untouched by combat**, making it a safe haven from the eastern front

## The Jewish Community

### Pre-War Jewish Population

Tashkent had a mixed Jewish population before the war:

- **Bukharan Jews**: An indigenous community with roots stretching back centuries, speaking Bukhori (Judeo-Tajik)
- **Ashkenazi Jews**: Smaller numbers of European Jews who had settled in Tashkent under the Russian Empire
- **Religious Infrastructure**: Synagogues, mikvaos, and Jewish communal organizations
- **Relations**: Generally peaceful coexistence with the Muslim majority

### Wartime Refugee Influx

The Nazi invasion of June 1941 triggered a massive population displacement:

- **Soviet Evacuation Policy**: Stalin authorized the systematic relocation of industries, institutions, and populations eastward
- **Jewish Evacuation**: Jews from the western USSR — particularly those in major cities — were evacuated by train
- **Tashkent as Destination**: The city received enormous numbers of refugees, including a substantial Jewish contingent
- **Chabad Families**: Hundreds of Chabad families from Belarus, Ukraine, and Russia were among the evacuees

## Chabad Refugees in Tashkent

### Origins of the Refugee Community

Chabad families in Tashkent came from across the western Soviet Union:

- **From Belarus**: Families from [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]], [[entities/places/nevel|Nevel]], [[entities/places/homel|Homel]], [[entities/places/vitebsk|Vitebsk]]
- **From Ukraine**: Families from [[entities/places/kremenchug|Kremenchug]], [[entities/places/odessa|Odessa]], [[entities/places/kiev|Kiev]], [[entities/places/berdichev|Berdichev]]
- **From Russia**: Families from [[entities/places/moscow|Moscow]] and other cities
- **Underground Network**: Many were active participants in the Chabad underground under Soviet rule

### Community Life

Despite wartime hardships and Soviet restrictions, Chabad refugees maintained religious life:

- **Secret Minyanim**: Prayer services conducted in private apartments, hidden from authorities
- **Underground Education**: Torah study for children and adults continued in clandestine settings
- **Kosher Food**: Ritual slaughterers (_shochtim_) provided kosher meat at great personal risk
- **Mikvah**: Ritual baths were arranged secretly, often using natural water sources
- **Holiday Observance**: Jewish holidays celebrated privately, sometimes in hiding from neighbors

### Differences from Samarkand

While [[entities/places/samarkand|Samarkand]] became the larger Chabad refugee center, Tashkent had distinct characteristics:

- **Larger City**: More urban environment with greater economic opportunities
- **Transit Hub**: Many refugees passed through Tashkent en route to Samarkand or other destinations
- **Administrative Center**: Proximity to government offices, useful for obtaining documents and permits
- **Mixed Population**: More diverse refugee population, including non-Chabad Jews

## Wartime Conditions

### Hardships

Chabad refugees in Tashkent faced severe difficulties:

- **Overcrowding**: Severe housing shortage; multiple families sharing single rooms
- **Food Shortages**: Wartime rationing and limited food supplies
- **Disease**: Typhus, malaria, and other diseases spread in crowded conditions
- **Climate**: The hot Central Asian summer was unfamiliar and difficult for refugees from northern climates
- **Separation**: Many families had been separated during the chaotic evacuation
- **Soviet Surveillance**: Despite the war, the NKVD continued monitoring religious activity

### Economic Survival

Refugees adapted in various ways:

- **Factory Work**: Many were assigned to wartime factories and industries
- **Skilled Crafts**: Artisans and craftsmen found work in their trades
- **Trade**: Small-scale commerce in the local markets (_bazaars_)
- **Mutual Aid**: Community members supported each other through charitable networks
- **Correspondence**: Coded communication with the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] in America brought both spiritual encouragement and financial support

## The Postwar Period and the Great Escape

### War's End (1945)

With Germany's surrender in May 1945, Chabad refugees faced a critical decision:

- **Return Home**: Should they return to their destroyed communities in Belarus and Ukraine?
- **Stay in Central Asia**: Remain in the relative safety of Uzbekistan
- **Attempt Escape**: Try to leave the Soviet Union entirely

### The Great Escape (1946-47)

Tashkent played a key role in [[events/great-escape|the Great Escape]]:

- **Gathering Point**: Chabad families in Tashkent obtained forged Polish identity documents
- **Moscow Transit**: Many traveled from Tashkent to [[entities/places/moscow|Moscow]], where they boarded trains westward
- **Route**: Trans-Siberian Railway to [[entities/places/berlin|Poland]], then to displaced persons camps
- **Final Destinations**: Most reached [[entities/places/eretz-yisrael|Eretz Yisrael]] or America
- **Those Left Behind**: Some families remained trapped in the Soviet Union for decades

---

## Later History

### Soviet Period (1945-1991)

After the war:

- **Remaining Community**: A small Chabad presence persisted in Tashkent
- **Soviet Restrictions**: Religious life continued under severe limitations
- **Bukharan Jews**: The indigenous Jewish community maintained its traditions
- **Emigration**: Gradual emigration to Israel, particularly after the 1970s

### Independent Uzbekistan (1991-present)

- **Mass Aliyah**: Most of Tashkent's Jews emigrated to Israel in the 1990s
- **Small Remnant**: A small Jewish community remains today, including a Chabad-operated synagogue and community center
- **Chabad Shluchim**: Chabad has established a permanent presence in independent Uzbekistan

---

## Tashkent's Place in Chabad History

Tashkent represents a crucial chapter in the Chabad wartime experience:

- **Refuge**: Thousands of Chabad Jews found safety from Nazi genocide within its borders
- **Continuity**: Religious life was maintained despite Soviet oppression and wartime hardship
- **Gateway**: The city served as the departure point for the Great Escape that seeded Chabad communities worldwide
- **Memory**: Tashkent's role in Chabad survival is preserved in family histories and memoirs
