# Ponary (Paneriai) — פאנאר

**Site of the Vilna Massacres**

**Location**: Approximately 10 km southwest of Vilnius (Vilna), Lithuania  
**Significance**: Mass execution site where ~100,000 people were murdered during the Holocaust  
**Chabad Connection**: Chabad community of [[entities/places/vilna|Vilna]] was among the victims

---

## Overview

Ponary (Lithuanian: Paneriai; Polish: Ponary; Yiddish: פאנאר / Ponar) is a suburb of Vilnius, Lithuania, that became one of the Holocaust's most notorious mass murder sites. Between July 1941 and July 1944, Nazi SS units and their Lithuanian collaborators systematically murdered approximately 100,000 people at Ponary — the vast majority of them Jews from [[entities/places/vilna|Vilna]] and surrounding areas. The site stands as a memorial to the destruction of Lithuanian Jewry and the vibrant Jewish world that was annihilated.

For Chabad, Ponary represents the tragic culmination of the complex relationship between Chabad and Vilna. Although Vilna was historically the center of Mitnagdic opposition to Chassidism, a small Chabad community had developed in the city by the 20th century, and chassidim from Vilna were among those murdered at Ponary.

---

## The Massacres (1941-1944)

### Pre-War Context

Before the Nazi invasion, Vilna was home to approximately 60,000 Jews — one of Eastern Europe's greatest Jewish communities. When Nazi Germany occupied Lithuania in June 1941, the destruction began almost immediately. Lithuanian nationalist collaborators, organized into death squads known as the Ypatingasis Burys (Special Squad), carried out mass killings with local knowledge and enthusiasm.

### The Killing Site

Ponary had been the site of Soviet fuel storage facilities before the war. Large pits had been excavated for fuel tanks, and these pre-existing pits became mass graves. Victims were brought by train and truck from Vilna, forced to strip, and shot at the edge of the pits. The systematic nature of the killings — organized, methodical, and relentless — made Ponary one of the most efficient murder sites in the Holocaust.

### Scale of Murder

Approximately 70,000 Jews, along with up to 20,000 Polish intellectuals and resistance fighters, and several thousand Soviet POWs, were murdered at Ponary. The Jewish victims included men, women, and children from all walks of life — scholars, workers, the pious, and the secular alike. The massacres are documented in detail on the [[events/ponary-massacres|Ponary Massacres]] event page.

## The Burning Brigade

One of Ponary's most remarkable stories is that of the "Burning Brigade" — a group of Jewish and Polish prisoners forced by the Nazis to exhume and burn the corpses to destroy evidence of the massacres (known as Aktion 1005). Beginning in late 1943, approximately 80 prisoners labored under horrific conditions, burning thousands of bodies.

The prisoners secretly dug an escape tunnel over a period of months. On April 15, 1944, approximately 40 prisoners escaped through the tunnel. Eleven survived the war and provided testimony about the atrocities at Ponary. Their escape was one of the most dramatic acts of resistance during the Holocaust.

---

## Memorial and Legacy

Today, Ponary is a memorial site. A monument was erected by the Soviet government after the war, and subsequent memorials have been added, including ones specifically acknowledging Jewish victims. The pit graves remain visible, and the site serves as a place of pilgrimage and remembrance.

For the Jewish world, and for Chabad specifically, Ponary symbolizes the catastrophic loss of Lithuanian Jewry. The [[entities/people/vilna-gaon|Vilna Gaon's]] community, the great yeshivas, the rich cultural and religious life of Vilna — all were consumed at Ponary. The site stands as an enduring reminder of both the magnitude of the destruction and the necessity of remembrance.
