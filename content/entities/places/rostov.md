# Rostov-on-Don

**Chabad's Final Russian Headquarters (1915–1920)**

---

## Overview

Rostov-on-Don — a major city in southern Russia at the mouth of the Don River — served as the headquarters of Chabad from **1915 to 1920**. The [[entities/people/rebbe-rashab|Rashab]] relocated here when WWI made [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]] untenable, and died here in 1920. His son the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] continued operating from Rostov until the increasingly dangerous Soviet conditions forced a move to Leningrad and ultimately to exile abroad.

---

## Why Rostov?

In 1915, with the German-Russian front approaching Lubavitch and the town itself being burned, the Rashab needed a new headquarters. Rostov-on-Don was chosen because:

- It was far from the front (in the south)
- It had a significant Jewish community
- It offered relative stability during the upheaval of WWI

---

## The Rashab in Rostov (1915–1920)

The Rashab spent his final five years in Rostov. Despite failing health, he:

- Continued leading the Chabad movement
- Dictated teachings and letters
- Watched with anguish as the Russian Revolution (1917) transformed the landscape overnight
- Died on **2 Nissan 5680 (April 1920)** in Rostov

His _ohel_ (grave) in Rostov remains a pilgrimage destination for Chabad chassidim — one of the most important in the Diaspora.

---

## The Rayatz in Rostov (1920–1924)

After the Rashab's death, the Rayatz continued operating from Rostov while organizing the underground Tomchei Temimim network across the Soviet Union. By the early 1920s, the Soviet crackdown made Rostov increasingly dangerous. The Rayatz eventually relocated to Leningrad (Petersburg), where he was arrested by the GPU in 1927.

---

## Significance

Rostov represents the transition point:

- From the Czarist era (where Chabad operated openly in Lubavitch) to the Soviet underground
- From the Rashab's era to the Rayatz's
- From Russia as home to Russia as occupied territory

The Rashab's burial in Rostov (rather than Lubavitch, which was burned) means the sacred site of the fifth Rebbe is in what is now a Russian provincial city — reachable by Chabad pilgrims since the Soviet collapse (1990).
