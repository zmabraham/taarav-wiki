# The Lubavitch Courtyard

**The compound of buildings in Lubavitch that served as the headquarters of the Chabad movement from the era of the Mitteler Rebbe through the Rayatz; destroyed by fire in 1921**

## Overview

The Lubavitch courtyard (in Yiddish: "der hoif") was the physical center of the Chabad movement from approximately the 1820s through 1915. It consisted of a series of buildings including the Rebbe's residence, the main synagogue, study halls, the yeshiva, guest houses for visiting Chassidim, and administrative facilities.

## Construction and Development

The courtyard was built up gradually across multiple generations:

- **Mitteler Rebbe era** — Initial establishment of the Lubavitch center after the Mitteler Rebbe settled there
- **Tzemach Tzedek era** — Expansion of facilities as tens of thousands of Chassidim came annually
- **Maharash era** — Further construction; R' Avraham Zeligson served as town rabbi during this period
- **Rashab era** — Most significant expansion, including buildings for Tomchei Temimim (founded 1897); R' Dovid Yakovson served as town rabbi

## Key Structures

The courtyard complex included:
- **Beit HaMidrash** — Main synagogue and study hall where the Rebbe davened
- **The Rebbe's House** — Personal residence of the Rebbe and his family
- **Yeshiva Buildings** — Facilities for Tomchei Temimim students
- **Guest quarters** — Accommodations for the constant flow of visiting Chassidim
- **Mikveh** — Ritual bath

## World War I and Evacuation

During World War I (1914–1915), when the front approached Lubavitch, the Rashab evacuated the town and relocated to Rostov-on-Don. The courtyard buildings were left behind and suffered damage during the war years.

## The 1921 Fire

In 1921, a major fire devastated the Lubavitch courtyard, destroying the main buildings including the Rebbe's residence. The fire was a painful symbol of the destruction of the Chabad capital during the revolutionary era. By this time the Rayatz had already established the center in Rostov and then Leningrad, so while the fire was traumatic, it did not disrupt the movement's functioning.

## Later History

After the 1921 fire, the physical site of the Lubavitch courtyard fell into decline under Soviet rule. Decades later, a museum was established at the site.
