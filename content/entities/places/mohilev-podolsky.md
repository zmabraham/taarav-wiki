# Mohilev-on-Dniester (Mogilev-Podolsky / Mohyliv-Podilskyi)

**Town on the Dniester River at the Ottoman border; stopover for the Alter Rebbe during the 1777 attempted aliyah**

## Overview

Mohilev-on-Dniester — also known as Mogilev-Podolsky (Russian: Могилёв-Подольский) or Mohyliv-Podilskyi (Ukrainian: Могилів-Подільський) — is a town in the Podolia region on the Dniester River, which formed the border between the Russian Empire (or earlier the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth) and the Ottoman Empire. It is an entirely different city from Mogilev (Mahilyow) in Belarus.

Its location on the Ottoman frontier made it an important transit point for Jews traveling toward the Land of Israel, which lay within the Ottoman Empire. The river crossing here was one of the principal routes for travelers seeking to reach Eretz Yisrael from Eastern Europe.

## The Alter Rebbe's 1777 Stopover

In תקל"ז (1777), Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Vitebsk led a major aliyah of White Russian Chassidim to the Land of Israel. This aliyah, the largest Chassidic emigration to Eretz Yisrael of the eighteenth century, included hundreds of Chassidim and several senior figures.

The young Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi (the Alter Rebbe) initially intended to join this aliyah. He traveled south with the group and stopped at Mohilev-on-Dniester, poised at the border crossing. However, at Mohilev-on-Dniester, the Alter Rebbe made the decision — apparently after deliberation and possibly after receiving some form of divine guidance or instruction from Rabbi Menachem Mendel — not to continue to the Land of Israel. He turned back to White Russia.

This decision at Mohilev-on-Dniester was consequential for Chabad history: had the Alter Rebbe emigrated to Israel with Rabbi Menachem Mendel, there would have been no dominant leader left in White Russia to guide the developing Chabad community, and the movement's subsequent history would have been entirely different. The sources present the Alter Rebbe's return as a form of self-sacrifice — giving up the spiritual opportunity of settling in Eretz Yisrael in order to serve the communities of Russia.

## Later History

Mohilev-on-Dniester remained an important Jewish community through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The town's Jewish population was largely destroyed during the Holocaust, when Romanian forces — who occupied the area as part of Transnistria — carried out mass killings.
