# 770 Eastern Parkway

**The Heart of the Chabad World**

---

## Overview

**770 Eastern Parkway**, Brooklyn, New York — universally known simply as "770" — is the world headquarters of the Chabad-Lubavitch movement. Purchased on **12 Av 5700 (August 16, 1940)** by the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]], it has served as the movement's nerve center for over 80 years. Its distinctive red-brick façade has been replicated in dozens of Chabad communities worldwide.

---

## The Purchase (1940)

When the Rayatz arrived in America in March 1940, Chabad was searching for a home. The Rayatz's American aides — led by Rabbi Yisrael Jacobson — initially sought a property in a suburban setting, expecting the Rebbe would prefer quiet surroundings. The Rayatz insisted otherwise: he wanted to be in the heart of a Jewish neighborhood where he could be accessible.

The three-story brick building at 770 Eastern Parkway in the Crown Heights neighborhood of Brooklyn was purchased on **12 Av 5700 (August 16, 1940)** for approximately $7,500.

The address became the global address of Chabad: "770 Eastern Parkway, Brooklyn, New York."

---

## The Building in the Rayatz Era

Under the Rayatz (1940–1950), 770 became:

- The Rebbe's private residence (upper floors)
- The headquarters of the three Chabad organizations founded in 1942:
  - **Merkos L'Inyonei Chinuch** (educational arm)
  - **Kehot Publication Society** (publishing arm)
  - **Machne Israel** (social services arm)
- The location of Tomchei Temimim USA (eventually relocated to separate facilities)
- A gathering place for Chabad chassidim in America

---

## 770 Under the Seventh Rebbe

When the [[entities/people/rebbe-7th|seventh Rebbe]] accepted leadership on **10 Shevat 5711 (1951)**, 770 became the center of an explosive global expansion. The main synagogue at 770 hosted:

- The Rebbe's farbrengens (Chassidic gatherings) — often lasting 4-6 hours, attended by thousands
- Yechidus (private audiences) — the Rebbe met with thousands of individuals per year
- Major Jewish holidays — Tishrei gatherings drew tens of thousands to Brooklyn
- The Rebbe's daily prayers (with the public)

The Rebbe's farbrengens, held on Shabbat, major holidays, and special occasions, were broadcast by radio and later video worldwide. His transcribed talks (Likkutei Sichos, Igrot Kodesh) were published by Kehot from this address.

---

## Physical Expansion

Over the decades, 770 expanded:

- A large extension was added to the rear of the building
- Adjacent buildings were purchased for offices, yeshiva classrooms, and living quarters
- The main sanctuary was expanded multiple times
- By the 1980s, 770 hosted thousands for major farbrengens

---

## The 770 Replicas

The significance of 770 to Chabad chassidim led to a remarkable phenomenon: **exact replicas of the 770 facade have been built** in Chabad communities worldwide, including:

- Kfar Chabad, Israel
- Buenos Aires, Argentina
- Sao Paulo, Brazil
- Melbourne, Australia
- Johannesburg, South Africa
- Paris, France
- Netanya, Israel
- And dozens of others

The replica tradition expresses the chassidic principle that the Rebbe's headquarters is spiritually present wherever Chabad is.

---

## After the Seventh Rebbe's Passing (1994)

The Rebbe's library, office, and private rooms at 770 have been preserved largely as he left them. Hundreds of thousands visit annually. The building remains the global center of Chabad-Lubavitch operations.

The Rebbe's private library — containing thousands of rare volumes, many with his handwritten annotations — is housed in an adjacent building and maintained by the Chabad library (headed by [[entities/people/shalom-dov-levin|Rabbi Shalom DovBer Levin]]).

---

## Cultural Significance

770 appears on:

- Millions of Chabad publications, posters, and mezuzahs
- The Kehot Publication Society's logo
- Countless pieces of Chabad art
- The global Chabad website (chabad.org)

For Chabad chassidim, 770 is not merely a building — it is the _makom_ (place) of the Rebbe, carrying a spiritual weight parallel to Jerusalem in the Jewish imagination.
