# Warsaw — ווארשע

**The Rayatz's Headquarters and Largest Jewish City in Europe (1933–1939)**

**Location**: Poland (independent republic 1918–1939; under Nazi occupation 1939–1945)  
**Chabad Headquarters**: 1933–1939 — the Rayatz's base before WWII  
**Jewish Population**: Approximately 350,000 Jews (over 30% of city population) — largest Jewish community in Europe  
**Current status**: Capital of Poland; small Jewish community

---

## Overview

Warsaw (Polish: Warszawa; Yiddish: ווארשע / Varsha) was home to the largest Jewish community in Europe and served as the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz's]] headquarters from 1933 to 1939. As the capital of independent Poland, Warsaw offered the Rayatz access to a massive Jewish population, robust communal institutions, and relative stability — at least until the Nazi invasion of 1939.

During six crucial years, the Rayatz rebuilt his movement in Warsaw, establishing a Chabad presence in one of the world's great Jewish centers. Warsaw was also the site of the dramatic September 1939 escape, as the Rayatz fled the advancing Nazi army in a journey that would eventually bring him to the United States.

The Warsaw chapter of Chabad history ends tragically with the destruction of Polish Jewry in the Holocaust, including the Warsaw Ghetto and its uprising. The vibrant Chabad community of Warsaw was virtually annihilated along with the rest of Polish Jewry.

## Geography and Demographics

### Physical Geography

Warsaw is located in east-central Poland:

- **Location**: On the Vistula River, approximately 350 km from the Baltic Sea
- **Region**: Historic Mazovia; capital of independent Poland (1918–1939)
- **Terrain**: River valley with relatively flat surrounding plains
- **Climate**: Continental — cold winters, warm summers

### Warsaw's Jewish Community

Before the Holocaust, Warsaw had the largest Jewish community in Europe:

- **Population**: Approximately 350,000 Jews (over 30% of city population) by 1939
- **Diversity**: Wide spectrum from ultra-Orthodox to completely secular
- **Institutions**: Hundreds of synagogues, schools, yeshivos, cultural organizations
- **Economy**: Jews prominent in trade, manufacturing, professions, and crafts
- **Politics**: Active Zionist, socialist, Bundist, and Orthodox movements
- **Culture**: Center of Hebrew and Yiddish culture, theater, literature, and press

## Poland in the Interwar Period (1918–1939)

### Political Context

Independent Poland (1918–1939) was complex for Jews:

- **New State**: Poland regained independence after 123 years of partition
- **Democracy**: Initial democratic period gave way to authoritarian rule (1926 coup)
- **Jewish Status**: Jews were Polish citizens but faced discrimination and antisemitism
- **Economic Policies**: Government policies often disadvantaged Jewish businesses
- **Rising Antisemitism**: 1930s saw increased antisemitic incidents and policies

### Jewish Life in Interwar Warsaw

Despite challenges, Jewish life flourished:

- **Religious Diversity**: Hasidic courts (including Ger, Alexander, Sochatchov, and Chabad), Mitnagdic, secular
- **Education**: Network of religious and secular schools
- **Press**: Vibrant Yiddish and Hebrew press
- **Politics**: Multiple Jewish political parties represented in parliament
- **Culture**: Theaters, libraries, cultural organizations

## The Rayatz's Arrival in Warsaw (1933)

### From Riga to Warsaw

The Rayatz moved from [[entities/places/riga|Riga]] to Warsaw in 1933:

- **Reasons for Move**:
  - Warsaw's much larger Jewish population (350,000 vs. Riga's 40,000)
  - More central location in European Jewish world
  - Greater institutional resources
  - Political concerns about Latvia's stability
- **Arrival**: Warm welcome from Warsaw's Jewish community
- **Establishment**: Set up court and institutions

### First Days in Warsaw

- **Residence**: Established home in Warsaw's Jewish district
- **Institutions**: Began building Chabad presence
- **Public Activities**: Delivered discourses, received visitors
- **Community Building**: Attracted followers to Chabad

## The Rayatz's Court in Warsaw

### Location and Structure

The Rayatz established his court in Warsaw:

- **Primary Residence**: Home in Warsaw (often spent summers in [[entities/places/otwock|Otwock]])
- **Synagogue**: Used or established synagogue for Chabad services
- **Study Hall**: Space for Torah study and receiving visitors
- **Office**: Administrative center for Chabad affairs worldwide

### Daily Life

The Rayatz's routine in Warsaw included:

- **Teaching**: Delivered _maamarim_ (Chassidic discourses)
- **Receiving Visitors**: Chassidim who traveled from across Poland
- **Correspondence**: Communication with underground Soviet Chabad and global network
- **Writing**: Composed works and correspondence
- **Fundraising**: Collected funds for various Chabad causes

Warsaw's Jews responded to the Rayatz:

- **Chabad Followers**: Existing Chabad chassidim welcomed their Rebbe
- **Broader Community**: Respect for the Rayatz's courage and scholarship
- **Media Coverage**: Jewish and general press covered his activities

- **Ger (Góra Kalwaria)**: Largest Hasidic group in Poland
- **Alexander**: Major court
- **Sochatchov**: Prominent court
- **Chabad's Position**: One of many respected but smaller courts

## The Years 1933–1939

### Building and Expansion

The Rayatz's years in Warsaw were productive:

- **1933–1936**: Establishing institutions, building community
- **1937–1939**: Consolidation, preparation for coming challenges
- **Otwock Connection**: Summers spent in Otwock health resort

### Rising Storm Clouds

Even as life continued normally, dangers approached:

- **German Resurgence**: Nazi Germany's growing power
- **Polish Instability**: Political tensions within Poland
- **Antisemitism**: Rising antisemitic incidents and policies
- **International Tension**: Growing threat of war

## The September 1939 Escape

### Nazi Invasion of Poland

- **September 1, 1939**: Germany invaded Poland, starting World War II
- **Blitzkrieg**: Rapid German advance overwhelmed Polish defenses
- **Bombing of Warsaw**: Luftwaffe bombed Warsaw repeatedly
- **Chaos**: City descended into chaos as government and civilians fled

### The Rayatz's Escape

The Rayatz's dramatic escape from Warsaw:

- **Decision to Flee**: Realized Poland would fall to Nazis
- **Escape Route**: Traveled from Warsaw to [[entities/places/otwock|Otwock]], then eastward
- **Journey**: Dangerous journey through war-torn Poland
- **Captured by Soviets**: Briefly held by Soviet forces
- **Release**: After intervention, allowed to continue journey
- **Journey to America**: Via [[entities/places/riga|Riga]], Sweden, and eventually the United States

### Final Departure from Poland

- **Last Days**: Leaving behind Chabad institutions and community
- **Faith in Future**: Despite tragedy, the Rayatz planned for rebuilding
- **Arrival in America**: Reached the United States in March 1940

## The Holocaust in Warsaw

### Nazi Occupation (1939–1945)

Warsaw under Nazi rule:

- **Terror**: Immediate persecution of Jews and Polish leadership
- **Forced Labor**: Jews conscripted for forced labor
- **Starvation**: Ghetto deliberately starved
- **Deportations**: Mass deportations to Treblinka death camp (1942)
- **Uprising**: Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (1943)

### Warsaw Ghetto

- **Establishment**: Ghetto established November 1940
- **Conditions**: Horrific overcrowding, starvation, disease
- **Deportations**: Summer 1942 saw mass deportations to death camps
- **Uprising**: April–May 1943 saw heroic but doomed uprising
- **Destruction**: Ghetto destroyed after uprising

### Fate of Warsaw's Chabad Community

- **Murder**: Most Warsaw Chabad families were murdered in the Holocaust
- **Survivors**: Very few survived; those who did escaped early or hid
- **Destruction**: All Chabad institutions destroyed
- **Memory**: The vibrant community exists only in memory

## Postwar Warsaw

### Communist Era (1945–1989)

Under communist rule:

- **Tiny Community**: Very few Jews returned to Warsaw
- **Suppression**: Religious life severely restricted
- **Emigration**: Most survivors emigrated to Israel or America
- **Memory**: Communist government minimized Jewish history

### Post-Communist Poland (1989–present)

Since fall of communism:

- **Democracy**: Poland became democratic
- **Jewish Revival**: Small renewal of Jewish life
- **Historical Recognition**: Poland has acknowledged its Jewish history
- **Memory**: Museums and memorials commemorate prewar Jewish community

## Modern Warsaw

### Contemporary Jewish Life

Today's Warsaw:

- **Population**: Approximately 1.7 million (tiny Jewish minority)
- **Jewish Community**: Small community of approximately 1,000–2,000 Jews
- **Institutions**: Synagogue, community center, Jewish Museum
- **Chabad Presence**: Limited Chabad activity

### Historical Memory

- **Former Sites**: Locations of Jewish institutions marked
- **Memorials**: Monument to Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (1948)
- **Museums**: POLIN Museum of History of Polish Jews (2013)
- **Recognition**: Warsaw's Jewish past increasingly acknowledged

## Warsaw's Place in Chabad History

### Peak of European Chabad

Warsaw represented:

- **Largest Community**: Access to 350,000 Jews
- **Institution Building**: Establishing Chabad presence in major Jewish center
- **European Hub**: Coordinating Chabad activities across Europe
- **Preparation**: Preparing for challenges ahead

### Tragic Loss

The Holocaust destroyed:

- **People**: Most Warsaw Chabad families murdered
- **Institutions**: All community institutions destroyed
- **Potential**: The vibrant future that might have been
- **Memory**: Lives on in memoirs and historical records

### Continuity

Despite destruction:

- **Survivors**: Those who escaped continued the tradition
- **Rayatz's Leadership**: The escape allowed rebuilding in America
- **Memory**: Warsaw remembered as tragic but important chapter
