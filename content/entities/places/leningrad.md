# Leningrad (St. Petersburg) — לענינגראד / פעטערבורג

**Capital City and Site of Chabad Rebbe Arrests**

**Location**: Northwest Russia (on the Neva River, Baltic Sea)  
**Names**: St. Petersburg (1703–1914, 1991–present); Petrograd (1914–1924); Leningrad (1924–1991)  
**Chabad Significance**: Site of Alter Rebbe's arrests (1798, 1800) and Rayatz's arrest (1927); location of Shpalerka prison  
**Current status**: Saint Petersburg, Russia's second-largest city

---

## Overview

Leningrad (known today as Saint Petersburg) was the capital of Imperial Russia until 1918 and remains one of Russia's most important cities. In Chabad history, Leningrad is infamous as the site of multiple arrests of Chabad Rebbes — the [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe]] in 1798 and 1800, and the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] in 1927.

The Shpalerka prison in Leningrad became legendary in Chabad memory as the place where the Alter Rebbe and Rayatz were imprisoned. Their arrests, interrogations, and subsequent releases became defining moments in Chabad history, exemplifying _mesirus nefesh_ (self-sacrifice) in the face of persecution.

## Geography and Demographics

### Physical Geography

Saint Petersburg is located in northwest Russia:

- **Location**: On the Neva River at the head of the Gulf of Finland
- **Coordinates**: 59°56′N 30°20′E — same latitude as Anchorage, Alaska
- **Terrain**: River delta with numerous canals; built on marshland
- **Climate**: Humid continental — cold winters, moderate summers, White Nights in summer

### Historical Names

The city has had multiple names reflecting political changes:

- **St. Petersburg** (1703–1914): Founded by Peter the Great; capital of Imperial Russia
- **Petrograd** (1914–1924): "Peter's City" — anti-German name during WWI
- **Leningrad** (1924–1991): Named after Vladimir Lenin after his death
- **Saint Petersburg** (1991–present): Original name restored after Soviet collapse

### Jewish Population

Historical Jewish community:

- **Settlement**: Jews were generally prohibited from living in St. Petersburg until the late 19th century
- **Cantonists**: Jewish conscripts to the Russian army were stationed there
- **Permission to Reside**: Certain categories of Jews (merchants, professionals, skilled artisans) could receive residence permits
- **Population**: By early 20th century, approximately 10,000–20,000 Jews (small minority in a city of 2 million)

## The Alter Rebbe's Arrests (1798, 1800)

### First Arrest (1798)

The [[entities/people/alter-rebbe|Alter Rebbe's]] first arrest was a watershed moment:

- **Denunciation**: Mitnagdic opponents in [[entities/places/vitebsk|Vitebsk]] region accused the Alter Rebbe of treason
- **Charge**: Accused of sending funds to Palestine to support the Ottoman Empire against Russia
- **Arrest**: Arrested and brought to St. Petersburg
- **Imprisonment**: Held in the St. Petersburg prison for 53 days
- **Interrogation**: Repeatedly interrogated by authorities
- **Release**: Released on 19 Kislev 5559 — celebrated as a Chabad holiday
- **Exoneration**: Fully exonerated; the charges were proven false

### Second Arrest (1800)

Just two years later:

- **Denunciation**: Again accused of subversion
- **Arrest**: Arrested a second time
- **Imprisonment**: Held in St. Petersburg
- **Release**: Again released after investigation cleared him
- **Pattern**: Established a pattern of persecution and exoneration

### Historical Significance

The arrests demonstrated:

- **Czarist Suspicion**: Government viewed organized Jewish religious movements as suspicious
- **Mitnagdic Hostility**: Opponents were willing to inform to authorities
- **Mesirus Nefesh**: The Alter Rebbe's willingness to suffer for Judaism
- **Divine Providence**: The releases were seen as miraculous

## The Rayatz's Arrest (1927)

### Background

The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] led Chabad under increasingly dangerous Soviet conditions:

- **Underground Activity**: Directed clandestine Torah education and religious observance
- **Suspicion**: Soviet authorities viewed him as a counter-revolutionary
- **Decision to Arrest**: GPU (secret police) ordered his arrest

### Arrest in Leningrad (1927)

- **Date**: Arrested on 15 Sivan 5687 (June 15, 1927)
- **Location**: Arrested while visiting Leningrad
- **Imprisonment**: Held in the Shpalerka prison
- **Initial Sentence**: Sentenced to death
- **Commutation**: Sentence commuted to exile; later changed to expulsion from USSR
- **International Pressure**: World leaders and Jewish organizations campaigned for his release
- **Release**: Freed on 3 Tammuz 5687 (July 3, 1927) — celebrated as Chabad holiday
- **Expulsion**: Immediately ordered to leave Soviet Union

### The Shpalerka Prison

The Shpalerka (Шпалерная тюрьма) became legendary:

- **Location**: Central prison in Leningrad
- **Conditions**: Harsh conditions, poor food, isolation
- **Interrogation**: Repeated interrogations attempting to extract confessions
- **Spiritual Resistance**: The Rayatz maintained his faith and dignity
- **Symbol**: The prison came to symbolize Jewish resistance to persecution

## Leningrad's Jewish Community

### Czarist Period (1703–1917)

- **Restrictions**: Jews generally prohibited from living in the capital
- **Exceptions**: Certain categories could receive residence permits
- **Cantonists**: Jewish conscripts stationed in the city
- **Gradual Relaxation**: Restrictions slowly eased in late 19th century

### Revolutionary and Early Soviet Period (1917–1927)

- **Initial Freedom**: Brief period of religious freedom after the Revolution
- **Yevsektsiya**: Jewish Section of Communist Party targeted religious life
- **Persecution**: Increasing restrictions on religious activity
- **Arrests**: Rabbis and religious leaders arrested

### Soviet Period (1927–1991)

- **Suppression**: Severe restrictions on Jewish religious life
- **Underground**: Secret religious observance
- **Holocaust**: Nazi siege of Leningrad (1941–1944) devastated the city
- **Postwar**: Limited Jewish cultural life under communism

---

## The Siege of Leningrad (1941–1944)

### Nazi Siege

During World War II, Leningrad suffered one of history's worst sieges:

- **September 1941**: German and Finnish forces surrounded Leningrad
- **Duration**: Siege lasted 872 days (September 1941–January 1944)
- **Starvation**: Over 1 million civilians died, primarily from starvation and cold
- **Bombing**: Constant artillery and air bombardment
- **Jewish Victims**: Many Jews died during the siege; others were murdered by Einsatzgruppen

### Jewish Fate

- **Siege Victims**: Many Jews died in the siege
- **Einsatzgruppen**: Mobile killing units murdered Jews in captured areas
- **Survival**: Some Jews survived the siege under horrific conditions
- **Liberation**: Red Army broke the siege (January 1944)

---

## Postwar Leningrad

### Soviet Period (1944–1991)

- **Reconstruction**: City rebuilt after devastating siege
- **Jewish Life**: Limited religious and cultural life
- **Refuseniks**: Jews who applied for emigration became refuseniks
- **Underground**: Some religious observance continued clandestinely

### Post-Soviet Saint Petersburg (1991–present)

- **Renaming**: City renamed Saint Petersburg after Soviet collapse
- **Jewish Revival**: Synagogues reopened, Jewish cultural life revived
- **Chabad Presence**: Limited Chabad activity today
- **Historical Memory**: Former prison sites and Jewish history acknowledged

## Modern Saint Petersburg

### Contemporary Jewish Life

Today's Saint Petersburg:

- **Population**: Approximately 5 million (small Jewish minority)
- **Jewish Community**: Approximately 20,000–30,000 Jews
- **Institutions**: Synagogues, Jewish community center, schools
- **Chabad**: Limited Chabad presence

### Historical Sites

- **Former Prison**: Shpalerka prison (still exists)
- **Synagogues**: Historic and contemporary synagogues
- **Jewish Cemetery**: Historic Jewish cemetery
- **Memorials**: Holocaust and siege memorials

## Leningrad's Place in Chabad History

### Symbol of Persecution

Leningrad represents:

- **Arrests**: Multiple arrests of Chabad Rebbes
- **Government Hostility**: Czarist and Soviet suspicion of Chabad
- **Mesirus Nefesh**: Self-sacrifice in the face of persecution
- **Miraculous Releases**: The releases celebrated as divine providence

### 19 Kislev and 3 Tammuz

Two dates are celebrated:

- **19 Kislev**: Alter Rebbe's release from first imprisonment (1798)
- **3 Tammuz**: Rayatz's release from Soviet imprisonment (1927)

Both dates are celebrated as holidays in Chabad, commemorating divine salvation and the triumph of religious freedom.
