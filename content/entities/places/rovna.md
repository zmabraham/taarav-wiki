# Rovna (Rivne / Równe)

**Town in Volhynia (present-day Ukraine); residence of the Maggid of Mezeritch in his final years; site of the 1772 Chassidic leadership assembly**

## Overview

Rovna (also Równe in Polish, Rivne in Ukrainian; Belarusian: Роўна) is a major town in the Volhynia region of present-day northwestern Ukraine. During the eighteenth century it was part of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and then came under Russian control through the partitions of Poland. Its Jewish community was substantial and played an important role in regional commerce and religious life.

## The Maggid's Residence and Death

Rabbi Dov Ber of Mezeritch — the Maggid and primary successor to the Baal Shem Tov — spent a significant portion of his later years in the Rovna-Anipoli area of Volhynia. After Mezeritch, Rovna and the surrounding region became his operational base. The Maggid died in Anipoli (Annopil), near Rovna, on 19 Kislev תקל"ג (1772).

## The 1772 Chassidic Assembly

Rovna is historically significant in early Chabad history as the site of a major emergency gathering of Chassidic leaders in תקל"ב (1772). When the Vilna Gaon issued his herem against Chassidim and waves of persecution began affecting communities across Lithuania and White Russia, the leading figures of the Chassidic world gathered at the Maggid's court in Rovna to coordinate their response.

The Rovna assembly brought together the Alter Rebbe (Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi), Rabbi Menachem Mendel of Vitebsk, Rabbi Avraham of Kalisk, and other senior disciples of the Maggid. The gathering served multiple purposes: to provide moral support during a difficult period, to discuss strategy for responding to the Mitnagdic campaign, and to reinforce the bonds of unity among the Chassidic leadership.

The assembly also preceded or coincided with the decision by the Alter Rebbe and others to travel to Vilna in an attempt to meet the Vilna Gaon personally — a mission that ended in failure when the Gaon refused to receive them.

## Later History

Rovna (modern Rivne) remained a significant Jewish center through the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and became an important Zionist and cultural center for Volhynian Jewry. The city's Jewish community was largely destroyed during the Holocaust.
