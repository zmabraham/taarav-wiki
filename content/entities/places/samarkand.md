# Samarkand

A major city in Uzbekistan (Soviet Central Asia), which became a refuge for thousands of Jews — including many Chabad chassidim — who fled the German advance during WWII. Samarkand was one of the most important Chabad centers during the war years.

## Description
The Levin family arrived in Samarkand after a grueling three-week journey by train from Kharkov in the fall of 1941. On arrival, they found the city overwhelmed with refugees. Disease was rampant, food scarce, and many people died within days of falling ill.

A devastating personal tragedy struck when the Levins' only child, Efraim Shmuel (age two and a half), passed away on the eve of Shabbat, 9 Kislev 5702, in the local hospital.

The author had to hide from the military draft, living under an assumed identity as a Polish refugee named "Naftali Bat." His wife Ita Dvora worked in the market to support them while he worked secretly on a knitting machine (brought from Kharkov). They lived in cramped, hidden quarters, constantly at risk of exposure.

Despite the hardships, Samarkand became a vibrant Chabad community. An underground Tomchei Temimim yeshiva was established, initially with just three orphaned students — Sholom Levertov (Kovelker) and Zelig Katzman, and then Yeishke (Yehoshua) Dobravsky. The yeshiva was organized initially by R. Mikhael Teitelboim, then formally run by R. Mendel Futerfas and R. Abba Pliskin.

Key figures in the Samarkand community included:
- R. Yehoshua Karf (hosted a farbrengen on Tu B'Shvat 5702)
- R. Aharon Yosef Byelinitsky (fell ill)
- R. Avraham Zelig Ganzburg (fell ill)
- R. Manie Dobravsky (widow of a Chabad rav, mother of Yeishke)
- R. Pinchas Loitin (business partner of the author)

The author's sister Freyde was found near Stalingrad in summer 5703 (1943) and eventually came to Samarkand. She later married R. Asher Sasonkin.

The author's father-in-law R. Meir Gorkow was released from imprisonment in summer 5704 and came to Samarkand with his wife Figa Rivka.

The Levin family left Samarkand in summer 5706 (1946), using Polish identity papers, among the first anash to leave the USSR.
