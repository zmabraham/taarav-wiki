# Samarkand — סמרקנד

**Wartime Haven for Thousands of Chabad Chassidim**

**Location**: Uzbekistan, Central Asia (then Soviet Union)  
**Chabad Significance**: Major wartime refuge for Chabad families fleeing Nazi invasion (1941–1945)  
**Current status**: City in independent Uzbekistan; small Jewish community

---

## Overview

Samarkand (Uzbek: Samarqand; Tajik: Samarqand; Russian: Самарканд / Samarkand; Yiddish: סמאַרקאַנד / Samarkand) was the unlikely wartime haven for thousands of Chabad chassidim who fled eastward during World War II. When Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union in 1941, Chabad families from Belarus, Ukraine, and European Russia escaped to Central Asia, with Samarkand becoming the largest Chabad refugee community.

For four years (1941–1945), Samarkand hosted a remarkable underground Chabad community. Despite Soviet restrictions on religious life, thousands of Chabad chassidim maintained Jewish observance, established secret educational institutions, and preserved their way of life in the shadow of war and communism. After the war, many Samarkand chassidim would participate in the Great Escape (1946–47), eventually reaching [[entities/places/eretz-yisrael|Israel]] or America.

## Geography and Demographics

### Physical Geography

Samarkand is located in modern Uzbekistan:

- **Location**: In the Zeravshan Valley, approximately 700 km east of the Caspian Sea
- **Region**: Historic Uzbekistan; Soviet Uzbek SSR (1924–1991); independent Uzbekistan (1991–present)
- **Terrain**: Oasis city surrounded by desert and mountains
- **Climate**: Continental — hot summers, cold winters, arid
- **Elevation**: Approximately 700 meters (2,300 feet) above sea level

### Historical Significance

Samarkand is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world:

- **Ancient City**: Founded approximately 2,500+ years ago
- **Silk Road**: Major stop on the ancient Silk Road trade route
- **Timurid Capital**: Capital of Timur (Tamerlane)'s empire in the 14th century
- **Architecture**: Beautiful Islamic architecture and monuments

## The Bukharan Jewish Community

### Indigenous Jewish Population

Samarkand had an ancient Jewish community long before Chabad arrived:

- **Bukharan Jews**: Distinct Jewish community with origins dating back 2,000+ years
- **Language**: Spoke Bukhori (Judeo-Tajik dialect)
- **Culture**: Unique blend of Jewish and Central Asian culture
- **Religious Practice**: Traditional Jewish observance with local customs
- **Relations**: Generally positive relations with Muslim neighbors

### Community Size

- **Pre-WWII**: Approximately 10,000–15,000 Bukharan Jews in Samarkand
- **Post-WWII**: Population increased due to refugee influx
- **Emigration**: Most Bukharan Jews emigrated to Israel and America after 1970s
- **Today**: Small remnant community remains

## Wartime Refugee Crisis (1941–1942)

### Nazi Invasion of Soviet Union

- **June 22, 1941**: Germany invaded the Soviet Union (Operation Barbarossa)
- **Rapid Advance**: German forces captured huge territories in Belarus, Ukraine, and Russia
- **Holocaust Unfolds**: Einsatzgruppen murdered Jews in captured territories
- **Soviet Evacuation**: Stalin authorized evacuation of industries and populations eastward

### Chabad Flight to the East

Chabad families fled eastward to escape Nazi capture:

- **Urgent Escape**: Families fled with whatever they could carry
- **Difficult Journey**: Long, dangerous journey by train through war-torn countryside
- **Destinations**: Central Asian cities (Samarkand, [[entities/places/tashkent|Tashkent]], [[entities/places/bukhara|Bukhara]])
- **Arrival in Samarkand**: Thousands of Chabad refugees arrived in 1941–1942

## The Chabad Refugee Community in Samarkand

### Community Size

The wartime Chabad community in Samarkand was substantial:

- **Population**: Approximately 3,000–5,000 Chabad chassidim (estimates vary)
- **Families**: Hundreds of Chabad families
- **Rebbes' Families**: Relatives of the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] and other prominent Chabad figures
- **Yeshiva Students**: Tomchei Temimim students and faculty

### Demographics

The refugees included:

- **From Belarus**: Families from [[entities/places/lubavitch|Lubavitch]], [[entities/places/homel|Homel]], [[entities/places/vitebsk|Vitebsk]], [[entities/places/nevel|Nevel]], and elsewhere
- **From Ukraine**: Families from [[entities/places/kremenchug|Kremenchug]], [[entities/places/odessa|Odessa]], [[entities/places/kiev|Kiev]], and elsewhere
- **From Russia**: Families from [[entities/places/moscow|Moscow]], [[entities/places/leningrad|Leningrad]], and elsewhere
- **Scholars**: Rabbis, yeshiva students, mashpiim
- **Families**: Men, women, and children of all ages

## Underground Religious Life

### Maintaining Observance

Despite Soviet restrictions, Samarkand's Chabad community maintained Jewish life:

- **Secret Minyanim**: Private prayer services in apartments
- **Shabbos and Holidays**: Jewish holidays celebrated in private homes
- **Kosher Food**: Difficult but possible to obtain kosher food
- **Mikvah**: Ritual bath constructed or arranged clandestinely

### Underground Education

Education continued despite dangers:

- **Secret Schools**: Torah taught to children in secret
- **Yeshiva**: Tomchei Temimim operated clandestinely
- **Mashpiim**: Spiritual mentors continued teaching despite risks
- **Chevrusas**: Study partners learned together privately

### Mohalim and Shochtim

Essential religious functionaries operated at great risk:

- **Mohalim**: Ritual circumcisers performed _bris milah_ in secret
- **Shochtim**: Ritual slaughterers provided kosher meat
- **Risk**: Discovery meant arrest and imprisonment
- **Sacrifice**: Individuals risked their lives for the community

## Connection to the Rayatz

### Coded Correspondence

Samarkand's refugees maintained contact with the [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] in America:

- **Letters**: Coded correspondence sent via trusted messengers
- **Euphemisms**: Letters used seemingly innocent language to convey religious content
- **Challenges**: Communication was difficult and dangerous
- **Encouragement**: The Rayatz provided spiritual guidance from abroad

### Financial Support

- **Funds from America**: The Rayatz sent money to support refugees
- **Distribution**: Funds distributed to needy families
- **Community Support**: Mutual aid within the refugee community

## Wartime Conditions

### Hardships

The refugees faced severe difficulties:

- **Housing**: Overcrowded living conditions
- **Food**: Shortages and rationing
- **Health**: Disease outbreaks and inadequate medical care
- **Climate**: Adjustment to hot, arid Central Asian climate
- **Separation**: Many families separated during evacuation

### Economic Survival

Refugees adapted to survive:

- **Work**: Whatever work was available
- **Skilled Crafts**: Using existing skills to earn income
- **Mutual Aid**: Community members helped each other
- **Local Relations**: Generally positive relations with Bukharan Jews and local population

---

## The End of the War and Aftermath

### Victory and Uncertainty (1945)

- **May 1945**: Germany surrendered; war in Europe ended
- **Dilemma**: Should refugees return to their homes or remain in Central Asia?
- **Westward Return**: Some refugees returned to Belarus, Ukraine, Russia
- **Destruction**: Most found their communities destroyed by the Holocaust

### The Great Escape (1946–47)

Many Samarkand chassidim participated in the mass escape:

- **Polish Passports**: Forged documents claiming Polish citizenship
- **Moscow Hub**: Gathering point for escapees
- **Trans-Siberian Railway**: Journey across Russia to Poland
- **Refugee Camps**: Displaced persons camps in Germany and Austria
- **Final Destinations**: Most reached [[entities/places/eretz-yisrael|Israel]] or America

### Those Who Remained

- **Underground Continuation**: Some remained in Soviet Union
- **Postwar Repression**: Stalin's final years brought renewed persecution
- **Continued Struggle**: Religious life remained extremely difficult
- **Later Emigration**: Some emigrated in the 1970s–1980s

## Postwar Samarkand

### Soviet Period (1945–1991)

- **Refugee Departure**: Most Chabad refugees left by 1947
- **Remaining Community**: Small Chabad families remained
- **Bukharan Jews**: Continued community life
- **Emigration**: Gradual emigration to Israel and America

### Independent Uzbekistan (1991–present)

- **Democracy**: Uzbekistan became independent
- **Jewish Community**: Small remnant community
- **Emigration**: Most remaining Jews emigrated to Israel
- **Historical Memory**: Samarkand's role as wartime refuge remembered

## Modern Samarkand

### Contemporary Samarkand

Today's Samarkand:

- **Population**: Approximately 500,000 (small Jewish minority)
- **Jewish Community**: Very small remnant of Bukharan Jews
- **Historical Sites**: Ancient city with Islamic architecture
- **Chabad Memory**: The wartime Chabad community remembered in memoirs

## Samarkand's Place in Chabad History

### Haven of Refuge

Samarkand represents:

- **Survival**: Thousands saved from Nazi genocide
- **Continuity**: Chabad traditions preserved despite displacement
- **Sacrifice**: Maintaining Judaism under difficult conditions
- **Community**: Remarkable communal solidarity

### Bridge to Future

The Samarkand period:

- **Great Escape**: Refugees from Samarkand participated in 1946–47 escape
- **Israel Pioneers**: Many Samarkand refugees were among founders of [[entities/places/kfar-chabad|Kfar Chabad]]
