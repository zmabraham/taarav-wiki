# Shanghai

**Chabad's WWII Sanctuary in the Far East**

---

## Overview

Shanghai, China served as one of the most unusual Jewish refuges of WWII. For approximately 40 Chabad yeshiva students from Vilna, it was the end point of a remarkable escape route and home for nearly four years of war. The Chabad community in Shanghai, led by the city's Ashkenazic Chief Rabbi [[entities/people/meir-ashkenazi|Rabbi Meir Ashkenazi]], maintained full Jewish life in the most improbable of settings.

---

## The Escape Route (1940–1941)

The path to Shanghai:

1. **Vilna, Lithuania** — Chabad's Tomchei Temimim students heard about the visa route
2. **Kaunas (Kovno)** — Dutch consul [[entities/people/zwartendijk|Jan Zwartendijk]] stamped passports "no visa required for Curacao"
3. **Soviet Union** — [[entities/people/sugihara|Chiune Sugihara]] (Japanese consul) issued transit visas; Soviet Union granted transit
4. **Trans-Siberian Railway** — Moscow to Vladivostok
5. **Kobe, Japan** — temporary stay (1940-41)
6. **Shanghai** — final destination; no visa required to enter

The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] coordinated from Brooklyn: negotiating with the US Embassy, raising $500 per student for Soviet transit fees, and maintaining contact with the students throughout.

---

## Jewish Shanghai

Shanghai in the 1940s was extraordinary: a major international city in occupied China (the Japanese controlled it after 1941) with no entry visa requirements. This made it a magnet for Jewish refugees when all other doors were closing:

- Sephardic Jews (Baghdadi community) had been established since the 19th century
- Russian Jewish refugees from the 1917 Revolution were already there
- ~20,000 German and Austrian Jewish refugees arrived 1938-1941
- The Chabad/Mir Yeshiva students arrived 1940-1941

---

## The Chabad Community in Wartime Shanghai

Rabbi Meir Ashkenazi, the Ashkenazic Chief Rabbi of Shanghai (and a Chabad chassid), led the community with remarkable skill:

- Maintained religious life and Torah study throughout the war
- Organized support (financial, social, spiritual) for refugee students
- Navigated relations with Japanese authorities
- The students from Tomchei Temimim maintained their yeshiva framework, continuing to learn even in this extraordinary setting

The [[entities/people/rayatz|Rayatz]] stayed in close contact — letters flowed between Brooklyn and Shanghai, and the Rebbe's concern for his students was constant.

---

## After the War

When WWII ended (1945), the Shanghai community dispersed:

- Most students eventually reached the United States, Israel, or other Western countries
- Rabbi Meir Ashkenazi eventually emigrated to Israel (after the Communist takeover of China in 1949)
- Many of the former Shanghai students became prominent Chabad rabbis and educators

---

## Significance

The Shanghai episode represents one of the most dramatic chapters in the history of Jewish survival during the Holocaust. That a group of yeshiva students from Vilna ended up continuing their Talmudic studies in wartime Shanghai — while their families and communities were being annihilated in Europe — is a story that encapsulates both the horror and the resilience of the Holocaust period.
