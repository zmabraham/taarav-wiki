# Slavuta (סלאוויטא)

**Town in Volhynia (present-day Ukraine); home of the Shapiro printing press; site of the first printing of the Tanya in תקנ"ז (1796/7)**

## Overview

Slavuta (Ukrainian: Славута; Polish: Sławuta) is a town in the Khmelnytskyi Oblast of present-day Ukraine, located in the historic Volhynia region. During the late eighteenth century it was part of the Russian Empire (after the Second Partition of Poland in 1793) and was home to one of the most celebrated Hebrew printing houses in Eastern Europe.

## The Shapiro Printing House

The primary Jewish institution that made Slavuta historically significant was the printing press established by Rabbi Pinchas of Slavuta, who was a grandson of the Baal Shem Tov. The Shapiro family — Rabbi Pinchas and later his sons — operated one of the most prestigious Hebrew publishing houses in the region for decades. The press was known for the quality of its productions and for its connections to the chassidic world through the Baal Shem Tov's family lineage.

The Slavuta press published many important works of Jewish scholarship and Chassidic literature, but its most historically significant production for Chabad was the first printing of the Tanya.

## The First Tanya Printing (תקנ"ז / 1796–7)

In תקנ"ז (1796/7), the Alter Rebbe's foundational work — the Tanya (also known as Likkutei Amarim or Sefer Shel Beinonim) — was printed for the first time in Slavuta. The decision to print the work had been delayed for years because the Alter Rebbe was concerned about accurate transmission; numerous manuscript copies had been circulated among Chabad communities, and errors and distortions had accumulated. The printing was intended to establish a definitive, authoritative text.

The Slavuta edition carried haskamot (approbations) from Rabbi Zusia of Anipoli and Rabbi Yehuda Leib HaKohen — endorsements from respected figures outside the Chabad circle that lent the work broader legitimacy. The editorial coordination was handled in part by the Alter Rebbe's son-in-law Rabbi Shalom Shachna.

The choice of Slavuta — connected to the Baal Shem Tov's family through Rabbi Pinchas — was itself symbolically meaningful: it positioned the Tanya within the continuity of the broader chassidic tradition traced back to the Baal Shem Tov.

## Later Printings

Subsequent editions of the Tanya were printed in other locations: Zolkiev (1799), Kapust (1800), Shklov (1814), and eventually worldwide. Each printing carried its own editorial history, and the Rashab (Rabbi Shalom Dov Ber Schneersohn) produced a corrected critical edition in תר"ס (1900) that became the standard reference text.
