# Pale of Settlement

**The region of the Russian Empire where Jews were legally required to reside; encompassing present-day Poland, Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, and parts of Russia; the setting for most of the Frierdiker Rebbe's early life and all of early Chabad history.**

---

## Overview

The Pale of Settlement (Cherta Postoyannoye Zhitelstva in Russian) was the western region of the Russian Empire in which Jewish residence was legally permitted. Established by Catherine the Great in 1791 and maintained until the February Revolution of 1917, the Pale encompassed present-day Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, Belarus, Moldova, and large parts of Ukraine, along with small portions of Russia proper. Within this region — roughly 20% of the Empire's territory — nearly five million Jews lived by the early 20th century, constituting the largest concentration of Jewish population in the world.

The Pale defined the geography of Eastern European Jewry. All of early Chabad's history — the Alter Rebbe's Liozna and Liadi, the Mitteler Rebbe's Lubavitch, the Tzemach Tzedek's court, the Maharash, the Rashab — unfolded within the Pale. The Rayatz was born in Lubavitch (within the Pale) in 1880. The vast networks of Jewish life — yeshivos, synagogues, rabbinical courts, Chassidic communities — that Chabad organized and served all existed within this defined geographic and legal space.

The Pale's abolition in 1917 was one of the first acts of the Provisional Government following the February Revolution. Jewish residence restrictions fell; Jews could now live anywhere in Russia. But within months, the Bolshevik Revolution replaced one set of restrictions with another — now directed not at Jewish residence but at Jewish religious life.

## Significance in Chabad History

The Pale is the geographic container of nearly all early Chabad history. The movement's founding (Alter Rebbe, 18th century), its expansion (Mitteler Rebbe, Tzemach Tzedek, 19th century), and its mature institutional development (Maharash, Rashab, early Rayatz, late 19th–early 20th century) all occurred within the Pale.

Life in the Pale was defined by poverty, discrimination, periodic pogroms (organized anti-Jewish violence), and intense internal Jewish communal life. The Jewish communities of the Pale — the shtetlakh (small towns) and the larger cities — developed rich religious, cultural, and intellectual traditions precisely because they were largely self-contained communities. Chabad thrived in this environment, providing spiritual leadership, educational institutions, and a network of personal relationships that gave meaning to difficult lives.

The destruction of Pale-area Jewry in the Holocaust — virtually every Jewish community in the former Pale was either decimated or entirely destroyed — represents the annihilation of the world that had produced Chabad and every other form of Eastern European Jewish life. The Rayatz's rescue from Warsaw in 1940 was a rescue from the very center of this world at the moment of its destruction.
