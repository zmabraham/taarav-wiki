# Rebbetzin Sheina Horenstein

**Youngest daughter of the Rayatz; perished in the Holocaust with her husband in 1942; her loss was one of the deepest griefs of the Frierdiker Rebbe's final years.**

---

## Overview

Rebbetzin Sheina Horenstein (nee Schneersohn) was born in 1903 as the youngest of the three daughters of Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (the Rayatz) and Rebbetzin Nechama Dina. She married Rabbi Menachem Mendel Horenstein. Unlike her two older sisters — Chana (married to the Rashag) and Chaya Mushka (married to the Ramash) — Sheina was unable to escape Nazi-occupied Europe when the opportunity arose in 1940. She remained behind and was murdered in the Holocaust in 1942 along with her husband.

Sheina's fate represents one of the most personal tragedies in the Rayatz's biography. The Rayatz arrived in America in 1940 having escaped the Nazi occupation, but he carried the knowledge that his youngest daughter was still in occupied territory. When news of her murder reached him, it was a wound that never healed. The "Undaunted" biography depicts this loss as inseparable from the Rayatz's emotional and spiritual landscape during his final decade in Brooklyn.

## Life and Role

Sheina grew up in the Chabad court alongside her two sisters, sharing the extraordinary experience of being daughters of the sixth Rebbe through the upheavals of the Soviet period, World War I, and the exile years. The details of her life before the Holocaust are less documented than those of her sisters; she lived in Poland with her husband Rabbi Menachem Mendel Horenstein.

When the opportunity arose to escape Nazi-occupied Warsaw in early 1940 — secured through extraordinary diplomatic efforts and personal intervention by American officials and Chabad representatives — the escape arrangements were complicated, the danger was extreme, and not everyone could be included. Sheina and her husband did not make it out. They remained in Nazi-occupied Poland and were killed in 1942 during the Holocaust, along with millions of other Jewish victims.

The awareness of Sheina's fate — first her entrapment, then her murder — was a constant presence in the Rayatz's final decade. The "Undaunted" narrative places this loss in the context of a man who faced persecution from the Soviets and the Nazis and who, despite personal tragedy, continued to lead and build Chabad until his dying day. Sheina's memory serves as a reminder that the Rayatz's survival was not without devastating cost.
