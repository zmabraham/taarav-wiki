# Rabbi Jacob Hecht (American Chabad)

**Rabbi Jacob J. Hecht; among the early students and supporters of the Rayatz in America; active in Chabad outreach and educational work in New York in the 1940s.**

---

## Overview

Rabbi Jacob J. Hecht, known in Chabad circles as Reb Yankel Hecht, was among the early American students and supporters of Rabbi Yosef Yitzchak Schneersohn (the Rayatz) after the Rebbe's arrival in New York in 1940. He became an important figure in the growing Chabad presence in Brooklyn, contributing to the outreach and educational work that the Rayatz directed from 770 Eastern Parkway.

Rabbi Hecht's activities in the 1940s placed him at the center of the early American Chabad story — the period when the Rayatz was building the institutional infrastructure of Chabad in America from a new home that had initially seemed impossibly alien. The challenge of transplanting Chassidic Jewish life to America — which the Rayatz famously declared "is no different" — required people like Hecht who could work within the American Jewish context while maintaining Chassidic commitment.

He was part of the community that gathered around the Rayatz in Crown Heights, Brooklyn, and contributed to the outreach activities that would later, under the seventh Rebbe, become the global Chabad shlichus (emissary) network.

## Life and Role

The American Jewish community that the Rayatz encountered on his arrival in 1940 was largely acculturated and unfamiliar with Chassidic life. Building a Chassidic community in this environment required devoted students and activists who could both learn from the Rebbe and serve as bridges to the broader American Jewish world. Rabbi Hecht was among those who took on this dual role.

His work in outreach and education placed him in the vanguard of what would become Chabad's distinctive American contribution: the proactive engagement with Jews of all backgrounds rather than the inward-focused community model common in pre-war Europe. This approach — which the seventh Rebbe would systematize and expand into a global network — was already being tested in the early 1940s by figures like Hecht.
